TITLE: Character.ai's creditors pick a $1,500-an-hour adviser to check the sale
OUTLET: PPC Land

The committee that speaks for Character.ai's unsecured creditors on November 24 asked the federal bankruptcy court in Delaware to approve its choice of financial adviser, M3 Advisory Partners, LP, at hourly rates running from $500 for an analyst to $1,500 for a managing partner. The bill would be paid from the bankrupt company's estate. And the committee wants the arrangement to count from November 4, twenty days before the request was filed.
In Short
Character.ai's creditors' committee asked the court on November 24 to approve M3 Advisory Partners as its financial adviser, at $500 to $1,500 an hour for M3's own staff and up to $2,500 an hour for outside contractors. The money would come out of what is left of the bankrupt company, the same pot from which unsecured creditors, including tech and marketing vendors, hope to be paid. M3's job list covers checking the sale of the company's assets, estimating what creditors could recover and looking at deals made before the bankruptcy that might be challenged.
The request
The application is filed as Docket No. 231 in case 26-11603 (BLS) and runs to 34 pages with its exhibits. It is signed by Euki Guererro, "solely in my capacity as a representative of Alder FM LLC, the Chair of the Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors of Character Technologies, Inc." Character Technologies is the legal name of the company behind the Character.ai chatbot app.
An official committee of unsecured creditors is a small group of creditors chosen by the US Trustee, the Justice Department office that oversees bankruptcy cases, to speak for everyone the company owes money without any collateral standing behind the debt. In practice that usually means suppliers, service firms and others holding unpaid invoices. According to the application, the US Trustee appointed this committee on November 4, 2026 (Docket No. 115). The committee chose M3 the same day, "subject to Court approval."
Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11 on October 12, 2026, and continues to run its business as a debtor-in-possession, the filing states. It named Disney as the first bidder at the outset, offering $20 million for a license to the company's intellectual property rather than for the chatbot platform itself.
The notice sets a deadline of December 8, 2026, at 4:00 p.m. ET for written objections. A hearing, "if necessary," is scheduled for December 15 at 10:00 a.m. ET before Judge Brendan Linehan Shannon in Courtroom No. 1 at 824 Market Street in Wilmington. The notice warns that if nobody responds, the court may grant the request without a further hearing.
Who sits on the committee
The list of parties M3 checked for conflicts names three committee members: "A.D., a minor, by and through her guardian ad litem K.H."; Alder FM LLC; and "K.T., a minor, through her father S.T." Two of the three members are children, identified only by initials. A guardian ad litem is a person a court appoints to act for a child in a legal proceeding.
Alder FM, LLC, the committee chair, also appears on the same list among the holders of the 20 largest unsecured claims against the company. The filing does not say what Alder FM supplied or how much it is owed.
The initials created a practical problem for M3's own vetting. A footnote in the supporting declaration states: "Although Committee members (a) A.D., a minor, by and through her guardian ad litem K.H., and (b) K.T., a minor, through her father S.T., are listed on Schedule 1, M3 Partners does not have access to the full names of A.D. or K.T., and therefore these parties were not searched in connection with the preparation of this Declaration." Put simply, the adviser could not run its conflict check on two of the three members it will be working for.
The committee's lawyers are McDermott Will & Schulte LLP, which signs the notice as "Proposed Counsel to the Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors." The signature block lists David R. Hurst and Andrew A. Mark in Wilmington, Darren Azman, Kristin Going and Julia Winters in New York, and Gregg A. Steinman in Miami.
M3's role was not a complete surprise. The conflicts list filed when Lucy Thomson was appointed as the case's privacy ombudsman at $650 an hour on November 18 already named "M3 Partners, LP" among the committee's professionals. The committee filed six deposition notices that same day. Five days later, at the November 23 hearing, its lawyer told the judge that the committee did not object to Houlihan Lokey's hiring as the company's investment banker.
The price list
M3 would bill by the hour, at its standard rates "for services rendered from and after January 1, 2026," which can change "at such time as M3 Partners adjusts its hourly rates generally." The application sets out the following schedule.
The last line deserves attention. M3 says it hires independent contractors "from time to time, including in connection with the Chapter 11 Case," and passes their fees through "at the same rate that M3 Partners pays the contractors," with expenses passed on "without mark-up." The top contractor rate of $2,500 an hour is $1,000 above M3's own managing partner rate, about 67% higher, by PPC Land's calculation. The declaration refers to "the independent contractor who M3 Partners currently expects will provide support" on this engagement. It does not name that contractor.
Out-of-pocket costs are charged at cost, covering "travel, lodging, duplicating, research, messenger, and telephone charges," at rates "consistent with or discounted to" those charged to other M3 clients and subject to the US Trustee's guidelines.
Three statements in the declaration of Robert Winning, a managing director at M3, set the outer limits of the deal:
- "M3 Partners has received no retainer in connection with this representation and is not owed any amounts with respect to prepetition fees and expenses in connection with the Chapter 11 Case."
- "Neither the members of the Committee, nor any of their agents or their representatives, shall be responsible or liable for any fees or costs incurred by M3 Partners in connection with its representation of the Committee, regardless of whether M3 Partners is paid in full from the Debtor's estate."
- "M3 Partners does not intend to seek a success fee in connection with this engagement."
That last line separates M3 from the company's own banker. Houlihan Lokey's engagement letter pairs a $100,000 monthly fee with a $1.75 million transaction fee on a sale of up to $25 million, plus 3% of anything above that. M3, by contrast, would be paid only by the hour, plus expenses. How many hours? The filing gives no estimate and no budget. As a rough guide, 100 hours of managing partner time at the listed rate would cost $150,000, while the same 100 hours from an analyst would cost $50,000, by PPC Land's arithmetic. The managing partner rate is about 2.3 times the ombudsman's $650.
The committee calls the structure "competitive, appropriate, and reasonable in the context of the Chapter 11 Case," and Winning describes it as "reasonable, market-based, and designed to fairly compensate M3 Partners for its work in the Chapter 11 Case and to cover fixed and routine overhead expenses."
What M3 would be paid to do
The application lists 16 tasks, lettered (a) to (p). In plain terms they fall into four groups.
Measuring the money. The first item is "an assessment of the unsecured claims pool and potential recoveries for unsecured creditors." M3 would also "develop information regarding the Debtor's assets and their potential values under different scenarios," review the company's financial reports, and investigate "whether any unencumbered assets at the Debtor exist," meaning assets that are not already pledged to a lender.
Watching the sale. M3 would "monitor and, to the extent appropriate, assist the Debtor in efforts to develop and solicit transactions that would support unsecured creditor recoveries." It would also review any proposed financing or use of cash collateral and the company's cost-benefit analysis on whether to keep or reject contracts and leases.
Looking backwards. Two items point at the period before the bankruptcy. M3 would assist in "identifying, valuing, and pursuing estate causes of action, including, but not limited to, relating to prepetition transactions," and in "evaluating and analyzing avoidance actions, including fraudulent conveyances and preferential transfers."
The endgame. M3 would review any disclosure statement and Chapter 11 plan and, "if appropriate, assist the Committee in developing an alternative chapter 11 plan." It would also attend and present at meetings, advise in negotiations, and "provide testimony on behalf of the Committee as and when may be deemed appropriate."
The "looking backwards" group needs a plain-language gloss. Avoidance actions are claims a bankrupt company's estate can bring to recover money or property that left the company before the filing. A fraudulent conveyance, in this legal sense, can be a transfer made to put assets beyond creditors' reach, or one made for less than fair value while the company was in financial trouble; it does not require anyone to have committed a crime. A preferential transfer is a payment made shortly before a bankruptcy that put one creditor ahead of the others. The application names no transaction and does not say that any such claim exists.
What were the company's big dealings before the filing? Earlier filings set out the main ones. Sixty-six of its 86 staff took jobs at Disney ahead of the bankruptcy, a structure often described as an acqui-hire. Filings put about $180 million in pay and retention for those staff over three years on top of the $20 million license price; that $180 million goes to the employees, not to the company's estate. A second stalking horse bid, $10 million from Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC for everything outside Disney's license, followed on November 14.
Paid from November 4
The committee wants M3's hire to count from the day it was chosen. The application explains why: "Due to the size and complex nature of the Chapter 11 Case, as well as the expedited schedule occasioned by the Debtor seeking relief during the early stages of the Chapter 11 Case, there was an immediate need for M3 Partners to perform services for the Committee upon its retention and prior to the Court's consideration and approval of this Application."
It adds that M3 "turned its attention to urgent matters including, but not limited to, addressing a variety of issues relating to the Debtor's first day motions and ongoing sale process." Without the earlier start date, the 20 days of work between November 4 and November 24 would fall outside the approved engagement.
The calendar makes the backdating more significant. The hearing on the sales themselves was moved to December 7 and 8. The hearing on M3's hire is set for December 15, a week after the sale hearing ends, by PPC Land's count. The objection deadline falls on the second day of the sale hearing, so the court cannot rule on M3's hire until the sale hearing is over or nearly over. If the judge approves the request as written, M3's pay would reach back over all of that work.
If M3 is sued
The application asks the court to make the company stand behind M3 if anyone brings a legal claim over its work. The wording in the proposed order is: "The Debtor is authorized to indemnify, and shall indemnify, M3 Partners for any claims arising from, related to, or in connection with the services to be provided by M3 Partners."
There are carve-outs. The company would have no duty to cover a claim "judicially determined (the determination having become final) to have arisen from M3 Partners' actual fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, if any, gross negligence, willful misconduct, self-dealing, or bad faith."
