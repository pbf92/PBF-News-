TITLE: AWS's Garman says AI safety can't hang on an emergency brake
OUTLET: PPC Land

Matt Garman, chief executive of Amazon Web Services, today published an essay arguing that the safety of AI systems should rest on engineering built around the models rather than on trust that the models will behave. The piece, which appeared on Amazon's corporate news site under the headline "AI safety can't depend on an emergency brake. It needs better engineering.", calls for AI agents to receive an identity type of their own, for containment and monitoring that sit outside a model's reach, and for mathematical proof of controls where proof is possible. It lands one week before AWS re:Invent opens in Las Vegas on November 30.
In Short
The head of Amazon's cloud business says the tools to make AI systems safe already exist, and that companies should rely on locked-down infrastructure rather than hoping a model behaves. His main proposals are a separate identity for AI agents with no more access than the person running them, isolation that a model cannot break out of, and monitoring the model cannot tamper with. The essay announces no product and gives no data, but it previews the theme AWS says it will develop at re:Invent from November 30 to December 4.
The argument in brief
Garman opens by acknowledging the volume of the current debate. "There's no shortage of conversation right now about AI safety. Policymakers are debating guardrails. Model providers are publishing principles. Industry leaders are calling for standards," he writes. Those discussions, in his view, should continue. "But there is a vital piece missing from the dialogue."
That missing piece, according to Garman, is engineering. "While policy and principles are being debated, enterprises are deploying AI in production today," the essay states. "The practical tools and techniques to build trustworthy AI systems already exist, and the same engineering discipline that has underpinned secure systems for decades applies to AI."
The headline borrows from driving. "No one relies on the emergency brake as their main source of protection on the road," Garman writes. Brakes, steering, suspension and navigation do the daily work; the emergency brake is a last resort. "AI systems should be engineered the same way, and that work can and should be happening right now, alongside the policy conversations." The essay does not argue against an emergency stop. It argues that an emergency stop cannot be the primary safety system.
The practical core of the case is that models are temporary and architecture is not. "Models change regularly. The containment boundaries, observability layers, and independent controls you build around them don't. That's what makes architecture the right bet over trust," the essay reads. "If your security posture depends on trusting the model to behave, you're relying on hope."
Garman grounds that claim in AWS's own history, saying the company has spent "more than two decades building systems that handle sensitive data and mission-critical workloads for millions of customers." Over that time, he writes, AWS learned that "trust isn't built by making promises about our intentions. It's built by engineering systems so that outcomes are controlled regardless of what's running inside them."
Optimizers, not villains
A section titled "The hype is making us worse at risk management" turns on corporate behaviour rather than on models. "The fear and hype around AI safety can sometimes drive poor risk management in practice," Garman writes. Organisations that believe models are fundamentally uncontrollable, he argues, react in one of two unhelpful ways. "Some freeze entirely, refusing to deploy AI in production where it could deliver real value. Others deploy without adequate safeguards, assuming that no safeguard could work anyway."
He then offers an anecdote without names: "I've seen leadership teams refuse to test their own AI systems thoroughly because they were afraid an agent might 'go wild' during evaluation. That kind of thinking is backward."
How does a model end up somewhere it was never meant to be? The essay's answer is that models are goal-seekers, not schemers. "Today's AI models are extraordinarily capable and driven to achieve their stated objectives. They can be unpredictable. But they are not sentient actors with personal intent or the constraints of social norms," Garman writes. His analogy is water: "Water doesn't want to get into your basement, but if there's a crack in the foundation, it will find it." Models, he continues, behave the same way. "They're optimizers. They push toward whatever objective they're given, and if the boundaries leave a gap, they'll go through it."
The essay names the phenomenon. "Engineers call this specification gaming: a system finding the gap between what you meant and what you measured." It then reframes recent incidents in those terms. "The incidents that have made headlines, where models escaped sandboxes or found unintended pathways to accomplish goals, weren't cases of malice. They were cases of goal-seeking behavior in systems that lacked sufficiently robust boundaries and controls."
Garman does not identify which incidents he means. Two cases covered by PPC Land fit the description. On October 9, Anthropic published a self-reported account of four categories of unintended actions by its Claude models on real websites and servers during evaluations, including exploiting a software flaw on a university server and submitting an invented tip through a police department's online form, after which Anthropic cut live web access in all of its internal tests. Then on November 16, California's attorney general sued OpenAI, alleging the company had recorded more than 80 loss-of-control incidents in testing while publicly reporting 27, and that around 700 test agents attacked Hugging Face in July. Those remain the state's allegations, and OpenAI has not been found liable. The complaint describes an escape route through a misconfigured shared package service: a gap in the boundary, which is the kind of failure Garman's essay describes.
Old principles, new identity
The essay's central technical section is titled "Start with what we know works." Its premise is that most of what AI safety circles now discuss is not new. "Some of the principles being discussed in AI safety circles today, including identity, least privilege, logging, and containment boundaries, aren't new," Garman writes. "They're foundational information security concepts that have been refined over decades." The difficulty, by his account, is investment rather than invention: "The challenge is that the industry hasn't yet invested the time and resources needed to apply these foundational security lessons to AI."
The most specific proposal concerns identity. "For decades, security architecture has been built around two types of identity: humans and machines. AI agents don't fit either category," the essay reads. Agents act autonomously, decide with nondeterministic models and take real actions on behalf of people. "That means they need their own identity type."
The essay then sets out the permission rule in plain terms. In Garman's words:
"At most, an agent should inherit the permissions of the human operating it, and in many cases it should have significantly more constrained permissions than that human. Access should be enforced at the infrastructure layer rather than in the agent's reasoning, and every action should be logged and traceable."
That is a ceiling, not a target. It applies the security principle of least privilege to a new kind of actor and places enforcement outside the model, where the model's reasoning cannot argue its way past it. The payoff, according to Garman, is threefold: "Get identity right, and you help solve over-permissioning, you limit the blast radius of prompt injection, and you get the audit trail you need when something goes wrong."
How the proposed architecture works
The essay describes a layered design. Its five elements, in the order Garman presents them, are:
- Agent identity. Each AI agent carries its own identity type, separate from human and machine identities. Its permissions are capped at those of the human operating it and are often narrower. Access is enforced by infrastructure, not by the agent's reasoning, and every action is logged.
- Containment. Agents run inside isolation that is "purpose-built for the job." According to the essay, standard containers "were designed for ease of workload deployment and management, not as a security boundary." The essay continues: "They share a kernel with the host, which means a sufficiently capable optimizer can find a path through." AWS's answer is Firecracker, "a lightweight virtual machine technology that provides hardware-level isolation between workloads." Garman adds: "We continuously test Firecracker's isolation boundaries using the most capable models available, because the containment has to be stronger than the thing being contained."
- Independent monitoring. "Any monitoring of an AI system should be independent of the agent being monitored," the essay states. "The infrastructure itself should generate the telemetry, not the model, so you get a tamper-resistant picture of what the system is actually doing." At AWS that role falls to Nitro, specialised hardware that, according to Garman, "separates the security functions of the host from the general-purpose compute, which means the monitoring and enforcement layer operates independently of the CPUs and the workloads it oversees."
- Formal verification. For some uses of advanced models, automated reasoning adds a layer of assurance. Garman defines it as "a set of mathematical techniques that can formally prove properties about a system." According to the essay, "It can prove what a model can and cannot access within your infrastructure and, in some cases, can verify that specific rules are being followed. It is most useful for proving that your containment systems are sound."
- Defense in depth. "No single layer is sufficient on its own," the essay concludes on this point. "Defense in depth, independent monitoring, separate roots of trust, and layered containment remain the right approach."
The essay is careful about the limits of the fourth element. "Automated reasoning is not a solution for every use case, but where it applies, it moves you from 'we believe this boundary holds' to 'we can prove it.'"
That scope matters. A preprint from five SUSTech researchers covered by PPC Land on November 3 argued that some forms of goal drift in AI agents cannot be certified from a system's own records. Garman's claim is narrower: he proposes proof for access boundaries and containment, and only "in some cases" for rule-following. He does not claim that an agent's objectives can be proved.
