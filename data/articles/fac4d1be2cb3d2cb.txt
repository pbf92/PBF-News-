TITLE: LGB Clinicians Report More Depression, Study Finds
OUTLET: Gayety

Gay, lesbian and bisexual clinicians said they’d been diagnosed with depression at some point at about twice the rate of straight colleagues, 41.2% to 19.1%, in survey-weighted findings from a study in JAMA Network Open published Nov. 10. They were also more than four times as likely to go without prescriptions over cost.
“Sexual minority HCPs faced greater disparities in access to care,” wrote the authors, who use HCPs as their shorthand for health care professionals.
Of 2,890 clinicians who answered the National Health Interview Survey in 2020, 2021, 2023 and 2024, only 159 identified as gay, lesbian, bisexual or something other than straight.
Vanderbilt’s Sample Mixed Doctors, Nurses and Pharmacists
Gilbert Gonzales, Eric Connelly and Christopher Terndrup of Vanderbilt University wrote the paper with Jesse M. Ehrenfeld of the Medical College of Wisconsin.
“The broad category of health-diagnosing and treating practitioners encompasses a diverse workforce, including physicians, registered nurses, advanced practice clinicians, pharmacists, and therapists, among others,” they wrote. The public survey files can’t tell a doctor from a nurse.
Women made up about three in four respondents, which the authors said “may partly reflect the representation of nursing.”
The 159 skewed younger and more often never married.
Depression and Cost Gaps for LGB Health Care Workers
“Sexual minority HCPs were more likely to report depression,” the paper’s Key Points say. On moderate to severe psychological distress, which the survey asked about only in 2021 and 2024, it was 36.5% of LGB clinicians against 17.9% of straight ones.
“Beyond the worse health reported by SGM health care professionals, we apparently make for worse patients,” Boston University physician Carl G. Streed Jr. wrote in an invited commentary published with the paper. SGM means sexual and gender minority.
LGB clinicians were “more likely to forgo mental health care,” the abstract continues. Over cost, 20.5% of them did, compared with 4.6% of straight clinicians.
Prescriptions: 14.9% to 3.4%. Delayed medical care: 13.5% to 5.7%.
“Consistent with the broader SGM populations, SGM health care professionals are delaying seeking necessary care, especially due to cost,” Streed wrote.
“Another notable finding was the coexistence of high rates of private health insurance coverage with substantial disparities in cost-related barriers to care,” the authors wrote. Private coverage: 92.8% of LGB clinicians, 89.8% of straight ones. In survey-weighted figures, 7.4% of the LGB group lived below the federal poverty line, against 1.1% of straight clinicians.
Missing Details on Trans Clinicians and Workplace Stigma
“The NHIS does not include measures of gender identity during all study years,” the authors wrote. That “limited our ability to examine health and access to care among transgender and gender-diverse HCPs,” so nonbinary and trans clinicians aren’t analyzed separately in these results.
NHIS doesn’t ask about workplace discrimination, stigma or hiding one’s identity at work.
“Workplace experiences may represent one potential contributor to the disparities observed in this study, although this mechanism could not be evaluated with the NHIS,” wrote the researchers, whose cross-sectional data can show an association but can’t prove cause.
“The relatively small sample of sexual minority HCPs limited statistical precision and precluded disaggregation of results by sex and race and ethnicity,” the paper says. There are no separate numbers for gay men, lesbians or bisexual people, or for Black or Latino LGB clinicians, and the 41.2% depression figure carries a 95% confidence range of 32.4% to 50.7%.
Some people may misreport or skip the sexual orientation question over stigma and privacy worries, the authors noted, and clinicians who had not worked within the past year weren’t counted. That leaves out some who had left health care because of burnout or discrimination.
Streed on the New Data: ‘The Story Here Is Nothing New’
Even with age, sex, race and ethnicity, income, education and other factors held constant, LGB clinicians’ odds of depression stayed about three times as high, an adjusted odds ratio of 3.05, and 4.81 for skipped prescriptions.
“The story here is nothing new,” Streed wrote.
In a 2021 JAMA Network Open study of 26,123 graduating U.S. medical students, 23.3% of LGB students reported mistreatment tied to their sexual orientation, compared with 1.0% of straight students. The LGB students had higher odds of burnout, too.
The new paper “contributes to the burgeoning research,” Streed wrote, and he’s done some of it. His 2024 survey of eight academic medical centers found burnout in 181 of 382 sexual and gender minority attending physicians, or 47.4%, and in 35.4% of other attendings.
Ehrenfeld was sworn in as president of the American Medical Association in June 2023. “Dr. Ehrenfeld is the first openly gay person to serve as AMA president,” the AMA said.
“Institutional investment in sexual minority wellness and support may be an essential step toward achieving health equity for sexual minority HCPs,” the study concludes.
Streed’s last line: “If we do not take action to address these disparate outcomes, we will be facing a rapid erosion in the ability to treat and meet the needs of the patients and communities we are sworn to serve.”
