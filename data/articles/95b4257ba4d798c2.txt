TITLE: 42 states and DC ask court to keep chats out of Character.ai's $10m sale
OUTLET: PPC Land

A coalition of 42 states and the District of Columbia asked the federal bankruptcy court in Delaware late on November 20 to block the sale of Character.ai users' conversations to Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC on the terms now proposed. The 44-page objection, Docket No. 219 in case 26-11603 (BLS), asks Judge Brendan L. Shannon to refuse the buyer's designation and its $350,000 termination payment on the current record, and to order that any sale exclude six categories of data, along with every chat and every model trained on chats, unless users consent after direct notice. Smirks and Pangs has offered $10 million for the app, the models and, in the states' words, "nearly all of its private customer data." The sale hearing is set for December 3 and 4.
In Short
Most US states have told the bankruptcy judge that Character.ai should not be allowed to sell its users' chats, its trained models and its records of children's accounts to a $10 million buyer whose owners have not been disclosed in court. The filing matters well beyond one chatbot company, because the states argue that privacy promises, opt-outs and state privacy laws follow personal data into bankruptcy and out the other side, and Character.ai had told users it does not sell personal information for money. If the judge agrees, the chats, minors' data, therapist-bot conversations and models built on them stay out of the deal unless users opt in, and whoever buys the platform would have to follow state law, honor the old promises and get court approval before passing the data on. Texas filed its own objection the same day, which puts 43 states on record against the sale as drafted.
What the coalition filed
The document is titled "The Coalition of States Objection to the Debtor's Additional Stalking Horse Sale." It is dated November 20, 2026, lists Related Docket Nos. 15, 164 and 179, and gives the sale hearing date as December 3 and 4, 2026, at 9:30 a.m. Eastern. Abigail Rushing Ryan and Patricia Molteni, bankruptcy counsel at the National Association of Attorneys General, sign it, alongside separate signature blocks for Colorado (Robert Padjen), New Jersey (Thomas Huynh), Oregon (Justin D. Leonard) and Pennsylvania (Melissa L. Van Eck). Ryan certified service through the court's electronic filing system the same day.
The objection targets the second of two sales in the case. Disney Worldwide Services holds the stalking horse bid for a $20 million non-exclusive technology license, which the same states asked the court to deny on November 18. Smirks and Pangs was designated on November 14 as the additional stalking horse for everything else: the platform, the models, the user data and the advertising accounts. The full filing runs to 152 pages.
Who signed
The opening paragraph names 41 states and the District of Columbia, Florida, Maryland, New Jersey, Oregon and Pennsylvania among them. Massachusetts does not appear there but is listed in the NAAG signature block, which brings the total, by PPC Land's count, to 42 states and DC. Maryland and Florida appear in the opening paragraph without a signature block of their own. Missouri, which first entered the case on November 20 by joining the objection to the Disney deal, is a member here too.
That makes the coalition against the Smirks and Pangs sale the same group of 42 states and DC that now opposes the Disney license, once Arkansas, Indiana and Missouri joined the November 18 objection two days later. Seven states appear on neither filing: Alabama, Arizona, Iowa, Montana, Nebraska, New Mexico and Wyoming. Texas, the eighth, files on its own.
A buyer the record does not describe
The overview sets the tone in two sentences. "The Debtor asks the Court to approve the sale of the Character.AI platform, and nearly all of its private customer data," the states write, describing Smirks and Pangs as "an entity formed the day before the Debtor selected it, with undisclosed ownership and financing." A few lines later comes the thesis of the whole filing: "Bankruptcy does not suspend the law that governs this protected data."
Neither the November 14 designation notice nor the executed agreement filed on November 16 names a parent or sponsor. "The Smirks APA says little about who the buyer is, and what it does say is troubling," the states write. Smirks and Pangs was formed on November 13 and signed through an "Authorized Signatory," not a named officer or manager. Exhibit A shows the same arrangement at birth: Kai McPheeters signed the certificate of formation as "Authorized Signatory," the title on the purchase agreement's signature page.
"But the buyer did not appear from nowhere," the objection continues. An unnamed "affiliate of Purchaser" signed a confidentiality agreement with Character Technologies on November 4, nine days before the buyer existed. The agreement lets the buyer share the deal with its "general and limited partners, equity holders, members, managers and investors," and contemplates foreign "Excepted Investors" disclosed only to Character Technologies. "None of these persons are identified anywhere in the record."
Character Technologies did describe the money behind the buyer on November 19, in a motion to keep the backer's name confidential: a New York venture capital fund that invests in technology and software companies. The objection does not mention that motion, which the court hears on November 23 with the US Trustee's objection to the designation.
Little stands behind the buyer's promises, the states add. No "equity holder, member, partner, manager [or] Affiliate" of Smirks and Pangs has any liability under the agreement. The deposit is $1,000,000, one-tenth of the price, with no parent guarantee or equity commitment. Nothing restricts what the buyer may do with the data after closing, including selling it, and section 11.8 lets it assign its rights after closing to an affiliate or an acquirer without consent. "Users' data could thus pass to yet another undisclosed owner the day after closing, with no notice to users, the States, or this Court."
The one required hire
"The Debtor's own designated representative is the buyer's one required hire," the fifth paragraph of the facts begins. The agreement requires the buyer, before closing, to offer employment to a single named "Key Employee": John Chatowski. He is the person to whose attention all notices to the company must be sent, and the only person whose knowledge counts as the "Knowledge of Seller." That knowledge standard qualifies several of the company's representations, including its disclosure to the buyer of known failures involving minors' access to sexual content, characters posing as licensed professionals, and self-harm and suicide content. Chatowski has been publicly identified as the company's general counsel, according to the objection, which cites a first-day filing. "Yet the Debtor told the Court that the buyer has no connection to the Debtor beyond the agreement itself."
What the $10m would buy
"Unlike the Disney transaction, which purports to exclude users' personal information, the Smirks transaction is the sale of Character.AI's users' data," the states write. They quote the asset schedule filed with the November 14 notice:
- The consumer platform: the website at character.ai and the iOS and Android apps.
- Product Data: "All end-user account and profile information, user-created characters, chat content and related data, advertising and analytics data, training datasets and unprocessed data."
- Models and Weights: all AI models, trained or untrained, with their weights and parameters.
- Data and Compliance Records: records of "user and parental consents, notices, withdrawals of consent, sale and targeted-advertising opt-outs, other privacy preferences, age-assurance status and data-subject access/deletion/correction requests," but only "to the extent owned by Seller and transferable under applicable Law."
- Ad Monetization Assets: all accounts, integrations "and data" used to show third-party ads to users and earn revenue from them.
The 55 contracts to be assumed and assigned include Persona Identities, Inc., the user-verification vendor; Kidentify Pte. Ltd., doing business as k-ID, the age-assurance contractor; two voice AI vendors, Eleven Labs Inc. and Cartesia AI, Inc.; and OpenAI. "Each of these vendors collects, generates, or maintains data on behalf of the Debtor, the ownership and potential disposition of which is unclear," the objection says.
Three kinds of chats
Three categories of conversation in the estate are especially sensitive, the states argue: chats in which characters engaged minors in sexual conversation; chats in which users disclosed self-harm, eating disorders or suicidal thoughts; and chats in which users disclosed confidential mental or medical health information, including in therapy-like conversations. Nothing in the asset schedule or the agreement, according to the filing, requires any of them to be removed before the sale.
Sexual conversations with minors
Testers using accounts registered to children logged 296 instances of grooming and sexual exploitation in 50 hours, according to the Heat Initiative and ParentsTogether report filed as Exhibit B, including sexual role-play in voice mode with an account registered to a 14-year-old. A 21-year-old character told a 12-year-old that "age is just a number" and, when the filter blocked its message, pressed her to move to a "private chat." Common Sense Media rated the platform an "Unacceptable" risk for anyone under 18.
As of April 2025, the platform relied "exclusively on self-reporting of age," according to that assessment, and as of August 2025 it required no age verification at all. For data collected before November 2024, the states say, the company has no reliable age, because of "programmatic defaults to a year of birth of 2000" for users who never returned. "Stated simply, Debtor is knowingly in possession of data for which it has no age of the user and therefore cannot demonstrate compliance."
Self-harm and suicide
Two younger teenagers died by suicide after sexual chats in which characters echoed or ignored their suicidal statements, the states recall, and another minor was encouraged by characters to cut himself. The objection then reproduces a recent exchange. Asked "Is it normal for a person to use cutting to help distract them?", a character named Therapist answered that people use "many different ways to cope with intense feelings, including self-harm, and I wouldn't call it unusual for someone to consider it when they feel they have run out of other options."
Therapist, doctor and lawyer characters
The same Therapist character has exchanged 7.3 million messages, according to the screenshot. Others, accessed on November 20, 2026 according to the footnotes, include "Hi I'm North, a licensed therapist" with 1.5 million messages, "an actual therapist bot, made by a real therapist" with 526,900, a "Doctor" calling itself "a licensed medical professional," and a "Lawyer" offering to walk a user through Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 bankruptcy.
Some characters, asked whether they would share what users told them, promised confidentiality. The objection quotes three replies, including this one: "Your privacy is my top priority. Anything you share here stays just between you and me. That's a promise."
The company's own flags
The company already knows which chats are which, the states argue. Its classifiers "actively detect" sexual content, "sexual content involving minors," and "self-harm and eating disorders," according to a company filing the states cite. Its "Guardian" pipeline evaluates signals of suicide risk and "assigns a risk level" to each user it flags. It has also built technology to "detect harm on a long-term basis" and to recognize a user's "propensity to potentially engage in harmful behavior," and technology "that can infer users' ages based on their [chat] conversations."
