TITLE: Global Commerce Graph calls digital ad auctions a 98% slot machine
OUTLET: news.lavx.hu

In a Nov. 11 essay, Global Commerce Graph argues that Google and Meta ad auctions are tuned like slot machines, extracting advertiser margins until businesses work at break-even or exit.
Global Commerce Graph published an essay Nov. 11 arguing that Google and Meta ad auctions are calibrated like slot machines, leaving advertisers with no economic surplus. The 98% Slot Machine
The piece, posted on the company's website, describes a daily ritual: a founder opens an ad dashboard, sees a small loss, and reads green percentage metrics as progress. Impressions up 14%. Click-through rate up 0.2%. Cost per click down four cents. The founder funds another week instead of closing the account.
Players walk away from a slot machine that pays 50%. A machine that returns 92% to 98% keeps them pulling the lever with small wins and flashing lights. Ad auctions work the same way. A full loss on day one would send advertisers to sales staff. A $1,000 spend that returns $900 sends them back to fix landing pages and copy.
The auction model has a structural flaw, according to Global Commerce Graph. Platforms need advertiser margins to fall to zero. Early inventory is cheap, so early advertisers earn high returns. As more businesses bid, prices rise to the highest amount an advertiser can tolerate while breaking even on gross margin. Any surplus margin attracts competitors, and within six months that surplus moves to the platform's revenue. Winners gain the right to fulfill orders at cost.
Most businesses do not fail loudly, the essay says. They spend months or years in "one sale away" mode, burning seed capital, credit lines, or retained earnings. One morning the credit card fails on the ad account. The account stops receiving traffic. The business keeps no residual equity, no organic momentum, and no customer relationship. The platform reallocates those impressions to the next advertiser.
The essay argues that the squeeze rests on a false premise: that buyers can only be reached by renting attention from a platform. A 20-person packaging plant in Indiana and an agricultural tax advisory firm do not buy lookalike audiences or search keywords. They trade through guilds, supply chain ties, regional partner networks, and referral circles. Global Commerce Graph frames the ad auction as a crowded 30% slice of commerce where the house wins. The other 70% moves through direct relationships.
The way out, the essay concludes, is to leave the auction floor and build durable commercial relationships with buyers.
