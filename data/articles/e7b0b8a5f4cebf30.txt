Atropine Eye Drops Slow Myopia in Children With Exotropia
A single-hospital randomized trial in Nanjing, China found the 0.04% dose slowed nearsightedness progression more than lower doses over 12 months.
Updated on Nov. 23, 2026 in Children’s Health

A randomized trial of 264 children aged 6 to 12 with myopia and intermittent exotropia found that nightly 0.04% atropine eye drops produced less myopia progression than 0.01% or 0.02% doses over 12 months. The study was published online November 19, 2026, in JAMA Ophthalmology.
Why it matters
Myopia is a common childhood eye condition, and the trial suggests a higher atropine dose may slow its progression in children who also have exotropia. Results from one hospital in China are an early signal that will need replication before shaping treatment guidance elsewhere.
The trial reported 21.6% of children on 0.04% atropine had light sensitivity, and 84.8% of participants completed the 12-month assessment. Whether these short-term effects persist beyond the study period has not yet been established.
The players
JAMA Ophthalmology
A peer-reviewed clinical journal published by the American Medical Association that covers eye disease research and treatment.
The details
Children were randomly assigned to nightly 0.01%, 0.02%, or 0.04% atropine eye drops and followed every three months, with the main outcome being change in cycloplegic spherical equivalent over 12 months. No child stopped treatment because of light sensitivity, which was the most frequently reported side effect at the highest dose.
Timeline
- September 2023: Trial began at a hospital in Nanjing, China.
- March 2026: Enrollment and follow-up concluded.
- November 19, 2026: Study published online in JAMA Ophthalmology.
Health Landscape
Low-dose atropine has been studied for childhood myopia since the ATOM2 trial in Singapore, and this Nanjing study tests whether higher concentrations offer greater control in a subgroup with exotropia. Its single-site design means broader adoption would depend on larger multicenter replication.
Parents of children with nearsightedness and exotropia may want to ask their eye doctor whether low-dose atropine is appropriate. The trial also reported light sensitivity as a side effect, which is worth discussing before starting treatment.
The takeaway
Higher-dose atropine slowed myopia progression more than lower doses in this trial, but the results come from one hospital and need confirmation. Families considering treatment should discuss the benefits and light-sensitivity side effects with an eye specialist.
