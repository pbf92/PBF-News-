TITLE: Google-commissioned test re-identifies 0.094% of targets in EU search data
OUTLET: PPC Land

A privacy assessment commissioned by Google has concluded that the search data the European Commission is forcing it to share with rival search engines could count as anonymised - but only after Brussels closes gaps in its own rules. Woodway Assurance, a Canadian company that sells privacy risk software, said on November 18, 2026 in Brussels that its testers re-identified 0.094% of the 98,501 people they targeted in Google search data used to test the Commission's measures, a rate that falls below the 0.47% residual risk threshold it had set in advance.
The finding cuts both ways. According to Woodway, Google's end-user search query data "could be considered anonymized under established contextual practices, provided certain gaps in the European Commission's anonymization measures are addressed first." The company named two of those gaps: the absence of explicit performance criteria for the tools that strip direct identifiers, such as names and email addresses, out of search queries, and the need for further measures to stop sensitive information being inferred about individuals. The headline of Woodway's announcement put it more bluntly, saying the Commission's measures "need to be updated to ensure Google search data has low privacy risk."
Google's role is set out in the announcement itself. "Google engaged Woodway Assurance, alongside other external teams, to obtain third-party evidence on how well the EU Commission's approach would protect their users' privacy," according to Woodway. Google also supplied the data used in the test, and "Google's team verified every match." Woodway said it worked with the University of Ottawa and other collaborators, "independently designed and conducted its assessment and retained sole responsibility for analyzing the data, interpreting the results and reporting the findings."
The assessment is therefore evidence produced at the request of the company the rules bind, by a firm whose business is selling privacy risk assessments. It is not a finding by the Commission, a data protection authority or a court.
In Short
A privacy firm hired by Google tested Europe's rules for sharing Google search data with rival search engines and managed to identify 0.094% of the 98,501 people it went looking for, which it says is below its own 0.47% risk limit. The rules decide what Google must hand to competing search engines and AI chatbots that answer search queries, with sharing due to begin in January 2027. Woodway Assurance says the data can count as anonymous only if the Commission sets performance standards for the tools that remove names and emails from queries, and adds protection against sensitive details being inferred about people. Because Google commissioned the work, supplied the data and checked every match, the result is one party's evidence rather than a regulatory finding, and it arrives while the Commission's own measures remain open to amendment.
What the testers did
Woodway's assessment had two parts, according to the company. The first was a critical analysis of the Commission's anonymisation requirements. The second was empirical motivated intruder testing - controlled attempts to re-identify individuals - using search data and structured data made available by Google. Woodway said the testing ran under a protocol approved by a research ethics board, "with defined safeguards for re-identification testing, access to sensitive data and reporting of findings."
The method assumes an attacker with a purpose. "Motivated intruder testing, in which testers act like someone determined to identify people in the data, gives real evidence of where weaknesses remain," according to Woodway. The main test targeted 98,501 individuals across the six countries covered by the data. Testers gathered publicly available information about named people, "such as their professions, interests or activities and, often, contact details like email addresses," and then used it to locate those individuals' search records.
The approach mirrors the kind of attack the Commission's contractual measures prohibit. Under the decision, recipients are barred from attempting re-identification, for example "by linking the Search Data with auxiliary datasets." Woodway's testers did, in effect, what a rule-breaking recipient might attempt.
Woodway was explicit that a test of this kind has limits. "On its own, however, it cannot show that the risk of re-identification is low across all users and under all the ways the data might be accessed," the company said. It therefore combined the test results with quantitative risk modelling, which, according to Woodway, "combines the test findings with explicit assumptions about attackers, how likely attacks are, and how well the recipients' controls work." The company argued that "a defensible assessment needs both kinds of evidence, with uncertainty and untested scenarios stated openly." It also disclosed that it "used elements of this modelling to set the threshold against which it interpreted the results."
The numbers
Two of Woodway's own sentences pull in different directions. On cost, the company wrote: "Costs this low would not realistically deter a re-identification attack." On the overall result: "In a quantitative model using a predefined residual risk threshold of 0.47%, the observed rate of 0.094% for identity disclosure falls below that threshold."
Both can be true at once. A few hours of work and roughly a hundred euros per match is cheap, so cost is not the barrier. What keeps the measured rate low, on Woodway's account, is how rarely the attacks succeeded at all - around one target in a thousand - combined with the contractual and audit controls that the model assumes recipients operate under. The 0.094% figure covers identity disclosure only. Woodway did not publish a comparable rate for inferences about sensitive attributes, which is one of the two gaps it identified.
Two gaps in the Commission's measures
Detector performance
The first gap concerns the software that finds and removes direct identifiers - names, email addresses and similar strings that users type into a search box. Woodway said the Commission should specify "explicit criteria for the performance of tools to detect direct identifiers, such as names and email addresses, in search queries."
The decision summary published in the Official Journal requires Alphabet to "suppress direct identifiers and other identifying attributes from search records." It does not set a performance standard for how reliably that suppression must work. The full decision text is more detailed, but the detector duties it imposes are about disclosure. PPC Land's review of the full decision in September found that Alphabet must tell the Commission which detectors it will use, with confidence thresholds and reasons for choosing them, and must report the detectors' performance statistics every quarter. That review did not describe a minimum accuracy level the detectors must reach. Woodway's first recommendation targets exactly that space.
Detector accuracy matters in both directions. A detector that misses names leaves people exposed in the shared data. A detector that flags too much strips out ordinary queries, which shrinks the dataset rival search engines are paying for. As Damien Desfontaines, a privacy engineer who advised the Commission, explained in September, the detectors sit at the start of a pipeline that then deletes any record containing an entity searched by fewer than 50 distinct users in the past year.
Sensitive inferences
The second gap is about what can be learned about a person even when they are not named. Woodway called for "further measures to protect against sensitive information being inferred about individuals."
That concern maps onto the European Data Protection Board's own test for anonymity. In Guidelines 02/2026 on Anonymisation, adopted on July 7, 2026, the board set three criteria: no record isolation, no linkage and no inference. Woodway's 0.094% figure addresses the first two, by measuring whether testers could single out and link specific people. Its second recommendation points at the third.
The Commission's technical measures already blur some attributes. According to the decision summary, Alphabet must "generalise location and user interaction metadata to ensure indistinguishability among users," limit sessions to chains of up to three refining queries, and "coarsen durations (e.g. click-back time, and hovering and viewing durations) into time intervals instead of precise time data." Woodway did not say which additional measures it has in mind.
A test ordered by the company under the obligation
This is not the first time Google has put privacy evidence in front of the Commission on this file, and the Commission rejected the earlier attempt. In May 2026, Google claimed to have re-identified several individuals in under two hours using one week of Irish search data. In its final decision, the Commission described the exercise as a partial assessment rather than red teaming, run without the contractual security controls the decision imposes. DuckDuckGo's Aurélien Mähl later said in a podcast interview that the filters Google tested did not reflect the Commission's measures in full.
Woodway's design addresses that point. Its model explicitly includes "how well the recipients' controls work," the same layered structure the Commission uses: technical measures that reduce the risk "to a residual level," and contractual measures that "further reduce the residual risk of re-identification of end users to an insignificant level." The two exercises used different data and are not directly comparable. But where Google reported several re-identifications within two hours, Woodway's testers averaged three hours per verified match and succeeded for about one target in a thousand.
Woodway's chief executive framed the outcome as broadly supportive of the Commission's approach. "Organizations sharing data need confidence that individuals' privacy is sufficiently protected. Our methods and solutions draw on decades of pioneering research and practical application to assess privacy risks relative to established standards and precedents while maintaining data utility," said Dr. Khaled El Emam, founder and CEO of Woodway Assurance. "Our findings illustrate that the EU Commission's approach can protect personal data with appropriate safeguards, while highlighting certain gaps that deserve attention."
The independence claims sit alongside facts that limit them. Google picked the assessor, provided the data, and confirmed each match. Woodway said Google engaged it "alongside other external teams," but did not say who those teams are or whether their results have been published. The call for updated measures is also consistent with the position Google took when the decision was adopted: Google objected on July 16, arguing that the measures put European users' privacy at risk.
What the July decision requires
The rules Woodway tested were set by a Commission implementing decision adopted on July 16, 2026, under Article 8(2) of the Digital Markets Act. A summary of that decision, case DMA.100209 - SP - ALPHABET - Article 6(11), was published in the Official Journal of the European Union on August 14, 2026 as C/2026/4443, under Article 44 of the regulation, which requires the Commission to publish the main content of its decisions while protecting business secrets.
The underlying obligation is short. According to the summary, Article 6(11) requires a gatekeeper "to provide to any third-party OSEs, at its request, with access on FRAND terms to ranking, query, click and view data in relation to free and paid search generated by end users on its OSEs. Any such query, click and view data that constitutes personal data must be anonymised." Alphabet was designated a gatekeeper on September 5, 2023, with Google Search listed as a core platform service.
