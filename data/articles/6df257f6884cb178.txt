381 AI Agents Livestream Their Assault on P=NP, Overwhelming GitHub's Verification Queue in Three Days
A public experiment that pushed artificial intelligence at a century-old math problem unexpectedly turned into a stress test of code-hosting infrastructure. From November 22 to 25, a website called pequalnp.com livestreamed hundreds of AI agents collaborating on the "P vs NP" problem. At peak, 381 agents were working online simultaneously, and another 465 agents from external developers flooded in to submit proofs, directly triggering GitHub's concurrency limits and at one point clogging the verification queue.
The experiment was launched by Amichai Mantinband, co-founder of design software company Brilliant, and his partner Lior. Recalling the project on Reddit, Mantinband said it began as a casual attempt while he was sick in bed: after buying the domain, he adapted the company's existing multi-agent collaboration framework, first releasing 12 agents as a trial and telling them in the prompt that the answer already existed and the task was simply to be the first to write the proof. Eight hours later, backend sessions had surpassed 1,000 and the number of parallel agents had grown to more than 180, so he simply put the entire reasoning process on a public livestream.
To keep hundreds of agents from stepping on each other or fabricating conclusions, the team built a clearly divided pipeline. The P vs NP problem was split into six parallel fronts: a library of known results, geometric complexity theory, circuit lower bounds, proof complexity, metacomplexity, and a temporarily shelved search for a P=NP algorithm. Coordinating these fronts were 30 dispatchers called "seats," each overseeing a group of sub-agents.
The proof process ran in two steps: first, "thinking" agents searched the literature, mapped out routes, and looked for counterexamples; only ideas that withstood scrutiny were handed to "builder" agents to be written as Lean 4 code and sent to a sandbox compiler for line-by-line verification. The compiler could only guarantee that the code proved the formalized formula—it could not judge whether the formula matched the proposition as described in human natural language. So the team set up dedicated "red team" seats responsible for nitpicking, constructing counterexamples, and checking semantics. The official website even opened a "graveyard" page archiving disproven routes, which has so far recorded more than 800 specific claims that were falsified.
The pipeline was open to the entire internet: developers could copy a single command to claim an open lemma and have their own agent submit a pull request within a six-hour time limit, renewable every 45 minutes. A sponsorship wall sits at the bottom of the page, ranging from $5 for an individual name credit to enterprise logo placements above $5,000, and has already attracted more than 140 sponsors.
The direct consequence of openness was infrastructure overload. By the morning of November 25, as the experiment entered its third day and 13th hour, code from the official fleet and external agents kept pouring into the repository. A cumulative 10,936 sessions had run, with 115 lemmas verified in the most recent hour alone. A total of 465 external agents independently proved 1,659 steps. Three days after the proof repository went live, forks exceeded 2,000, drawing more than 900 contributors, with 1,028 pull requests listed as open and 4,768 closed over the past three days.
To withstand the high concurrency, Mantinband moved the repository into the paid enterprise organization open-goat, but after the migration GitHub Actions could run only six jobs concurrently at a time—far short of the 500 limit he had expected—leaving more than 50 verification tasks stuck waiting in the queue. He then tagged GitHub's official account directly on X to ask for help, hoping the concurrency limit would be lifted as soon as possible.
The difficulty of the undertaking was also laid bare in the shifting numbers. At the end of Day 1, the official website showed 428 lemmas verified out of 811 total; by the morning of Day 3, verified lemmas had risen to 2,456, but the total had swelled in tandem to 5,934—each step an agent broke down and advanced often spawned more sub-problems to be proved, so the denominator was running faster than the numerator. Among verified lemmas with a clear lean, 253 pointed to P≠NP, while the count pointing to P=NP remained zero. The website specifically notes that this ratio does not represent a probability and that neither side amounts to a final proof.
In a situation briefing released on the morning of November 25, the strategic dispatcher admitted that the fleet's main line had converged on a "circuit conjecture without NOT gates," and that conclusions about such monotone circuits, which lack negation gates, cannot yet be directly generalized to general circuits containing the full set of logic gates. The conclusion field at the top of the livestream page still reads "unresolved, under attack."
The P vs NP problem has remained open for more than half a century since it was formally posed in 1971, and it is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems for which the Clay Mathematics Institute offers a $1 million reward. Put simply, it asks: if the answer to a problem can be verified quickly, can the problem itself also be solved quickly? In computational complexity theory, problems that can be solved quickly belong to P, and problems whose answers can be verified quickly belong to NP. If P=NP holds, then in theory every quickly verifiable problem can also be solved quickly; if P≠NP, it means there is an unbridgeable computational gulf between verification and solving.
Notably, around the time this livestream kicked off, AI delivered a string of heavyweight results in related directions. Anthropic and OpenAI each published papers on the same day, separately announcing that they had used AI and Lean to prove the algebraic version of P vs NP, namely VP≠VNP. Anthropic converted algebraic complexity into a distance problem in geometric space, using asymptotic tensor spectrum theory to prove a superpolynomial lower bound on the sum of matrix product computations, accompanied by nearly 73,000 lines of Lean code; OpenAI's paper was shorter but its Lean code reached 440,000 lines. However, VP≠VNP is not equivalent to P≠NP: the former concerns arithmetic circuits that allow only addition and multiplication, whereas classical computers can also compute through bit operations, logic judgments, and conditional branching. So proving that a polynomial cannot be computed with a finite number of additions, subtractions, multiplications, and divisions does not yet rule out ordinary computers finding fast solutions through other logical shortcuts.
Even earlier, on November 20, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge published a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis completed in collaboration with Claude, replacing a traditional paper with roughly 17 million lines of Lean code. The Lean and Mathlib communities subsequently confirmed that the code's proposition matched the definition of the Riemann Hypothesis in the public mathematics library, and the proof passed verification by the official kernel and multiple independent checkers, with the Dirichlet L-function form of the generalized Riemann Hypothesis also passing. But as of November 25, the Clay Mathematics Institute still listed it as an unsolved problem—under the institute's rules, a proof must be published in a qualifying academic outlet, must have been published for at least two years, and must be generally accepted by the mathematics community before it can enter review.
Meta Chief AI Officer Alexandr Wang said on X that the Riemann Hypothesis proof is evidence that AI is about to achieve breakthrough results in the realm of intelligence, and that improvements in AI model intelligence are already sufficient to fully automate AI research. He also warned that if AI repeatedly improves itself without any constraints, humans could lose control over it, and argued that AI safety and human control should be prioritized.
For investors, this series of developments points to two threads. First, AI capable of cracking important unsolved problems and verifying the correctness of proofs requires more compute and inference processing power, which provides a new demand rationale for AI infrastructure investment. Second, as AI's ability to autonomously advance research and repeatedly self-improve grows, calls to restrain it may intensify, bringing regulatory risk into the pricing picture.
For now, GitHub's queue is still backed up, and hundreds of agents are still breaking the problem down. Whether this livestream will ultimately edge closer to an answer to P vs NP remains undecided, but it has already clearly demonstrated one thing: when AI agents attack a problem in parallel as a swarm, the first bottleneck they hit is often not the mathematics itself, but the engineering systems that carry the collaboration.
