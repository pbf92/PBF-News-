TITLE: Q&A: For King & Country’s Joel Smallbone on Christian Art Reaching Mainstream
OUTLET: Christianity Today

For King & Country is one of the top contemporary acts in Christian music, with four Grammy awards, numerous chart-topping hits, and an annual Christmas tour that’s about to resume nationwide. Joel David Smallbone, who leads the pop duo alongside his brother Luke, is also a rising name in film. Two years after playing his own father in Unsung Hero, Smallbone is headlining Angel Studios’ Drummer Boy, a Revolutionary War musical that outperformed box office expectations on its opening weekend.
Here, Smallbone speaks with CT on finding rare moments of stillness amid international fame, growing Christian influence in the entertainment space, and an anticipated “pause” after a long, prayerful journey to Drummer Boy.
This interview has been edited for length and clarity.
You’ve been touring, dropping albums, and making movies left and right. Fans will take all they can get. But how do you stay spiritually stable between all the big screens and stages?
I’m fortunate to be married to a superhero—the greatest partner of my life—and we’ve been married for 13 years now. It’s funny how even in Scripture, so much of the allegory for connectedness to God was a bride and a groom—a marriage.
After that? Community, community, community. I’m so proud, in music, to do it with my family. But I was also desperately proud of the incredible humans on our Drummer Boy crew. You’ve got to stay in a community of some sort. That’s sort of our church, in a sense.
Finally, you’ve got to have stillness. Yesterday, I set my timer for 10 minutes, and I just breathed and prayed and said the Lord’s Prayer.
You mentioned your community on these projects almost doubling as your church. As you travel between cities for work, is that how church works for Joel Smallbone?
Well, I want to be really careful about this terminology because, yes, the church is the body of Christ with different members. But there is order, and I believe in order. So we filmed in Ireland, for instance, and we found this incredibly diverse nondenominational church that we would actually go to during the weekend and had a lovely time.
But I feel like we got to have a form of that on set. Every morning we would share and pray. It felt like an extension of what I think the body of Christ functions like on a day-to-day level—this idea that you can sort of have church and community fellowship in all these different environments, not just in the four walls on a Saturday or Sunday.
When you walk into a local church, I imagine some people must recognize and approach you. Does that distract or take away from the church experience for you?
People are going to say hi, but I like people. People are cool. And the people I met in Ireland were very sweet. Luke’s and my mission really is to uplift. We’ve been given so much opportunity by the grace of God. So anytime someone comes up to me and says, “Hey, that song met me right where I was,” I just take it as radical proof that I—this Australian who lives in America now—can do something in Nashville, Tennessee, that reaches a tiny little nondenominational church in Ireland.
How do you see Christian influence growing in the film space?
I think it’s a really beautiful time for the religious arts. If you go back, the church is responsible for so much of the greatest art ever made and stories ever told. From stained-glass windows to the Sistine Chapel, they were commissioned by the church. And then somewhere along the way in the 21st century, it felt like we lost our way a bit; we became second fiddle at best or copycats at worst. In my opinion, we did it to ourselves. We put ourselves in our own corner.
When I read the words of Jesus, I just don’t see that. The last thing Jesus said was “Go out.” Not go inward. Go outward and make disciples. We’re all fumbling, stumbling humans, shadow and light all mixed up. But whenever I see people like Dallas Jenkins or Andy and Jon Erwin or the Harmon brothers with Angel—whenever I see people go out and just try, I just want to give them all a standing ovation. Try. Fail. That’s what all of our heroes did in the Bible.
As Christians, the guy that we named ourselves after was a guy who told stories. That’s what he did. He pulled people in, and he told fake stories with incredible truth packed into them.
If the pendulum is swinging back to push Christian art into the mainstream, what comes next?
I think we became afraid, at some point in the religious arts, to get down and dirty, if you will—to try and risk and fail. I look at folks like Forrest Frank, Brandon Lake, Josiah Queen, and Jamie MacDonald, and I’m just so proud that they’re there. There was a time I was really sad about where I felt the religious arts were going, and I thought it either had to evolve or it was going to die. The Spirit is speaking. I think we’re on the precipice of something really amazing.
How do you take risks to become more inviting to the culture while also standing firm on truth?
Drummer Boy is a great example. It has been riddled with failure. We went location scouting in Ireland on January 5 of this year. It was a scouting trip taken on faith. We weren’t even fully funded yet. My wife, Moriah, and I took a week away, switched our phones off, hunted down an island, and prayed. We flew back to the States, and six weeks later, we were back in Ireland. We did two months of production, three months of editing and touring, two months of promotion, and then we were the No. 3 movie in America this week. It has been a tragedy and a triumph.
And man, I’m telling you, I just don’t think it could have happened if not for the grace of God. There’s something about biting off more than you can chew and letting the Spirit do more than you could do on your own. Faith is believing in things that are unseen, things that are yet to be. This is prophecy. You see something that is aligned with God and the will of God, that has goodness and light in it, and you go out and chase it.
If there’s a Creator out there who knows us intimately, what have we got to lose? Dream big dreams. I think Jesus was so much about, “Do you believe? Do you have faith?” And he spoke so vehemently against worry and anxiety. The worst thing we can do is not believe.
If we could peek into your prayers and dreams for what’s next, what would it be? You’ve got the film credits, the albums, the concerts. Will the next thing be live theater?
I doubt it will be that, but I appreciate your creativity! For the first time in my life, I don’t know, and I’m okay. Drummer Boy, to me, is a finale of sorts. This song was introduced to us at the beginning of the band, it’s something we’ve been working on for almost a decade, and it’s here. I’m going into next year with my family with great expectations and open hands—for a control freak—of “What next?” And I’m kind of liberated that for the first time in my adult life, I don’t really have a plan. I have belief.
A spiritual mentor of mine gave this analogy: In ancient Jerusalem, there were these different entrances and exits. You had the entrance and exit of Shalom—it was the entrance and exit of peace. And you would have another exit that was for going out to battle. This season for me was sort of going out, putting on the armor, and going out to battle. But it’s important, this mentor said, to go back through the door of peace. To recalibrate.
Because if you just keep going when you’re in the battle, at some point you forget what you’re fighting for. You forget who you’re fighting for. Are you winning? Are you losing? Where are you going? What’s the purpose? So you fight the good fight, you run the race, and then you sort of pause for a minute and go, “Okay, what next?” I’m looking forward to that pause.
