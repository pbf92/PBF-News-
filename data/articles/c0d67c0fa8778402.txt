TITLE: Celiac Disease Linked to Increased Risk of Eating Disorders: JAMA
OUTLET: Medical Dialogues

Celiac Disease Linked to Increased Risk of Eating Disorders: JAMA
A cohort study from Sweden published in JAMA Network Open found that individuals diagnosed with celiac disease had an increased risk of developing eating disorders. The findings highlight the importance of monitoring psychological and nutritional health in patients with celiac disease. The study was conducted by Jialu Y. and colleagues.
The risk of eating disorders across the years in biopsy-proven patients with celiac disease, along with their biological first-degree relatives (parents, siblings, and children), was examined through a national multigenerational cohort study conducted in Sweden from January 2002 to December 2023, analyzed in December 2025 to August 2026.
From a detailed database of the histopathology departments in all 28 provinces of Sweden, investigators were able to identify 37,853 biopsy-confirmed cases of celiac disease within the years 2002 to 2023, along with 5 comparators from the general population per one celiac patient (a total number of 185,159 comparators), their biological first-degree relatives.
The main outcome was an eating disorder at the age of 6 years and above. Investigators calculated aHRs and time-varying aHRs applying Cox proportional hazard regressions and parametric survival models with sensitivity analyses that addressed information biases and confounding effects.
Key findings:
- Patient-level and familial risk assessments through statistical analysis of 37,853 patients with biopsy-verified celiac disease (23,704 females [62.6%]; median age at index date 28.7 years [IQR, 11.7–53.0]) and 185,159 matched general population reference subjects over a median follow-up of 10.9 years (IQR, 5.9–15.8 years) demonstrated unique patient-level vs familial risk trajectories.
- Over the follow-up period, 485 patients with celiac disease and 1,578 general population reference subjects developed an eating disorder.
- Celiac disease showed a significant 39% increased hazard of developing any eating disorder (aHR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.27–1.53).
- The elevated risk was more prominent within the first 5 years from the diagnosis of celiac disease.
- However, despite the increased hazard risk, the absolute risk remained low, with 1 excess eating disorder case per 222 patients with celiac disease per 20 years of follow-up.
- No statistically significant increased hazard for any eating disorder was noted in the biological first-degree relatives of patients with celiac disease, including 53,435 parents (aHR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.61–1.12), 48,743 full siblings (aHR, 1.11; 95% CI, 0.99–1.24), and 38,695 offspring (aHR, 1.05; 95% CI, 0.92–1.20).
To conclude, Swedish nationwide cohort study findings prove that the risk of having eating disorders is moderately increased in patients with celiac disease confirmed by biopsy, mainly in the first 5 years after the diagnosis, while the absolute risk is quite low and does not involve the first-degree biological relatives. In addition, the conclusions of this study highlight that it is important to have constant attention from clinicians on the eating behavior of these patients.
Reference:
Yao J, Sun J, Bergman D, et al. Eating Disorders in Individuals With Celiac Disease and Their Biological First-Degree Relatives. JAMA Netw Open. 2026;9(11):e2643815. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.43815
