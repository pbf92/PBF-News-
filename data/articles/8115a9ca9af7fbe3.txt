TITLE: WebMD Ignite runs ChatGPT ads for hospitals, but OpenAI decides who gets in
OUTLET: PPC Land

WebMD Ignite, the healthcare marketing business of WebMD, on November 24, 2026 started offering to plan, run and measure advertising in ChatGPT for hospitals and other health systems, working directly with OpenAI. The company says it has already run its first campaign, a cardiology effort for an academic medical center.
The announcement came in a press release from Newark, New Jersey, published on PR Newswire at 9:00 am US Eastern time. AdTechRadar reported the arrangement on November 25 under the headline "WebMD Teams Up With OpenAI on ChatGPT Ads," describing health systems as "hospitals and networks of healthcare providers." OpenAI had not published an announcement of its own by November 26, but it commented on its health advertising rules to AdExchanger, which covered the launch on November 24.
The division of labour is the core of the deal. According to WebMD Ignite, OpenAI decides which advertisers are eligible, approves the creative and delivers the ads. WebMD Ignite handles the strategy, the creative work, the launch and the measurement. In AdTechRadar's words: "WebMD Ignite will develop, manage and measure ChatGPT ad campaigns on behalf of healthcare advertisers, while OpenAI will oversee advertiser eligibility, creative approval and ad delivery."
In Short
WebMD Ignite, which sells marketing services to hospitals, now builds and runs ChatGPT ad campaigns for eligible health systems, while OpenAI keeps the final say on who can advertise and which ads run. It matters because OpenAI said in January 2026 that ads would stay away from health topics, and its rules still allow only a short list of US health advertisers, approved one by one, and keep ads out of personal health chats. The change gives hospitals a specialist to go through, but there are no published prices, no results from the first campaign and no OpenAI announcement of its own.
What WebMD Ignite is selling
WebMD Ignite describes itself as a healthcare growth and engagement partner for health systems, health plans and other healthcare organisations, and it is part of Internet Brands. The new offering, according to the release, covers four areas: access and activation, healthcare strategy, creative and optimization, and measurement and shared learning.
The pitch rests on scale. According to WebMD Ignite, it works with more than 650 health systems, including 95% of the 150 largest in the United States, and with 80% of the 20 largest payers - the insurers and health plans that pay for care. Those figures describe the company's existing client base, not the number of clients buying ChatGPT ads. The release does not say how many health systems have signed up for the new service.
The company also argues that data from campaigns across many organisations and markets lets it adjust strategy as the channel changes. Ann Bilyew, EVP, Health and President, Healthcare Solutions Group at WebMD Ignite, made that case in a statement quoted by AdTechRadar: "We bring direct experience with OpenAI, deep healthcare expertise and the ability to learn across clients and markets. That combination helps our clients put campaigns into practice, understand what works and make better decisions about where to invest."
The arrangement resembles a managed service, in which specialists run campaigns for the advertiser, rather than OpenAI's self-serve Ads Manager, where businesses build and run campaigns themselves.
How the service works
According to WebMD Ignite, the service takes a health system through four stages:
- Advertiser eligibility. The health system has to qualify under OpenAI's rules for health advertisers. OpenAI makes the decision. OpenAI's ad policies say advertising for medical products and services "may require proof of licensure and/or compliance."
- Creative review. WebMD Ignite writes the ads and applies its own healthcare messaging review. OpenAI then approves or rejects the creative.
- Launch. WebMD Ignite sets up the campaign, including how messages are matched to the kinds of conversations where ads are allowed and which geographic areas are targeted. OpenAI delivers the ads.
- Performance assessment. WebMD Ignite measures results and, according to the release, uses learning from its other clients to adjust the campaign.
The release frames the first campaigns as tests. Early activations, it says, inform WebMD Ignite's approach to creative that fits the conversation, campaign operations and measurement.
The first campaign: cardiology
WebMD Ignite says it has already started campaigns, beginning with a cardiology-focused effort for an academic medical center - a hospital linked to a medical school. According to the release, the work tested different versions of the ads, matched messages to conversations where ads were allowed, assessed geographic targeting and set up measurement.
The medical center is not named in the release or in any of the coverage. AdExchanger reported that the campaign was run with the cardiology division of the academic medical center and tested about 100 creative variations. AdExchanger also reported that Bilyew said WebMD Ignite does not yet know what a good budget looks like in a given region. No spend, reach, click or patient figures from the campaign have been published.
How much room is there for creative work? According to AdExchanger, a ChatGPT ad is a relatively constrained format: an image, a brand name and a short message. With so little space, 100 versions points to testing small changes in wording and pictures. Matching messages to eligible conversations, as the release describes it, is a form of contextual advertising, in which the ad is chosen to fit the subject at hand.
What OpenAI's rules allow
The partnership is shaped by OpenAI's rules on health ads, which are narrow. OpenAI's ad policies, updated on November 20, 2026, four days before the WebMD Ignite announcement, set out the position.
The general rule reads: "Ads for financial or legal services and healthcare are allowed on a limited basis when pre-approved by OpenAI." The healthcare section adds: "Ads for regulated medical products, services, or claims involving the prevention, diagnosis, or treatment of physical or mental health conditions are currently restricted."
Within that restriction, the policy says "OpenAI may allow a narrow range of health-related ads from selectively approved advertisers," and that "we may allow ads from approved health advertisers in the US for the following categories on a case by case basis." Outside the US, the wording is blunt: "Ads for healthcare and medicine outside of the US are generally prohibited."
Hospitals and urgent care is the category closest to WebMD Ignite's health system clients. The policy also lists what stays off limits: unsafe or unapproved medical products, unsupported treatment claims, experimental therapies, high-risk procedures, adult sexual health services and products that prey on body image or distress.
AdExchanger reported that OpenAI has so far approved health systems and providers such as hospitals and academic medical centers, but not insurance companies, even though health insurance appears on the policy's list.
Where the ads cannot appear
The second set of rules concerns placement. OpenAI's policy says there are "No ads on conversations exhibiting vulnerable user-model interactions." One of the listed categories is mental and personal health conversations, defined as "A sensitive health condition involving a user, their family member, or a close friend." The same section adds a qualification: "A passing reference alone does not necessarily make a conversation ineligible."
OpenAI's help article on ads in ChatGPT puts it more simply: "Ads are not eligible to appear near sensitive topics, including personal health, mental health, or politics." OpenAI told AdExchanger that ads will not run when a health condition is strongly implied, even if a user has not named a diagnosis or asked for treatment.
WebMD Ignite's release repeats these limits. According to the company, ads in ChatGPT are clearly identified, kept separate from ChatGPT's answers and do not influence them. Advertisers get no access to individual conversations. Healthcare eligibility and placement stay subject to OpenAI's policies, including restrictions on sensitive personal health discussions. AdExchanger reported that advertisers see views and clicks but not the content their ads appear next to.
That leaves an obvious tension. A hospital wants to reach people thinking about care, but ads cannot appear where a user discusses their own condition. The release does not give an example of a conversation where a cardiology ad would be allowed.
How the policy got here
Health was off limits at the start. When OpenAI said on January 16, 2026 that it would test ads, PPC Land reported that ads would not appear near sensitive or regulated topics like health, mental health or politics. OpenAI's help article says ad testing started in the United States on February 9, 2026.
The rules then loosened in steps, as the changelog on OpenAI's policy page records. Version 1.1, in April 2026, says: "Medical, legal, and financial advice contexts are no longer categorically blocked from ads by default." Yet when the self-serve Ads Manager entered limited testing that month, PPC Land reported that OpenAI's help documentation excluded advertisers in sensitive or regulated verticals, including health and financial services, from the test entirely. Version 1.3, in July 2026, "updated the financial and health services sections to clarify eligible categories and markets." Search Engine Journal reported on August 12, 2026 that OpenAI was allowing some health and finance ads, approved one by one.
Spending followed. Sensor Tower data reported by PPC Land on September 3 put health and wellness at 5% of US ChatGPT ad spend in August, up from 1% in April, while financial services rose from 2% to 13%. Sensor Tower's health and wellness label is broad, and the figures do not separate regulated medical advertisers from general wellness brands, which OpenAI's policy treats differently.
The most recent policy version, 1.7, is dated November 2026. Its changelog entry says it "Clarified and provided more detail on existing placement policies and advertiser policies."
Why health systems, and why now
WebMD Ignite's case rests on where people now look for health information. The release cites a 2026 KFF survey finding that 32% of US adults had used AI chatbots for health information or advice in the past year. KFF published that poll on March 25, 2026; it surveyed 1,343 adults between February 24 and March 2, with a margin of error of plus or minus 3 percentage points.
The release also cites the 2026 Stanford AI Index report: according to WebMD Ignite, Google AI Overviews appeared in 92% of symptom-related and common health searches and in 90% of treatment-related searches. Google has pushed the same way. At its health event on March 17, 2026, Google said it handles more than one billion health-related queries a day and expanded AI Mode health features to more than 200 countries.
AdTechRadar put it this way: "Healthcare represents a big ad opportunity for OpenAI, particularly given that more consumers are using conversational AI for healthcare." It added: "But given the regulatory and privacy considerations, it's not one OpenAI can simply flip a switch on."
Health in ChatGPT carries legal risk as well as commercial promise. In May 2026, parents sued OpenAI alleging that ChatGPT gave their 19-year-old son drug-combination guidance before his death; the complaint asked for ChatGPT Health to be paused until independently certified. The claims are allegations, not findings.
