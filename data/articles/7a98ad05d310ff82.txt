TITLE: Character.ai faces a privacy ombudsman before its users' data can be sold
OUTLET: PPC Land

A federal bankruptcy judge in Delaware today ordered the US Trustee to appoint a consumer privacy ombudsman in the Chapter 11 case of Character Technologies, the company behind Character.ai, finding that its planned sale "may involve a transfer of personally identifiable information" as well as "other sensitive personal information of its users." Judge Brendan L. Shannon signed the four-page order, Docket No. 181, after a hearing in Wilmington, at the request of US Trustee Andrew R. Vara and with the company's consent. The ombudsman's report is due no later than seven days before any hearing on a sale, transfer or lease of that information. It goes on the docket under seal first, shown in full only to the court, the company, the creditors' committee's proposed counsel, the state attorneys general and the US Trustee, and becomes public only once redactions are agreed and approved or ordered by the court.
In Short
A judge has ordered that an independent privacy reviewer, known as a consumer privacy ombudsman, be appointed before Character.ai's user data can change hands in the company's bankruptcy sale. The reviewer will tell the court how a sale would affect the people whose information is involved, and it decides which sensitive information beyond names, addresses and email addresses falls within the Bankruptcy Code's definition of personal data for the purposes of its report. The report is due seven days before any sale hearing, which on the current schedule means Thanksgiving Day, November 26, and it stays sealed until the parties or the judge settle what to black out, so the public version may arrive only days before the December 3 hearing.
What the judge signed
The order sits in case 26-11603 (BLS), In re Character Technologies, Inc., in the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware. Its title spells out the mechanism: "Order Directing the United States Trustee to Appoint a Consumer Privacy Ombudsman Pursuant to Section 332(a) of the Bankruptcy Code." It carries the reference "Re: D.I. 148," the competing proposed orders the company filed on November 12, and is dated "November 17th, 2026," in Wilmington, under Judge Shannon's name. It brings a consumer privacy ombudsman into the case for the first time.
The recitals explain why the court acted. The order was entered "upon request of Andrew R. Vara, the United States Trustee for Regions 3 and 9" and "as discussed on the record at the hearing held on November 17, 2026." The court found that the company "is pursuing a sale transaction that may involve a transfer of personally identifiable information (as defined in Section 101(41A) of the Bankruptcy Code, 'PII'), as well as other sensitive personal information of its users." It also found that "a hearing pursuant to Section 363(b)(1)(B) of the Bankruptcy Code will, therefore, be required to consider approval of the sale transaction." The final recital reads: "upon consent of the Debtor."
The operative instruction is in the first paragraph. Under section 332(a) and Rule 6004(g)(2) of the Federal Rules of Bankruptcy Procedure, "the U.S. Trustee is directed to appoint one (1) disinterested person (other than the U.S. Trustee) with the requisite experience applicable to the present case to serve as the consumer privacy ombudsman in the above-captioned Chapter 11 Case."
The order does not name that person. Nor does it name Disney, Smirks and Pangs, Character.ai's app, its chatbots or its users' conversations. The word "users" appears once, in the recital about sensitive personal information.
How the company came to consent
The ombudsman has been the subject of filings since the first week of the case. On October 14, attorneys general from 39 states and the District of Columbia reserved the right to seek one, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai faced 40 attorneys general over who could buy its users' chats. Their notice set out nine conditions for any buyer, among them honoring account-level advertising opt-outs and limiting the use of chats for training and advertising beyond what users had agreed to.
On October 27, the US Trustee objected to the company's bidding procedures (Docket No. 69, according to the docket). The company's reply the same day held that its privacy policy, as in force on the petition date, already permitted the sale and transfer of user data, so no ombudsman was required, while saying it would consent to one under appropriate circumstances, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai kept its $21.7m bid floor for Disney rivals despite 32 states. Two days later, the same coalition of states joined the US Trustee and told the court an ombudsman was necessary if any property holding users' personal information were sold, as PPC Land reported when 39 states and DC said Character.ai user data needed a privacy ombudsman.
The bidding procedures order that followed left the question open. On November 12, the company filed a "Notice of Filing of Competing Proposed Orders Directing the United States Trustee to Appoint a Consumer Privacy Ombudsman," with three exhibits, according to the docket. The notice's title describes every version as an order directing an appointment, which suggests the argument had moved from whether to have an ombudsman to how the role would work. On November 15, PPC Land asked the US Trustee Program about the status of those orders and whether the review would extend to users in the European Union and the United Kingdom. The Justice Department, which runs the program, declined to comment on November 16, and PPC Land added the exchange to its report on how Character.ai users' chats faced a $10m sale to Smirks and Pangs.
The signed order does not describe what separated the competing drafts. What it records is the outcome: a request from the US Trustee, a discussion on the record, and the company's consent.
How the process works under the order
According to the order, the ombudsman process has eight steps.
- Appointment. The US Trustee appoints one disinterested person, not the US Trustee, "with the requisite experience applicable to the present case" (paragraph 1). Section 332(a) of the Code sets the outer limit: no later than seven days before the sale hearing begins. Rule 6004(g)(2) requires the US Trustee to give notice of the appointment at least seven days before that hearing.
- Scope of work. "Subject to further order of the Court," the ombudsman performs the functions in section 332(b), "including providing to the Court information to assist the Court in its consideration of the facts, circumstances, and conditions of any and all proposed sales or leases of PII, which may include sensitive personal information determined by CPO to be included within 11 U.S.C. § 101 (41A)(B)(ii)" (paragraph 2).
- The report. The ombudsman files "a complete report (the 'Report') pursuant to 11 U.S.C. §§ 332 and 363(b), including supporting documentation, with the Court no later than seven (7) days prior to any hearing involving the sale, transfer, or lease of PII" (paragraph 3).
- Sealed filing. "The Report shall be initially filed under seal in its entirety" (paragraph 3).
- Redaction talks. The court, the company's counsel, proposed counsel to the official committee of unsecured creditors, counsel to the NAAG Client States, counsel for any other state attorney general that has entered an appearance, and the US Trustee receive the unredacted report "solely for the purpose" of discussing redactions. Sharing it for that purpose waives no privilege or protection (paragraph 3).
- Two-day window. If the parties and the US Trustee cannot agree on a proposed redaction "within two (2) days of the filing of the Report," they "shall jointly apply to the Court for resolution" (paragraph 3).
- Public version. Once an agreement is approved by court order, or the court rules by order on what to redact, "the CPO shall then file on the public docket a copy of the Report with the approved redactions." The parties keep the unredacted version confidential, and the US Trustee "shall not disclose the redacted parts of the Report (if any), consistent with 11 U.S.C. § 107(c)" (paragraph 3).
- Payment. The ombudsman "shall be compensated pursuant to Section 330 of the Bankruptcy Code, the applicable Fed. R. Bankr. P., and Local Rules upon approval by the Court of a request for compensation" (paragraph 6). Under section 330, that means fees paid from the estate after court review.
Two further paragraphs frame the work. Paragraph 5 requires the ombudsman to "comply with Section 332(c) of the Bankruptcy Code," the provision that bars an ombudsman from disclosing personally identifiable information obtained in the role. Paragraph 8 keeps the court's jurisdiction over "the implementation or interpretation of this Order."
Who sees the report, and when
The sealing provisions decide who reads the ombudsman's findings before the sale hearing. The order names five groups beyond the court.
The list leaves out several parties that have filed on the sale. The bidders are not on it: Disney Worldwide Services, the stalking horse for a technology license, and Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, the stalking horse for the app, models and user data. Nor are the advocacy groups whose letters about users' chats reached the docket on November 16. PPC Land covered them when Common Sense Media told the judge AI could unmask kids in Character.ai chats, when the Heat Initiative and ParentsTogether Action asked the judge to treat every Character.ai chat as a child's, and when the Consumer Federation of America warned about advertising technology companies and data brokers in What the therapist bot heard. Users themselves are not on the list either.
There is a route for them. Paragraph 4 states that nothing in the order "affects or waives the rights of parties in interest, by motion on notice, (i) to seek access to a document filed under seal or (ii) to seek to unseal a document filed under seal."
The definition doing the work
The order's second paragraph hands the ombudsman a determination that will shape the rest of the sale. The Bankruptcy Code defines personally identifiable information in two layers, in section 101(41A). The first covers information an individual gives a debtor in connection with obtaining a product or service from the debtor primarily for personal, family or household purposes: a first name or initial and last name, the address of a physical residence, an electronic address such as an email address, a telephone number dedicated to contacting the individual at that residence, a Social Security account number, or a credit card account number. The second layer, subparagraph (B), applies to information identified in connection with one or more of those items. Its clause (i) covers a birth date, a birth or adoption certificate number and a place of birth. Its clause (ii) is a catch-all for any other information concerning an identified individual that, if disclosed, will result in contacting or identifying that person physically or electronically.
That catch-all is where the order points. Paragraph 2 refers to "sensitive personal information determined by CPO to be included within 11 U.S.C. § 101 (41A)(B)(ii)." The same wording returns in paragraphs 3 and 7. It leaves the ombudsman to say which of the platform's other data, beyond account identifiers, is tied closely enough to an identified user to count.
The question is not abstract for this company.
