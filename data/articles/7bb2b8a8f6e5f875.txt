TITLE: No sale, no pay: Taboola links 60 brands with 21,000 creators
OUTLET: PPC Land

More than 60 brands that advertise through Connexity, the shopping-ads business Taboola bought in 2021, can now work with more than 21,000 content creators from Howl, an invite-only creator network Taboola acquired in August 2026, according to an announcement Taboola published on November 24, 2026. Brands in the program pay only when a creator's recommendation turns into a sale, not for views or impressions, according to the company. Taboola's headline names Vans, Champion, Timberland and Vitamix among the brands; the body of the announcement names none of them.
In Short
Taboola, best known for the "recommended stories" ads at the bottom of news sites, now lets more than 60 brands from its shopping-ads unit work with more than 21,000 creators who post on YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, X and Discord. Brands pay only when those creators' posts lead to a purchase, which matters to marketers who want creator campaigns judged on revenue rather than reach. The catch is what the announcement leaves out: how much creators earn per sale, how sales are counted, and which countries are covered.
What Taboola announced
The announcement, datelined New York and issued at 9:00 a.m. Eastern Time through GlobeNewswire, runs to a handful of paragraphs. "Taboola today announced that more than 60 leading brands can now tap into more than 21,000 new content creators via Connexity's platform," according to the company. The brands come from Connexity's roster in personal computing, sportswear, wellness and home products. The creators belong to what Taboola calls "Howl's invite-only social commerce platform." Social commerce is shopping that begins, and often ends, inside social apps.
For creators, the change means more brands to pick from. Creators in the Howl program will have access to more brands across X, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Discord "and more," according to Taboola, "giving them more campaigns to choose from and more ways to earn."
Howl's catalogue has been narrow. The network is "rooted in consumer electronics, gaming, collectibles and activewear," according to Taboola, and "has driven more than $1.5 billion in gross sales." No time period is attached to that figure, and the announcement does not say how a sale is credited to Howl. The new brands, the company says, strengthen those categories "while also expanding Howl into similar categories of interest among consumers."
Why does the word "new" matter in "21,000 new content creators"? Read in context, the creators appear to be new to Connexity's brands rather than newly recruited; the announcement does not say how many joined Howl since August. The headline uses a looser phrase, "thousands of new content creators," which is consistent with the body but less precise.
There is a second gap between headline and body. The headline names Vans, Champion, Timberland and Vitamix. The subheadline describes "top names in electronics, footwear, wellness and more." The body lists categories only - personal computing, sportswear, wellness and home products - and names no brand at all. The other 56-plus brands are not identified anywhere in the announcement.
How the offering works, according to Taboola
The announcement describes the arrangement in four parts. According to Taboola:
- Brands. More than 60 brands already working with Connexity can now reach Howl's creators. Connexity's portfolio spans personal computing, sportswear, wellness and home products.
- Creators. More than 21,000 creators in Howl's invite-only program choose which campaigns to take on, posting across X, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram and Discord, among other platforms. Taboola describes their audiences as "enthusiast audiences that are passionate about gaming, tech, fitness, fashion and more, where trusted recommendations drive purchases."
- Payment. The program promises "performance-focused outcomes that ensure brands only pay for sales performance, not social impressions." In other words, a brand that gets views but no purchases pays nothing.
- Measurement. Brands get "measurement with clear tracking and reporting, showing which creators and content drive sales, so brands can invest in what works," according to the company.
The announcement uses neither the word "commission" nor the industry's usual label for this model. Yet paying only when a purchase happens is how affiliate marketing works: a creator shares a tracked link or code, and the seller pays a cut when someone buys through it. That sets the Howl model apart from the flat-fee brand deal, where a brand pays a creator for a set of posts whatever they sell.
The offering in figures
How Taboola came to own Howl
The announcement treats the Howl deal as settled history: "Taboola acquired the Howl creator platform in August 2026." It gives no price.
Adweek reported on September 14, 2026 that Howl had sold the creator division of its business to Connexity in August. According to Adweek, Howl had been a commerce platform connecting content creators to retailers to power affiliate businesses. Both companies confirmed the sale but declined to share financial details, and a source told Adweek the sum was too small to require disclosure in Taboola's quarterly earnings. The rest of Howl, Adweek reported, would return its focus to publishers. Taboola, then, bought the creator side, not the whole company.
Howl's founder, Li Haslett Chen, told Adweek that Connexity would continue to invest in Howl and grow it as a creator commerce platform in tech, gaming and collectibles.
Connexity itself is older Taboola territory. Taboola paid about $800 million for Connexity in 2021, buying an ecommerce advertising business that places retailers' products in front of shoppers. Robert Michaelian, quoted in the announcement as "President of Connexity, a Taboola company," speaks for the Howl program in the announcement.
What the three quotes say
Three people are quoted, and each speaks to a different audience.
Adam Singolda, Taboola's chief executive, spoke to investors. "Ecommerce continues to be one of the fastest growing verticals for Taboola," Singolda said. "We're continuing to connect leading brands with influential creators - those with passionate audiences that trust the recommendations they receive." No figure accompanies the claim about growth. Taboola does not break out ecommerce revenue in the announcement, and the growth rate of the vertical is not stated.
Michaelian spoke to creators. "Our focus has been giving creators more ways to grow their incomes and brands more access to unique creator influence," he said. "Adding more than 60 brands is the first big step to grow the Howl platform. We believe creators deserve to be paid for performance, and I can't wait to see what our community does with these new opportunities." The phrase "first big step" signals more brands to come, though no timetable or target is given.
The third voice is a creator's. Jeff Springer, who posts as @jspring86 and is described in the announcement as a "leading tech content creator," credited Howl with his income. "Howl gets creators. It's helped me turn honest tech recommendations into a real business, and now, with access to over 60 more brands, there's even more opportunity for creators across every niche," Springer said. The announcement does not say how much Springer earns through Howl or how many of the 21,000 creators earn a living from the program.
Why Taboola is pushing into commerce
Taboola built its business on native advertising: paid links styled to sit among a publisher's own stories. That business has been under pressure all year.
When Taboola reported second-quarter results on August 5, revenue grew 2.4% to $476.8 million. But Google's back button policy forced the end of Explore More, a Taboola product that had been expected to add more than $20 million of gross profit after publisher payments in the second half of 2026. Three months later the picture had worsened. In third-quarter results on November 4, revenue fell 6.9% to $462.3 million, Taboola cut its full-year outlook, and the company disclosed that it and Yahoo had each sent notices of mediation under their 2022 services agreement. In the same results, Singolda named social commerce alongside Taboola News as a growth area the company is investing in. He gave no revenue figure for it.
The Howl announcement fits a wider set of moves in 2026 that take Taboola beyond its widget. In August, Taboola's Realize platform took over programmatic display ads on NBCNews.com and TODAY.com, its first display deal with an existing native partner. On September 18 it made an offer for Dianomi, a UK ad network specialising in finance, and completed that deal on November 16 for 64 pence a share in cash, about £19 million, with up to about £27 million in total. Finance through Dianomi, shopping through Connexity and Howl: both are categories where advertisers measure results in sales or sign-ups rather than in clicks on news pages.
Creator commerce and the counting problem
Paying only for sales solves one problem in the creator economy and sharpens another. It removes the argument over what a "view" is worth. That argument is live: in November, the Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB), the US ad industry's trade body, found that 66% of 53 industry leaders it surveyed wanted a common definition of a creator "view", and it plans measurement work for 2027.
But when a brand pays per sale, the dispute moves to attribution. Which creator gets credit when a shopper watches three videos before buying? How long after a click does a purchase still count? Taboola promises "clear tracking and reporting" without describing how sales are tracked, how long a link stays valid or how competing claims are settled.
Trust is the other variable. Taboola's pitch rests on audiences "where trusted recommendations drive purchases." Research summarised by PPC Land points both ways. In Australia, six in ten shoppers said they often or sometimes find brands through creators, rising to 84% to 85% among those aged 18 to 39, yet 44% said they were less likely to act on a post disclosed as paid. In the UK, IAB UK launched a creator qualification built with the advertising regulator and cited research from that regulator finding that only around 57% of influencer ads met disclosure requirements. The Howl announcement does not mention disclosure at all.
Howl also enters a crowded field. Platforms and retailers run their own programs and set their own rules. TikTok Shop, for example, introduced in June a daily creator score that gates product reach, commission tiers and badges for its affiliate creators. Walmart marked four years of its own creator program on November 16, taking creators to Bentonville, though it disclosed no creator, sales or commission figures. Against those, Howl's selling point is focus: a smaller, invite-only network built around enthusiasts in gaming, tech and collectibles, now with a broader set of brands to offer.
What the announcement does not say
The announcement gives no commission rates and does not say how each sale is split between brand, Howl and creator. It names four brands only in its headline, gives no countries or markets, attaches no period or definition to the $1.5 billion in gross sales, and does not describe how sales are tracked or credited. It discloses no price for Howl and no revenue figure for Taboola's ecommerce business.
