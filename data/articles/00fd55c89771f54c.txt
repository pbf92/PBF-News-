TITLE: Eating for the aging brain: A review puts the gut microbiota at the center of the diet–cognition link
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Healthy diets are among the most consistently supported, modifiable factors for protecting the aging brain — and the gut microbiota may be the missing link that explains how food reaches it. That is the central message of a review published in The Innovation Nutrition, which maps evidence across dietary patterns, individual foods and nutrients, and the gut microbiota.
Dementia is a growing global health challenge with limited pharmacological treatments, which places prevention high on the research agenda. Evidence from prospective cohorts and randomized dietary trials suggests that what people eat matters for cognitive health, but findings on specific foods and nutrients have been inconsistent, leaving the practical question — what exactly should be eaten — unresolved.
A team led by Hui Chen and Changzheng Yuan of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, with collaborators in Spain, the United States, Sweden, and Denmark, mapped the epidemiological evidence on dietary factors and cognitive aging. Overall dietary patterns show the strongest and most consistent associations: the Mediterranean diet and the MIND diet (Mediterranean–DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay) have repeatedly been linked to better cognitive outcomes.
At the level of foods and nutrients, the picture is more differentiated. The review organizes each item by the strength and direction of evidence: vegetables and fruits appear beneficial; ultra-processed foods and trans fat harmful; sodium shows a harmful signal; saturated fat appears neutral or weak; and alcoholic drinks follow a J-shaped pattern. Among nutrients, vitamin B12, n-3 fatty acids, and carotenoids have shown potential benefits for cognitive health.
The review's unifying thread is the gut microbiota, which is largely shaped by diet. Drawing on evidence from human studies and animal experiments, the authors propose that gut microbiota may act in three distinct roles: as a mediator that carries part of the diet's effect to the brain, as a moderator that changes the size of that effect between individuals, and as an independent health indicator of cognitive aging. Candidate mechanisms run along the gut–brain axis through metabolic, immune, and neuroinflammatory pathways; the review distinguishes direct pathways from indirect ones that travel through the gut.
The authors are careful about the limits of the evidence. Most human data are observational and cannot establish causation; results across studies remain inconsistent; and exposure assessment, study design, and population heterogeneity complicate comparison. The review closes with a roadmap: large-scale, multi-omics studies across diverse populations to clarify causal pathways, as a step toward precision nutrition strategies for preventing cognitive decline and dementia.
