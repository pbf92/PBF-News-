TITLE: Big Tech's tax payments drop to 12.3% of profit, Fair Tax report finds
OUTLET: PPC Land

Six of the world's biggest technology companies paid just 12.3% of their profits in tax in 2025, the smallest share in ten years, according to a report published in November 2026 by the Fair Tax Foundation, a UK campaign group. The six are Alphabet (Google's owner), Amazon, Apple, Meta (Facebook's owner), Microsoft and Netflix. The group calls them the "Silicon Six".
For the first time, Meta comes out worst in the group's ranking, pushing Amazon into second place. And over the ten years from 2016 to 2025, the report estimates that the six paid $316.7bn less tax than they would have if their profits had been taxed at the official rates of the countries where their customers live.
That figure is an estimate, not a tax bill. The foundation says so itself: "Our figures are very much estimates. They indicate the scale of the issue rather than any company's tax liability." It also accepts that much of the gap comes from tax breaks that are perfectly legal. But it argues that a gap this large, lasting this long, raises questions the companies and governments need to answer.
In Short
A UK campaign group says six US tech giants paid 12.3% of their profits in tax in 2025, the lowest share in ten years, and about $316.7bn less over the decade than the official tax rates where their customers live would suggest. Meta now ranks worst of the six, ahead of Amazon, and the report says Europe missed out on about $110bn and Britain on some £4bn a year. These are the group's own estimates, not tax bills, but they arrive as Europe debates new taxes on tech firms and Democrats prepare to take over Congress in January 2027.
Who wrote the report
The Fair Tax Foundation is a not-for-profit based in Manchester. It started in 2014 and runs the Fair Tax Mark, a label for companies it judges to pay tax responsibly. According to the report, 275 businesses hold the label. The report is written by Paul Monaghan and runs to 30 pages. It gives November 2026 as its date but no specific day.
This is the group's fourth look at the same six companies. Earlier versions came out in 2019, 2021 and 2025. The foundation says it usually avoids singling out companies - "We largely leave the 'naming and shaming' to others" - but makes an exception for these six because of their size and political power.
The key idea: tax actually paid
Company accounts show two different tax figures. One is the tax charge in the profit statement. It includes tax a company expects to pay in future years. The other is the cash tax - the money actually sent to tax authorities during the year.
The report uses cash tax. Its reasoning is simple: over ten years, tax that is only delayed should eventually be paid, so a gap that never closes is a real gap. In the report's words, "genuine timing differences should largely wash out; persistent shortfalls should not."
The report compares that cash tax with what the companies would have paid at each country's headline rate - the official corporate tax rate set by law, before any breaks or deductions.
How the estimate is worked out
According to the report, the calculation has five main steps.
- Start with the companies' own numbers. The figures come from the annual reports each company files with the US Securities and Exchange Commission. These show profit, tax charged and tax paid in cash. New US rules this year also make companies show tax paid by country for the first time, for any country that accounts for 5% or more of the total.
- Split the profit by where customers are. Each company's worldwide profit is divided between countries in line with where it makes its sales. If a company makes 10% of its sales in Germany, 10% of its profit is treated as German. This is a change from earlier reports.
- Apply each country's official tax rate. The report uses rates published by the OECD, the Paris-based club of rich countries. On average, the rate where the six companies' customers live came to 26.2% over the decade. The US rate, including state taxes, averaged 28.4%.
- Compare with tax actually paid. The difference between the two is the "tax gap". Two large one-off payments are left out because they relate to profits from before 2016: Apple's $15.8bn payment to Ireland after an EU ruling, and $50.0bn of a one-time US tax on money the companies had built up abroad.
- Estimate Europe and Britain separately. For Europe, the report estimates tax paid using each company's own regional figures, which do not always match Europe's borders. Apple's "Europe" includes India, the Middle East and Africa, while Amazon only reports Germany and the UK. For Britain, the group went through the accounts the companies' main UK subsidiaries file at Companies House.
The report lists choices it made that keep the estimate low, and others that may make it too high. Official rates ignore legal breaks such as research credits. Some British sales figures are estimates that "may prove high." And some extra tax owed under the new global minimum tax for 2024 and 2025 will only be paid in 2026 or later.
One more warning: the method changed this year. Last year's report put the gap for 2015 to 2024 at $277.8bn. Using last year's method, the 2016 to 2025 gap would have been even larger, between $339.3bn and $387.6bn. The report itself says the headline figures from different years "are not directly comparable."
Ten years in one table
Over the decade, the six companies took in $13.1tn in sales and made $3.3tn in profit, according to the report. They paid $540.4bn of tax in cash. At the official rates where their customers live, the report puts the bill at $857.1bn. The difference is the $316.7bn gap.

The report also puts the companies' size in context. Together they were worth $16.7tn on the stock market at the end of October 2026, more than one and a half times all the companies in the UK's FTSE 100 and Europe's EURO STOXX 50 indexes combined. Their yearly sales are bigger than the economy of every country except eleven. And they spent $133.2mn lobbying politicians in Washington and Brussels in their latest reported year.

2025: bigger profits, a smaller share in tax
2025 was a bumper year. Their combined profit rose 29.4% to $680bn. Five of them made the highest profit margin - profit as a share of sales - of the whole decade: Microsoft 50.0%, Alphabet 39.4%, Apple 33.3%, Netflix 28.2% and Amazon 13.6%.
Yet the share of profit they paid in tax fell to 12.3%. Amazon paid 8.5% and Meta 8.8%. Alphabet and Microsoft both paid under 15%. The report calls these "tax-haven-like rates".
Why the drop? Much of it, the report says, comes from a new US rule on spending. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, passed in 2025, lets companies deduct the full cost of new equipment, such as servers for data centres, in the year they buy it, instead of spreading it over many years. That shrinks taxable profit now. And the companies are spending heavily on artificial intelligence. Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta, the four hyperscalers in the group, are expected to spend some $745bn on investments such as data centres in 2026 alone, according to figures the report takes from the Open Markets Institute and AFR.
PPC Land has tracked that spending. Alphabet spent $51.4bn on property and equipment in the third quarter of 2026, so much that its free cash flow turned negative. S&P turned its outlook on Meta negative as forecasts for Meta's 2027 spending passed $225bn.
The report expects the tax share to fall again in 2026. Amazon paid $4.0bn in tax in cash in the first half of 2026, 29% less than a year earlier, even though its profit rose. Meta's tax rate for the first nine months of 2026 was just 2%.
The headline tax rates in company results can look very different from the cash numbers. Meta is the clearest example. In late 2025 it took a one-off $15.93bn tax charge linked to the new US law - an accounting entry, not money paid. That pushed its reported rate for 2025 to 29.6%. Without it, the report says, the rate would have been 11.1%.

How each company ranks
The foundation ranks the six from worst to least bad. It looks at how much tax they pay, how much they disclose, and how many disputed tax claims they carry. Its 2026 order is Meta, Amazon, Alphabet, Netflix, Apple and Microsoft.

Meta: last place
Meta paid 12.1% of its profit in tax over 2023 to 2025, the lowest of the six, and 8.8% in 2025. It makes 63% of its sales outside the US but, over the last three years, recorded only 7% of its profit there. In Spain, the only EU country where it has published a public tax report so far, its profit margin was 4.4%. Across the whole company it was 42.8%.
Meta also carries a large pile of what accountants call uncertain tax positions: tax savings it has claimed but thinks tax authorities might reject. In the report's words, this is tax "that a company has not paid but considers it may yet have to." That amount grew fivefold in ten years to $16.45bn at the end of 2025, and reached $20.82bn by September 2026. PPC Land reported the same figure from Meta's quarterly filing. Meta says it expects "to continue to accrue unrecognized tax benefits for certain recurring tax positions" - in other words, the pile will keep growing.
The US tax authority, the IRS, is claiming $15.89bn of extra tax from Meta for 2017 to 2019 over how it moved the rights to its intellectual property to Ireland. Meta's claims for a US research tax credit also jumped from $0.7bn in 2023 to $3.9bn in 2025. Citing a New York Times investigation, the report says Meta treats its AI data centres as experimental "pilot models" that qualify. The congressman who created the credit in 1981 is quoted as saying this has "gone way, way beyond what anybody could have imagined." Meta's answer, as recorded in the report, is that it uses "the tax incentives Congress established decades ago to encourage this type of domestic investment."
Amazon: a close second
Over ten years, Amazon paid $46.7bn in tax on $4.1tn of sales - about 1.1 cents of tax per dollar of sales, the lowest of the six. Microsoft paid $140.0bn on less than half those sales, and Apple $169.9bn.
Amazon argues that comparison is unfair, because it is a retailer and retailers make thin profits. The report answers with Amazon's own numbers: its cloud computing arm, Amazon Web Services, made 57% of the company's operating profit in 2025 with less than a fifth of its sales. PPC Land reported AWS sales up 39.3% to $45.98bn in the third quarter of 2026.
In Britain, Amazon's main UK companies were charged £46m of corporation tax in 2025 on £16.5bn of sales, according to the report. Amazon's own annual report shows UK sales of about £33bn, so at least £16bn is recorded somewhere else - most likely in Luxembourg, the report says. Amazon says its total UK tax contribution was £6.5bn in 2025. But most of that, "more than £5bn", is tax it collects for the government, such as VAT and staff income tax, rather than tax on its own profit. In Europe, Amazon says it pays "hundreds of millions of euros every year."
