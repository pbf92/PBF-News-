TITLE: Polaris to pay $150,000 in Minnesota's first data privacy law case
OUTLET: PPC Land

Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison's office today announced a $150,000 settlement with Polaris Industries, Inc., the Medina-based powersports manufacturer, resolving allegations that its website refused consumers' requests to opt out of the sale of their data and of targeted advertising. According to the Attorney General's office, it is the first enforcement action under the Minnesota Consumer Data Privacy Act since the law took effect on July 31, 2025.
In Short
Minnesota's Attorney General settled with Polaris after finding that the company turned down privacy opt-out requests from people who did not answer a verification email, and that its website did not fully honor the Global Privacy Control browser setting. It is the first case brought under Minnesota's 2025 consumer privacy law, and it was run jointly with California's privacy regulator, which is collecting a separate fine of $910,986. Polaris must stop asking people to prove who they are before opting them out, check every website tracker each quarter for three years, and approve any new tracker before it goes live.
The settlement
The agreement takes the form of an Assurance of Discontinuance under Minnesota Statutes section 8.31, subdivision 2b, a mechanism that lets the Attorney General close an investigation with a court-enforceable set of commitments. Matthew Winings, Polaris's senior vice president, general counsel and secretary, signed it on November 2, 2026. Assistant Attorney General Caitlin Micko signed for the state on November 18, 2026, the same date as the petition asking Ramsey County District Court, Second Judicial District, to approve it.
The court file number is left blank in the document, and the one-page order at the end carries neither a date nor a judge's signature. The parties agreed that the Attorney General may file the Assurance "on an ex parte basis," and that the court "may issue the Order below without further proceedings, which shall constitute a final judgment."
Polaris will pay $150,000, defined in the Assurance as the "Settlement Sum," no later than 30 days after the court enters final judgment. A second $150,000 is a stayed civil penalty. It becomes payable only if the Attorney General applies to the court, Polaris has an opportunity to respond, and the court finds that Polaris "has materially violated the terms of this Assurance." The document adds that "An isolated, inadvertent mistake shall not be deemed a violation for the purposes of this paragraph."
The case was not Minnesota's alone. The Assurance describes a joint investigation with the Enforcement Division of the California Privacy Protection Agency, which now calls itself CalPrivacy, and states that Polaris "has executed a Stipulated Final Order" with that division "in parallel." According to the Minnesota Attorney General's office, Polaris will pay CalPrivacy an administrative fine of $910,986 to resolve claims under the California Consumer Privacy Act, and Minnesota and California are both members of the Consortium of Privacy Regulators. By a PPC Land calculation, the two payments total $1,060,986, before the stayed penalty.
What the investigation found
The Assurance sets out the Attorney General's allegations in 16 numbered paragraphs. They begin with the structure of the law. The Minnesota Consumer Data Privacy Act, codified at section 325M.10 and following, gives consumers the right to opt out of the processing of their personal data for targeted advertising, for its sale, or for profiling. Consumers can exercise it manually or automatically, the Assurance notes, for instance through "a browser set to transmit a Global Privacy Control signal." Global Privacy Control is a setting offered by some browsers and extensions that sends every site a standing instruction not to sell or share the user's data.
Minnesota's Attorney General explains why the sale opt-out carries weight. "Once sold, that data can be sold again, and again, to different entities interested in building profiles on an individual to sell products or services to them," the document states. An opt-out at the point of collection prevents "the dozens or even hundreds of subsequent transactions that might take place in the absence of an opt out."
The law also gave businesses a grace period. Until January 31, 2026, the Attorney General had to send a warning letter before bringing an enforcement action. Polaris received one on November 21, 2025, setting out the office's preliminary findings and asking the company to cure its deficiencies. By the end of December 2025, according to the Assurance, Polaris had told the office that it believed the problems were fixed.
They were not, according to the state. "Subsequent testing of Polaris's website uncovered continuing errors with Polaris's handling of Personal Data following Opt-Out Requests," the document states, including requests sent through universal opt-out mechanisms. The Attorney General then asked for limited information on the number of Minnesota consumers who visited the website, and for copies of Polaris's data privacy and protection assessments.
A single assessment, dated April 2026
Minnesota's law, at section 325M.18, requires a controller to conduct and document a data privacy and protection assessment before processing personal data for targeted advertising or sale, an exercise similar in purpose to a data protection impact assessment under European law. Polaris produced one such assessment covering sale and targeted advertising. It was dated April 2026, which, according to the Assurance, indicated "a lack of a formal written DPPAs prior to that date." The law had been in force for about eight months by then.
The verification email
The second finding concerns how Polaris handled opt-outs submitted by hand. According to the Assurance, "Polaris's practice was to deny Opt-Out Requests where consumers failed to respond to a Polaris email attempting to verify the requesting consumer's identity."
The Attorney General's office draws a line between rights. The law allows controllers to authenticate identity "for some rights requests," the document notes, but it "requires that controllers like Polaris comply with Opt-Out Requests upon receipt and without requiring additional steps," under section 325M.14, subdivision 4(h). Some requests may justify a check. Under the statute as the Assurance reads it, an instruction to stop selling data or using it for targeted advertising does not.
A signal that waited for the next page
The third finding is the most technical, and the one most directly relevant to anyone who manages tags on a commercial website. After Polaris made its initial changes, the office found that certain tracking technologies on the site were not properly configured to process opt-out preference signals, "resulting in Opt-Out Requests not being honored."
Polaris's tags did respond to the Global Privacy Control signal, but only from the next page onward. The Assurance spells out the gap: "because web browsers do not automatically refresh the page already in view when the Global Privacy Control signal is activated, consumer activity on the same webpage prior to the next page load continued to be processed in a manner that did not reflect the consumer's Opt-Out Request."
In practice, a visitor who switched on the signal while reading a product page continued to be tracked on that page until navigating elsewhere. The state treated that window as a failure to honor the opt-out.
The office also found that Polaris's privacy notice "failed to adequately disclose opt out information required by the MCDPA," under section 325M.16, subdivision 1(b).
How the opt-out process worked, according to the Assurance
The document describes two paths by which a Polaris website visitor could opt out, and where each one broke down.
- A consumer submitting a manual Opt-Out Request had to provide an email address.
- Polaris then sent an email to that address "attempting to confirm the requester's identity."
- If the consumer did not respond, Polaris sent a second email "indicating that the Opt-Out Request had been denied."
- A consumer using a browser that transmits Global Privacy Control could switch the signal on while a Polaris page was open.
- Polaris's tracking technologies honored the signal "for subsequent page views."
- Activity on the page already in view, up to the next page load, continued to be processed as if no opt-out had been made.
Polaris, according to the Assurance, "invested significant resources in identifying and resolving the technical issues described in this Section, including by engaging an external technical consultant and reconfiguring tracking technologies on its website." The document does not say when the reconfiguration was completed.
The provisions cited
The Attorney General alleges violations of four sets of provisions. The Assurance lists them as sections 325M.14, subdivision 1(a) and (f), and 325M.16, subdivision 1(b), "for failing to honor manual Opt-Out Requests"; section 325M.14, subdivision 3, "for failing to honor Opt-Out Requests submitted via a universal opt-out mechanism"; and section 325M.18(b)(1-5), "for failing to conduct and document a data privacy and protection assessment for its processing of Personal Data for sale and targeted advertising."
"Polaris denies the AG's allegations," paragraph 15 states. The agreement adds that it "shall not be considered an admission of a violation for any purpose," and that Polaris remains free to raise any defense in a private individual or class action. The document also records that Polaris "produced hundreds of pages of documents, answered questions, met with the AG and the CPPA numerous times to engage in candid conversations about its privacy practices, and took steps to address the issues raised."
What Polaris must now do
The injunctive terms in section III are permanent. They bind Polaris, its officers, employees and contractors, its parents, affiliates, subsidiaries and successors, and anyone acting in concert with the company who receives actual notice of the order. They cover what the Assurance calls "Digital Properties," defined as "all public-facing websites owned or operated by Polaris that are accessible by consumers and subject to the MCDPA, including without limitation, https://www.polaris.com/."
On receipt of an Opt-Out Request from a Minnesota consumer, Polaris shall not process that consumer's personal data for targeted advertising, shall not sell it, and shall not:
"Require authentication of the Minnesota consumer, provided, however, that Polaris may deny an Opt-Out Request if it has a good faith, reasonable, and documented belief that the request is fraudulent in accordance with Minnesota Statutes sections 325M.14, subd. 4(h). In such cases, Polaris must provide the consumer with a narrative explanation and summary of documentation regarding Polaris's rationale for denial."
The remaining obligations concern governance and tooling.
The audit clause names tracking pixels alongside cookies and web beacons, and asks whether each one is "properly configured to honor" the signal. The approval process does two things at once: it must confirm that a vendor contract exists where the law requires one, and that the new technology is configured to respect opt-outs before it is added. Several of the obligations, including the approval process and the consultant, apply "To the extent it has not already done so," which acknowledges that Polaris may have put them in place during the investigation.
An anti-circumvention clause bars Polaris from changing its corporate form, structure, ownership or management "as a method or means of attempting to avoid the requirements of this Assurance." Paragraph 36 prohibits the company from stating or implying "that the State of Minnesota or the AG have approved of, condone, or agree with any conduct or actions by Polaris" connected with the allegations.
