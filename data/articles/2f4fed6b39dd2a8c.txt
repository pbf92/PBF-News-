TITLE: Mail-order antibiotic kits pose safety, resistance risks, pharmacy expert warns
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Sometimes, inspiration can spark from interesting places.
For Wesley Kufel, clinical professor of pharmacy practice at the School of Pharmacy and Pharmaceutical Sciences, a call to action didn’t start in his lab, but from a pitch for an “urgent care at home” antimicrobial kit that caught his attention for reasons other than what the advertisers intended.
“This all started from watching the news one day, and I saw a commercial for ‘urgent care at home.’ A kit with seven different antimicrobials would be mailed directly to your house after a phone call and completion of a brief medical history form. Given the quantities included, you’d have a stockpile of these antimicrobials available,” he said. “I recognized the important safety concerns and decided it needed to be a bigger conversation.”
Kufel earned his Doctor of Pharmacy degree from the State University of New York (SUNY) Buffalo School of Pharmacy and Pharmaceutical Sciences, followed by a PGY1 residency at SUNY Upstate University Hospital and a PGY2 Infectious Diseases residency at the University of North Carolina Medical. He later earned a master’s degree in business administration from SUNY Oswego College of Business and Entrepreneurship. He has dedicated his career to studying infectious diseases and providing clinical services within that arena. He also maintains a clinical practice site as an infectious diseases pharmacist at SUNY Upstate University Hospital.
Kufel recently published a paper for Clinical Infectious Diseases, an official journal of the Infectious Diseases Society of America, titled “Antimicrobials in a Box: A Primer on the Risks of Direct-to-Consumer Antimicrobial Preparedness Kits.” Within, he reviews recent direct-to-consumer marketing of “emergency medical kits” containing prescription antimicrobials, a trend he views as concerning within the healthcare marketplace.
Available entirely online, with minimal screening, these kits are intended for treatment of a self-diagnosed infection. While described and marketed as preparedness tools, Kufel notes significant concerns for patient safety, antimicrobial stewardship, and public health.
“What’s concerning is the way these commercials market these kits as tools for preparedness, which reinforces misconceptions that antimicrobials are broadly useful for illnesses, such as the common cold or viral infections — which antibiotics should not be used for,” he said. “These narratives conflict with a lot of the stewardship efforts that have been put forth for educating the public and minimize or erode trust in traditional healthcare pathways.”
Without appropriate clinical evaluation, testing, or guidance, he says misuse is also likely, from side effects and drug interactions to incorrect or unnecessary treatment. Such access also runs the risk of fostering potential antimicrobial resistance.
“These commercials are essentially saying that you, as an individual, can safely manage your infections, doing self-diagnosis without medical training. And that's scary; that is going to have larger public health consequences,” he said. “From a safety perspective, there's enough for at least two, if not three or four, treatment regimens for a particular infection in these kits, and many of these antimicrobials are not without considerable potential side effects and risks.”
With limited or unclear instructions, these antimicrobials are potentially dangerous, or even ineffective. For example, Kufel references that the kit contains 25 “compounded” ivermectin 12 mg tablets, which is advertised as a “Nobel Prize-winning” antiparasitic. He notes that this may lead to inappropriate, unsupervised use in response to delusional parasitosis or COVID-19 — despite controlled trials demonstrating no clinical benefit for COVID-19 — and could lead to significant neurologic side effects, including confusion, gait imbalance, and seizures.
“A lot of these antimicrobials require diagnostic tests to support their use, and that's not happening in these cases," Kufel said. "For somebody to be self-treating themselves on episodic or syndromic incidents, I would urge them to seek out the supervision of a healthcare professional.”
To add to the potential danger, these kits are not covered by insurance and can only be purchased for cash, so there is minimal oversight. Furthermore, many healthcare professionals are likely unaware that these kits exist, or whether their patients have purchased and used the antimicrobials already.
“The language in this kit is also often at a healthcare professional level rather than a patient-friendly reading level,” Kufel said. “If they don't know what the indications listed are, how are they going to know how and when to take these antimicrobials? This also adds to the considerable safety risks associated with these unsupervised antimicrobial kits.”
He said he hopes to bring awareness to the topic and felt a responsibility in highlighting his concerns during a time of misinformation and medical distrust.
“Pharmacists may be the first point of contact for individuals who may have already purchased these kits, and they need to make sure that they are informing patients of the significant risks of taking antimicrobials without clinical oversight from a healthcare professional,” Kufel said. “They need to have communication that is counterbalancing the commercial marketing narratives that can be very persuasive.”
