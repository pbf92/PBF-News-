TITLE: Australia urged to remove religion from legal definition of terrorism
OUTLET: Premier Christian News

Australia's national security watchdog has recommended removing religion from the country's legal definition of terrorism, saying it risks reinforcing harmful stereotypes and discrimination against religious minorities.
The Independent National Security Legislation Monitor has published a review of counter-terrorism laws introduced following the September 11 attacks in the United States.
Its head, Jake Blight, says linking terrorism to religious causes gives those responsible “false credibility” and can reinforce the idea that they are acting in the name of their faith.
“Having a law that requires proof that terrorists were advancing a ‘religious’ cause gives these terrorists false credibility and reinforces their narrative that they are doing holy work,” the report said. “The same individuals will still be able to be prosecuted, but should be described as violent ideologues.
“Reference to religion in the definition of terrorism has also, in practice, contributed to discrimination and violence against religious minorities, especially Muslim Australians. This has a negative impact on social cohesion.”
The current law requires prosecutors to show that an act of terrorism was intended to cause serious harm to people or property in pursuit of a political, religious or ideological cause.
The proposals have been welcomed by eleven Muslim community and advocacy organisations, which have called on the Australian government to change the law.
However, Australia's main Jewish representative body has questioned the recommendation.
The Executive Council of Australian Jewry said removing religion as a motivating factor made “little sense when so many notorious terrorist acts have featured motifs, words and images that unmistakably refer to religious doctrines and beliefs”.
The report also recommends changing the penalties for making credible terrorist threats.
Under current legislation, those convicted can face life imprisonment, with a minimum sentence of six years.
The watchdog says this is “disproportionate, unnecessarily complex and out of step with international standards”, and recommends a maximum sentence of five years instead.
It also wants clearer distinctions between terrorist acts and threats, and greater protection for legitimate protest, dissent and industrial action.
