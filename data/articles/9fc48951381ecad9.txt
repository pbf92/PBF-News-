TITLE: Daily iron supplements reduce iron deficiency anemia in children and adolescents: Study
OUTLET: Medical Dialogues

Daily iron supplements reduce iron deficiency anemia in children and adolescents: Study
Giving children and adolescents a daily iron tablet significantly reduces the risk of iron deficiency anaemia with little to no effect on several common illnesses, according to a Cochrane review of 114 studies involving more than 50,000 participants.
Iron deficiency occurs when the body lacks enough iron for its needs. These include making haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to the rest of the body. Left untreated, it leads to iron deficiency anaemia.
Iron supplementation is the standard of care for the prevention and treatment of anaemia in many parts of the world particularly for adolescents and women. Previous evidence has pointed to the benefits of iron supplementation for improving iron deficiency anaemia in children. However, current World Health Organization guidelines, last updated in 2016, emphasize the gap in evidence on the long-term effects.
Led by researchers from Cornell Joan Klein Jacobs Center for Precision Nutrition and Health at Cornell University, the review included 114 studies with over 50,000 children and adolescents, looking at the effects of daily oral iron, alone or with folic acid or when other vitamins and minerals were added. Many of the studies were conducted in Asia and focused on children under five years of age.
Daily iron reduces the risk of iron deficiency anaemia
Findings from this review demonstrate that iron alone probably reduces iron deficiency anaemia compared with no intervention or placebo. Iron with folic acid probably reduces it too, although this finding rests on a single study, and iron with other vitamins and minerals also may reduce it. For anaemia and iron deficiency more broadly, there is some evidence that iron may be useful for reducing both.
Iron alone had no effect on how tall a child is or how much they weigh, compared with other children of the same age and sex. It also had no effect on several common illnesses, such as cough, fever or respiratory illness, and iron with folic acid makes little to no difference to diarrhoea or respiratory illness. Findings for other combinations ranged from moderate to very low certainty.
“Iron deficiency remains one of the most common nutritional problems affecting children worldwide,” says first author Dr Kripa Rajagopalan. “Our review shows that daily iron supplements do what they are meant to do, which is reduce iron deficiency anaemia, without any measurable impact on the common illnesses parents and health workers worry about.”
Side effects are mostly mild, but evidence is uncertain
The evidence on side effects was unclear for some outcomes. Iron may increase stool discolouration. Iron alone may make little to no difference to vomiting or diarrhoea, and the effect on constipation is very uncertain. Iron combined with other vitamins and minerals probably increases vomiting compared with the same supplement without iron.
Senior author Dr Saurabh Mehta explains: “While the evidence base supports the use of daily iron supplements where needed, some adverse effects do occur. Stool discolouration and, in some formulations, vomiting were more common with iron, and the certainty of evidence for several outcomes, including anaemia and iron deficiency themselves, was low or very low.”
Anaemia remains a major global burden for young children
Anaemia affects an estimated 874 million children and women worldwide; the greatest burden is seen among young children, with 40% of children younger than five years affected in 2019. Approximately half of these cases are due to iron deficiency. Reducing anaemia is a key goal of the 2030 Global Strategy for Women’s, Children’s and Adolescents’ Health.
“These findings give decision-makers a stronger evidence base for guidance on iron supplementation in children and adolescents,” says co-author Dr Julia L. Finkelstein. “The data supports continued use of iron supplementation where iron deficiency is a concern.”
The authors stress that the findings support iron supplementation but should not be read as meaning every child or adolescent needs daily iron supplements. They should be interpreted in light of individual nutritional needs, the prevalence of iron deficiency and anaemia, and the formulation of the supplement.
