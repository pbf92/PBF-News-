TITLE: Agent Orange Exposure Linked to Increased Parkinson’s Risk Among Vietnam Veterans
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A large-scale analysis of electronic health records spanning millions of Vietnam-era military veterans has identified a significant statistical association between exposure to the chemical defoliant Agent Orange and subsequent diagnoses of Parkinson’s disease and Lewy body dementia. Led by researchers at Oregon Health & Science University and the VA Portland Health Care System, the peer-reviewed investigation analyzed long-term health outcomes among 4.9 million service members who served during the Vietnam War era. Across the full study cohort, military veterans who had documented exposure to Agent Orange demonstrated an overall 57% greater risk of developing Parkinson’s disease and related neurodegenerative conditions compared to their unexposed peers.
The study, published in JAMA Neurology, was designed to investigate conditions that manifested decades following military service, focusing specifically on diagnostic records collected between 1999 and 2022. Clinicians and veterans had long presumed a correlation between neurodegenerative conditions including Parkinson’s and exposure to the chemical defoliant used during the Vietnam War, and the new study definitively draws the link using a vast electronic database of health records across decades. To isolate the specific association between chemical exposure levels and subsequent disease incidence, the research team adjusted their statistical models for potential confounding health factors, including documented frailty and history of traumatic brain injury.
When examining specific diagnosis categories and exposure categories, the researchers observed varying degrees of elevated risk across the veteran cohort. Veterans categorized with possible or presumed exposure to Agent Orange were 44% more likely to be diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease and 66% more likely to receive a diagnosis of Lewy body dementia relative to service members without chemical exposure. The statistical analysis further revealed that risk scaled notably with the intensity of physical contact: veterans who experienced heavier, direct exposure through the handling and spraying of the chemical defoliant faced a 99% higher likelihood of developing Parkinson’s disease.
Heightened neurological risks were particularly concentrated among service personnel whose duties involved direct dispersion or handling of the defoliant, specifically members of the Army Chemical Corps and personnel assigned to Operation Air Force Ranch Hand.
Co-senior author Gregory Scott, an associate professor of pathology in the OHSU School of Medicine and pathologist at the VA Portland, noted that the investigation represents the first veteran study to directly connect increased Parkinson’s risk with heavier, documented exposure levels to Agent Orange. Co-author Lee Neilson, who initiated the project during his tenure at OHSU before moving to the University of Iowa, observed that the findings offer formal empirical validation to veterans who have reported neurological symptoms over multiple decades.
Shereef Elnahal, president of OHSU and former undersecretary of health in the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, highlighted that the study reinforces the rationale behind the 2022 PACT Act. That federal legislation expanded healthcare access for veterans exposed to toxic chemicals, eliminating the individual burden of proof previously required to secure medical coverage.
Furthermore, Dr. Elnahal stated that the research offers critical insight for former service members diagnosed with Parkinson’s decades after exposure in Southeast Asia, underscoring that personnel who faced hazardous duties deserve access to the truth regardless of the time elapsed.
Additional contributors to the collaborative investigation included Dr. Miranda Lim, who holds an appointment with the Oregon Institute of Occupational Health Sciences at OHSU, and Dr. Jonathan Elliott of OHSU and the VA Portland Health Care System.
