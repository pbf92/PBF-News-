TITLE: Being Normal at Thanksgiving
OUTLET: Christianity Today

The Syllabus is a column that features student opinions on timely national and international topics. We aim to highlight how evangelical students in the US are thinking about important issues and how the Christian faith informs their worldview. Students should use this link to submit a response for December’s prompt: “What are your family’s Christmas traditions?” Responses are due by December 16.
For this column, students were asked, “What topic do you regularly talk about with your friends but would not bring up at your family’s house during Thanksgiving? How do you divert attention to a new topic when/if Thanksgiving conversations get tense?” Here is what they said:
Please, Don’t Make It Weird for Pastors (Young or Old)
There are two things that people agree should be avoided during Thanksgiving talks: religion and politics. This is easy for many but not for me. I’m a youth pastor and a seminary student, so if anyone asks me about school or work, the conversation will turn to God.
My family members have had two reactions to these conversations: They either avoid God-talk like the plague, or they won’t stop talking about it.
On the one hand, people become tense, more serious, or otherwise feel like they should put on a face, which can make the interactions awkward. Others, meanwhile, complain about their own church or feel like they should tell me what their favorite Facebook theologian thinks about a particular topic.
If someone seems scared to talk about my job, I usually bring up a funny story. If someone goes on a theology rant that is borderline heretical, I simply smile and ask them about themselves. People love to talk about themselves.
If you have pastors in the family, this Thanksgiving, treat them like a human. Talk about sports, your work, or simply goof off. Ministers are humans too, and they want to be treated like one.
Isaiah Bulman, graduate student at Baylor University’s Truett Seminary, divinity
Not the Time for Politics
Pass the turkey and the stuffing, but please skip your view on politics.
My family loves and respects each other. But because there are family members who have very different political views from each other, we tend to steer clear of those conversations at Thanksgiving.
Meanwhile, my college friends and I are all around the same age and tend to share the same views. When we talk about politics, we’re laid back and open-minded. If someone disagrees with the group, it’s not a big deal.
But with family, especially at Thanksgiving, sharing your views can feel like picking a side against another family member. Because I’m a college student and only get to visit my family a couple times a year, I’d rather keep the conversations peaceful and enjoy the time we have together.
Ryan Hendrick, senior at Baylor University, entrepreneurship
Perhaps Skip the Theological Debates Too
As a young seminarian and an aspiring pastor, I spend much of the week talking about what I believe, how I interpret Scripture, and how I understand God. Whether I’m talking to someone about the origins of evil, the sovereignty of God, or the challenges of biblical interpretation, the conversations often invite healthy disagreement.
However, I’ve learned—through experience—that not every conversation that belongs in a seminary classroom belongs at the Thanksgiving table. During that learning process, I’ve
wrestled with one question: At what point does our desire to be right become greater than our desire to love?
I believe Christians should be willing to engage in difficult conversations, even when disagreements arise. But I also believe there’s a difference between knowing how to respond and the wisdom to discern whether we should. Scripture reminds us to be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger (James 1:19). Sometimes, the most faithful response isn’t another theological argument but an opportunity to listen.
When conversations become tense, the best thing we can do is redirect the discussion toward something we can appreciate together, whether that’s a shared memory, a moment of gratitude, or simply asking someone about their life. For me, I’ve learned Thanksgiving shouldn’t become an opportunity to demonstrate how much theology we know while forgetting how deeply we’re called to love one another.
Kendall Petteway, graduate student at Baylor University’s Truett Seminary, divinity
The Beauty of Differences
Families gather around the TV every year to watch A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving. Although the feast in the special episode consisted of jellybeans and popcorn, it did depict something more relatable: People coming together, despite their differences, to share a meal and express gratitude.
Differences are prevalent in my family. Both my parents have siblings who live in completely different parts of the United States, which creates some cultural dissimilarities. Because of their differences, family members bring a variety of perspectives and ideas to the table, which often leads to engaging conversations. While we may not always see eye to eye, I have never seen a Thanksgiving gathering turn hostile.
The Book of Romans speaks to this very idea: We are not called to be exactly like one another, but we are called to build up the body of Christ.
To me, this is the beauty of Thanksgiving. I get to sit around a table with people who think differently, hold different beliefs, and bring different experiences. But by choosing to focus on the positive aspects of our differences, I get to see the beauty of being uniquely created.
Owen Witschonke, junior at Baylor University, supply chain management and finance
