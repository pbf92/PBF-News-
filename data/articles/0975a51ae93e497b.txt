TITLE: Gen-Z socialists may not care for 'free markets' anymore
OUTLET: ABC News (Australia)

Are Australia's living standards being hamstrung by too much government intervention?
Do we need to slash taxes, cut middle-class welfare, and reduce public spending to boost productivity and improve our lives?
That's the topic of an interesting note by AMP's chief economist Shane Oliver, published this week.
Oliver said the popular tide was turning against "free markets" and he felt the need to defend them. He said in the last three decades the pendulum had swung in favour of more government intervention, and it's gone too far.
"There is now much talk of gen-Z socialism," he wrote.
"This is a long way from Marxist dialectical materialism, and as one commentator observed is arguably more about getting free stuff.
"But its core tenets appear to be little faith in trickle-down economics (where growth benefits even the poor), a focus on redistribution by taking from the richest, and scepticism of private business and capitalism.
"[The] benefits of free markets and freedom more generally have been lost in debate about how to divide up the pie," he argued.
And he lamented the decline in the number of politicians who were still willing to say there was "no free lunch".
Let's divide the pie and have a free lunch
Now, none of this is a personal criticism of Oliver. It's an engagement with the ideas in his note.
But his note was a great example of how certain ideas can be shared with journalists, many of whom would have no idea about the ideological baggage contained in the arguments in the note but who may take them at face value and include them in their reporting about the economy and government spending.
In his note, Oliver referred to "free markets" but never defined the term.
"Here's a defence of free markets and why they generate more material prosperity than government diktat," he wrote.
But the term "free markets" is heavily coded.
American economist Milton Friedman was the great populariser of "free markets" in the 1970s and 1980s.
He wielded the term like a weapon to attack governments, laws, regulations, regulators, rules, codes, academics and unions; anything that prevented markets from working "freely" (according to him).
He was a major figure in the Reagan Revolution of the 1980s and infamously argued that a business's only social responsibility was to increase its profits and maximise returns for its shareholders (an idea that had "disastrous consequences").
There is marketing and tactical genius in the term "free markets": my favoured policies support free markets, so if you object to my policies you must hate freedom.
A casual reader of Oliver's note may not know any of that history.
What does 'economic freedom' mean?
Oliver made another interesting argument in his note, with an accompanying chart.
"There is plenty of evidence that free market economies deliver higher living standards. The next chart shows a clear rising relationship between the degree of economic freedom and GDP per person across countries," he wrote.

Oliver didn't define what he meant by "economic freedom" either. It was another undefined term with a lot of ideological baggage.
But he was referencing the "Index of Economic Freedom", which is produced by the Heritage Foundation.
The Heritage Foundation is a major "free market" think tank in the United States which published the notorious Project 2025, Donald Trump's blueprint for the MAGA takeover of the US government, and Ronald Reagan's Mandate for Leadership in 1981, which kickstarted the Reagan era.
Its "Index of Economic Freedom" was inspired by debates within the Mont Pelerin Society in the 1980s and Milton Friedman's attempt to define and measure economic freedom in different countries. It was launched in the mid-1990s.
To compile its index, the foundation measures "12 freedoms" across four broad categories in 184 countries:
Each of those 12 freedoms are graded on a scale of 0 to 100, and a country's overall score is derived by averaging those 12 freedoms, with equal weight given to each.
In the 2026 edition of the index, the top five countries in the world for economic freedom were: Singapore, Switzerland, Ireland, Australia, and Taiwan (in that order).
I want to break free
But what is that index actually telling us?
As you may notice, the economic freedoms in the index appear to have a pro-business bias. Low taxes? No tariffs? Little government spending? More freedom.
But Singapore ranks 1st in the world on the index.
And Singapore famously has a home ownership rate above 90 per cent thanks to a large-scale program of government-built housing and financing, broad powers of land acquisition, extensive land use planning, and numerous anti-speculation policies, which have kept housing affordable for the vast majority of its people.
It has also famously been governed by the same political party since 1959.
When the Singaporean economist Sock-Yong Phang visited Australia in 2024, she said her country may be classified as the world's "freest" economy on the index because it had no trade tariffs and its capital markets were open.
"However, this description doesn't quite capture the reality of its land and housing sectors where there is extensive government intervention and regulation," she said.
So, can heavy-handed "government intervention" in a "free market" economy still produce a world-winning amount of "economic freedom"? Apparently so.
But the casual reader-journalist of Oliver's note may not be aware of the inner workings of those indexes and definitions and the contradictions they carry.
Cheaper housing, more freedom?
Which takes us to a final point.

Those are pretty big problems.
And for those reasons, he said, there was a role for governments in market economies to do lots of things.
He said governments needed to protect the environment, to provide a legal system to enforce property rights, and to provide infrastructure and essential public goods like clean water, good roads and universal education.
They also need to regulate monopolies, ensure fair competition and provide a safety net to guard against extreme poverty and inequality, and so on.
"So, it's not simply a matter of completely free markets versus government control and planning but getting the balance right," he wrote.
"[But] in Australia, the balance has likely swung too far in terms of government involvement in the last 20 years," he argued.
And he said "to help get productivity and living standards back on track" in Australia, we should remove some regulations from labour and product markets, reduce taxes and cut the level of public spending.
But that raises a question.
Australia is already 4th in the world for economic freedom, according to the Heritage Foundation.
To get to 1st position, should we follow Singapore's lead and embrace heavy-handed government intervention in the housing market, with numerous anti-speculation policies, to produce affordable housing for all Australians?
Don't threaten us with a good time. Affordable housing would give gen-Z socialists a freedom they've never experienced.
