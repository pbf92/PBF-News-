TITLE: High on Health: Are Your Naps Shortening Your Lifespan?
OUTLET: Liberty Nation News

Who doesn’t enjoy a little relaxing daytime shuteye now and then? A study in JAMA Network says, “[B]etween 20% and 60% of older adults take naps during the day.” Naps are great for alleviating fatigue and improving alertness, but research shows they might be unhealthy for some people. “[E]xcessive napping in late life has been linked to adverse health outcomes, including neurodegeneration, cardiovascular diseases, and even greater morbidity.”
The Study
In a study published by JAMA Network earlier this year, researchers from Harvard Medical School and other institutions analyzed data from 1,338 adults in the Rush Memory and Aging Project in northern Illinois. Participants were 56 and older, with an average age of 81; about three-quarters were women. The group wore wrist monitors for up to two weeks to track their activity, and researchers followed them for an average of about eight years, sometimes up to 19 years.
During the study, 926 (69.2%) died. “[T]hose who reported daily napping had a 44% increased mortality risk over 7 years compared with nonnappers,” the study explained. Furthermore, morning naps “were linked to an increased risk of death compared with early-afternoon naps.”
Morning Naps – Why Are They More Concerning?
The study explained that feeling sleepy in the early afternoon is often part of the body’s natural daily rhythm. But feeling the need to nap in the mornings may indicate a disruption in the body’s internal clock, poor nighttime sleep, or underlying health problems.
A 2025 Communications Medicine study found that morning naps have also been linked to Alzheimer’s disease. Researchers followed 936 adults between the ages of 56 and 99, and examined 320 deceased participants for physical signs of the disease. They discovered that people who took most of their naps between 9 a.m. and 11 a.m. had a higher risk of developing Alzheimer’s. Those who napped between 1 p.m. and 3 p.m. had lower levels of amyloid beta, the protein that accumulates in the brains of people with the disease.
After accounting for several factors such as age, medications, nighttime sleep, depression, and chronic illnesses, the study published in JAMA Network found that those who napped in the morning had a 30% higher risk of death than those who napped in the afternoon. Each additional daily nap was associated with a 7% higher risk of death, and each additional hour of daytime napping was associated with a 13% higher risk of death.
The Risks of Daytime Sleeping
A 2024 study archived in PubMed Central claimed that napping for an hour or longer is linked to cardiovascular disease and premature death. Researchers looked at 21 studies that involved 371,306 participants and discovered that the length of naps made a difference in health. Compared to those who didn’t snooze during the day, people who napped an hour or more had a 22% higher risk of premature death and a 37% higher risk of cardiovascular disease. The researchers, however, didn't find a significant increase in either of the risks with those who napped for less than an hour.
A 2022 Alzheimer’s Association study looked at 1,401 older adults for up to 14 years. While adults tend to nap longer and more often as they age, the research found that this pattern of daytime naps accelerated among people developing cognitive impairment. Adults without the issue increased daily naps by about 11 minutes per year, but among those diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment, the increase rose to nearly 25 minutes. Once an Alzheimer’s diagnosis was made, that number rose again to approximately 68 minutes per year.
Strokes are another concern, especially among those with high blood pressure. A 2024 CNS Neuroscience & Therapeutics study looked at 11,252 people with high blood pressure and no previous history of stroke. The average age was 63, and participants were followed for around nine years. During the study period, 1,698 people had their first stroke. Researchers found that, compared with people who napped for 1 to 30 minutes, those who took a daytime snooze for 31 to 60 minutes had a 27% higher risk of stroke; those who napped for more than an hour had a 37% higher risk of stroke. However, participants who didn’t nap at all had a higher stroke risk than those who took short naps.
Not All Bad News
Naps aren’t all harmful, and correlation does not always equal causation. Increased napping could also be a symptom of declining health and not its cause. Plus, a daytime snooze can have some health benefits. A 2023 review published in The Journals of Gerontology examined 35 studies and found that short to moderate naps were linked to better cognitive function in older adults compared to people who didn’t nap or took excessively long naps. A single afternoon nap might also improve working memory.
Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience published a review in 2022 that looked at 15 studies of adults aged 61 to 78. It discovered that daytime naps helped reduce fatigue and sleepiness, increased speed and accuracy in performing tasks, and improved the ability to learn new information and physical skills.

A 2023 study published in Sleep Health looked at the genetic information of 378,932 UK Biobank participants aged 40 to 69 and found that people who took regular naps had larger overall brain volumes. The brain naturally shrinks with age, and accelerated shrinkage has been linked to cognitive decline and neurological diseases. The researchers estimated the difference in brain volume was equivalent to 2.6 to 6.5 years of brain aging.
A 2019 Swiss study published in the medical journal Heart followed 3,462 adults without a previous history of cardiovascular disease for approximately 5.3 years. They discovered that people who napped once or twice a week had a 48% lower risk of strokes and heart attacks compared to those who didn’t nap.
To nap or not to nap, that is today’s question. According to research, naps can be beneficial, but the time of day and their length and frequency might be the key – and they can be easily overdone, potentially to the detriment of your health.
