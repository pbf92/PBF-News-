TITLE: Prior authorization limits help expand opioid addiction treatment where approvals were a frequent roadblock, study finds
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

State laws limiting prior authorization requirements for opioid use disorder medications were associated with increased buprenorphine use in Medicaid, but only in states where those requirements were frequent barriers to access, according to a new study led by University of Michigan researchers.
Published in JAMA Network Open, the research found that in those states (Arkansas, Illinois and Wisconsin), the laws were associated with roughly a 19% increase in the monthly rate of patients receiving buprenorphine and a 34% increase in the rate of patients starting it, compared with baseline levels. Patients also received their prescriptions nearly a day sooner, and prescription claim rejections because of prior authorization fell by about 7.5 percentage points.
In the other 10 jurisdictions that enacted laws, where those rejections were less common, the researchers found no statistically significant associations with any study outcome. Across all 13 jurisdictions combined, the laws were not associated with statistically significant changes in buprenorphine dispensing, treatment initiation or the time between prescribing and dispensing.
“While prior authorization requirements may promote appropriate care in some situations, our findings suggest that reducing these administrative barriers can help more people receive buprenorphine treatment in settings where prior authorization is common.” said Thuy Nguyen, the study’s first author and an associate professor of Health Management and Policy at the University of Michigan School of Public Health.
Removing one barrier among many
Medicaid is the largest financing source for opioid use disorder treatment in the United States, according to the study. Prior authorization, the requirement to secure an insurer’s approval before it covers a medication, can delay medication treatment, making it an important target for efforts to improve access.
The findings suggest that limiting prior authorization may help expand treatment access when those requirements commonly stand in patients’ way. Where they are uncommon, other barriers may need greater attention, including limited access to pharmacies that stock buprenorphine and stigma surrounding opioid use disorder and its treatment.
“Buprenorphine is a highly effective treatment for opioid use disorder and can be prescribed in routine office-based care,” said Nguyen, who is also a member of the University of Michigan Institute for Healthcare Policy and Innovation. She noted that expanding access to this lifesaving medication is critically important as potent opioids continue to drive overdose risk.
About the study
The researchers analyzed two national pharmacy databases from 2014 through 2020, including nearly 12.7 million dispensed buprenorphine prescriptions for more than 652,000 Medicaid patients ages 15 to 64. They compared changes in 13 jurisdictions that implemented laws limiting prior authorization from 2015 to 2019 with changes in 25 states that did not. The study focused on immediate-release buprenorphine and on laws implemented before the COVID-19 pandemic, and it did not measure clinical outcomes such as overdoses.
Additional authors include Kao-Ping Chua, the study’s senior author, Yang Amy Jiao, Pooja Lagisetty, and Amy S. Bohnert, all of the University of Michigan; and Rena M. Conti of Boston University.
