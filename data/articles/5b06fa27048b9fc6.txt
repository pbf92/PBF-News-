TITLE: Scoping Review Identifies Systemic Barriers Limiting Impact of African Health Science Research
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive scoping review evaluating the scientific reach of health science research across Africa has identified persistent structural inequities, funding shortages, and capacity constraints as major impediments that continue to stifle the global visibility and policy influence of the continent’s medical investigations. Despite accounting for roughly twelve percent of the global human population and bearing approximately thirty-one percent of the worldwide disease burden, African nations collectively contribute less than one percent to total global research output. Furthermore, national expenditures dedicated to research and development remain beneath one percent of gross domestic product across every African country. The investigation, published in Public Health Reviews, synthesizes findings from dozens of specialized bibliometric and scientometric evaluations to clarify why Africa’s health science literature frequently struggles to achieve proportionate international citation impact and translational influence, while simultaneously outlining the institutional mechanisms and regional developments that facilitate academic growth.
To conduct this continental assessment, the research team implemented the Joanna Briggs Institute guidance and Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework for scoping reviews, systematically filtering literature across multiple academic indices to establish a rigorous evidence base. Structured search protocols were deployed across primary databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Dimensions, the Cochrane Library, and the African Index Medicus, complemented by targeted queries within African Journals Online, the Directory of Open Access Journals, Google Scholar, and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global. From an initial identification of 11,076 records, which included 11,055 database retrievals and 21 articles discovered via citation tracking and manual searching, deduplication procedures removed 843 duplicates, leaving 10,233 citations for title and abstract screening. Following rigorous appraisal by two independent reviewers utilizing the web-based Covidence platform, 220 candidate studies advanced to full-text assessment, culminating in a final analytical corpus of 78 peer-reviewed studies published between 2010 and 2023 that focused exclusively on African countries.
A critical methodological finding of the review concerns the geographical framing and analytical scope adopted by existing scientometric evaluations. The authors noted that studies adopting a worldwide perspective or pooling African jurisdictions with non-African states routinely overlook or minimize African contributions, because African researchers and academic centers rarely register among the highest-volume producers in unadjusted global productivity rankings. In contrast, analyses that specifically contextualize research metrics against socioeconomic indicators—such as national gross domestic product, gross healthcare spending, out-of-pocket health expenditure, or total population size—demonstrate that several African countries achieve substantially higher comparative efficiency and impact. Within the 78 included papers, the majority examined Africa as an entire continental region, followed by specific evaluations concentrated on South Africa, the broader Sub-Saharan African territory, Tunisia, Morocco, Ethiopia, and Cameroon, whereas other individual African sovereign territories were examined in only isolated publications.
The synthesized evidence revealed substantial concentration in the bibliographic data sources used to quantify African scholarly performance, exposing notable blind spots in how regional knowledge is tracked. PubMed emerged as the predominant primary data source utilized across the evaluated bibliometric literature, followed closely by Web of Science, Scopus, and diverse combinations of these three major commercial indexes. However, indigenous regional indexing platforms, specifically African Journals Online and the World Health Organization’s African Index Medicus, were utilized by only a small fraction of studies. The review notes that this stark underutilization stems largely from formatting constraints, as these regional databases historically have not structured records to track citation link data or export bibliographic metadata necessary for high-throughput quantitative scientometric measurements, leaving a substantial volume of locally relevant peer-reviewed output excluded from international visibility calculations.
An analysis of researched health themes across the included bibliometric corpus showed that public health scholarship across the continent remains heavily aligned with historic infectious disease challenges, while progressively expanding to address chronic non-communicable conditions. Communicable illnesses—principally malaria, human immunodeficiency virus infection, and tuberculosis—represented the most pervasive subject matters analyzed in citation and output surveys. This distribution directly mirrors the acute epidemiological burden that infectious pathogens have imposed across African communities for decades. Simultaneously, multiple reviewed investigations tracked growing bibliometric footprints in non-communicable categories, including cardiovascular diseases, oncology, surgical subspecialties, and diabetes mellitus, which are projected to overtake communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional conditions as leading health challenges by 2030, alongside dedicated assessments of health professional education, academic medical infrastructure, and doctoral research training programs.
Across the 78 analyzed papers, the scoping review categorized the fundamental barriers suppressing African health science research productivity, visibility, and scientific impact into three interrelated structural domains: systemic research capacity deficits, financial and motivational limitations, and imbalances embedded within collaborative partnerships. In terms of human capital and technical capacity, numerous bibliometric studies highlighted a shortage of researchers formally trained in advanced scientific methodology and manuscript writing for peer-reviewed dissemination. These individual hurdles are compounded by pervasive institutional resource disparities at national levels, which leave many academic centers without the laboratory equipment, stable computing infrastructure, and high-speed internet connectivity necessary to sustain competitive research programs.
Financial stringency and institutional economics present another formidable barrier that directly suppresses scientific throughput and drives talent migration away from basic and translational health investigations. Severe public funding limitations frequently prevent academic institutions from providing dedicated research time, leaving university-affiliated medical faculty burdened by overwhelming clinical service responsibilities and excessive teaching schedules. In response to uncompetitive compensation frameworks and nonexistent institutional research incentives, numerous qualified scholars engage in private clinical practice or outside consulting to supplement their livelihoods, generating an internal public-to-private brain drain that drains time and attention away from scholarly experimentation and writing. Furthermore, escalating open-access publication models create steep economic barriers, as underfunded African researchers often cannot afford exorbitant article processing charges required by leading international journals unless comprehensive institutional fee waivers are granted.
The review also detailed extensive evidence illustrating how international collaborative frameworks with institutions in the Global North can unintentionally distort African research agendas and impede scholarly autonomy. External funding agencies and collaborating partners from high-income countries frequently dictate scientific priorities, aligning investigations with donor interests rather than acute local public health necessities.
Foremost among these catalytic drivers is the rapid evolution of information and communication technologies, which has expanded institutional internet bandwidth, broadened access to electronic scholarly repositories, and facilitated streamlined online manuscript submission and peer-review workflows.
Capacity-building initiatives and organized mentorship consortia were consistently identified as powerful institutional catalysts for sustaining high-impact scholarship across the continent. Collaborative programs specifically cited within the analyzed literature include the African Journal Partnership Program, the AuthorAID project, the Consortium for Advanced Research Training in Africa, and the Sub-Saharan African Network for TB and HIV Research Excellence.
Synthesizing the evidentiary landscape, the scoping review articulated three foundational, interconnected recommendations to reshape the continental research ecosystem: expanding endogenous research capacity through dedicated training in scientific writing and investigative methodology; dramatically scaling local research financing while advocating for direct international grant disbursements to African principal investigators; and mandating equitable governance frameworks across collaborative agreements to secure local agenda-setting authority and fair authorship representation. To complement these core interventions, the authors emphasized the necessity of revising academic promotion criteria within African universities to balance teaching commitments with protected research time, ensuring appropriate institutional recognition for high-quality peer-reviewed outputs regardless of whether they appear in global or domestic journals. Simultaneously, the study urged major international citation databases and local publishers to modernize bibliographic metadata records, eliminating data discrepancies that historically mischaracterize legitimate African journals as predatory or invisible.
Finally, the authors delineated critical methodological boundaries that must be considered when interpreting their scoping findings, notably highlighting search limits and language constraints encountered during screening. While the search strategy encompassed primary academic databases, relevant literature unindexed by commercial aggregators or archived exclusively in physical institutional repositories may have escaped detection. Most importantly, although initial electronic searches applied no language filters, logistical constraints restricted full-text screening exclusively to English-language reports. This procedural screening limitation introduced potential geographic selection bias by excluding relevant scientometric investigations published in French, Portuguese, or Arabic, thereby potentially underrepresenting the distinct publishing experiences of Francophone, Lusophone, and North African health scientists. Consequently, future bibliometric evaluations must deploy multilingual screening teams to assemble an entirely inclusive, methodologically sound appraisal of Africa’s evolving health science enterprise.
