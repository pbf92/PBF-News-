TITLE: Tech Stocks 20% Off Highs, Nvidia at 9x Earnings
OUTLET: shattered.io

Seeking Alpha published an analysis on November 26, 2026 titled “AI Bubble Bursts: Welcome To The Bear Market,” built around the Invesco QQQ Trust, Series I (NASDAQ:QQQ). The piece argues that technology stocks sit about 20% below their highs and that the AI bubble bear market has arrived. It also claims Nvidia trades at 9 times next year’s earnings, a multiple that would have sounded absurd a year ago.
The timing matters. On November 20, the Nasdaq Composite fell 1,261.47 points, or 5.28%, to close at 22,617.73. Baird market strategist Michael Antonelli described the mood this way: “The whole notion that AI can continue to spend unlimited amounts of money and support the stock market, I think the notion of that is ending as we speak, that’s what’s being priced right now.” Our earlier look at two investors who doubt Nvidia’s growth covered the first wave of that doubt.
Here is the catch. The “bubble burst” label is an opinion, and the bear market label is not settled either. Contemporary reporting described the Nasdaq-100 as approaching or set to enter bear-market territory, which is not the same as confirming it got there. This analysis separates what the numbers show from what the headline asserts, then looks at what comes next for the Nasdaq selloff, chip valuations and AI spending.
What the Seeking Alpha Thesis Actually Claims
The article dated November 26 makes four concrete claims and one big framing claim. The concrete ones are valuation figures. Technology stocks trade roughly 20% below their highs. Nvidia Corporation trades at 9 times next year’s earnings. Broadcom Inc. trades at 15 times this year’s earnings. Alphabet Inc. trades at 18 times next year’s earnings.
The framing claim is the headline itself. Calling this the moment an AI bubble bursts is a thesis, not an established fact. Nobody can confirm a bubble has popped while prices are still moving, and the historical record shows that analysts often disagree about the date a bubble ended until years later. Treat the headline as a position, and the multiples as data points to test.
The piece also leans on forward earnings growth. It cites projected 2027 earnings-per-share growth of 72% for Nvidia, 66% for Broadcom and 118% for Advanced Micro Devices, Inc. Those numbers sit at the center of the argument. If they hold, today’s multiples look cheap. If they miss, the multiples look like the last stop before a longer drop.
How Far the Nasdaq Has Fallen: The Confirmed Numbers
Start with what is verified. The Nasdaq Composite closed at 22,617.73 on November 20 after losing 1,261.47 points. A simple subtraction puts the prior close at 23,879.20, so the one-day drop of 5.28% checks out. A loss that size in one session is hard to dismiss as noise.
Grizzly Bulls Research added a second marker. It reported that the Nasdaq-100 finished November 20 at 25,029 after what it called a sharp selloff. That figure gives readers a base for judging the bear market claim, and it shows the large-cap tech index and the broader Composite moved in the same direction.
One caution on the table. The 20% figure comes from an opinion piece, so it is an approximation of drawdown, not an index-level measurement. The Composite and Nasdaq-100 figures are closer to hard data. Readers should weigh them differently.
Is the Nasdaq-100 Really in a Bear Market?
Market convention defines a bear market as a decline of 20% or more from a recent peak. That makes the question mechanical. Either an index closed 20% below its high or it did not. The available reporting does not settle it for the Nasdaq-100. Coverage this week described the index as close to a bear market, and one broker commentary framed the trade as having entered a far more dangerous phase.
The Wall Street Journal ran a Streetwise column asking whether the AI bubble had already burst, and noted that this year’s selloff has concentrated in AI-related stocks while money rotated into other sectors, with healthcare up 24% from late in the period it examined. A question mark in a headline is the right posture. The Seeking Alpha headline drops the question mark entirely.
Two details separate a bear market from a bad month. First, depth, which is the 20% threshold. Second, duration, since many sharp drops reverse within weeks. A November 20 plunge of 5.28% in the Composite counts as a violent day, but one day does not make a regime. The label becomes official only after a close below the threshold, and it fades if a rebound lifts the index back above it.
For readers tracking QQQ, the practical reading is simple. The fund is deep in a correction, flirting with the bear market line, and the headline writer has decided the line no longer matters. The honest summary is that the verdict on the AI bubble bear market is pending.
Nvidia at 9 Times Earnings: What the Multiple Implies
Nvidia sits at the center of every AI valuation debate, so the 9 times next year’s earnings figure deserves the most scrutiny. A multiple that low for a company with projected 2027 EPS growth of 72% implies the market doubts the forecast. Investors are paying very little for each dollar of expected profit because they do not trust the profit to arrive.
Divide the multiple by the growth rate and the gap stands out. Nine divided by 72 gives about 0.13, a ratio that most growth investors would call a bargain if the forecast were reliable. That is my arithmetic on the article’s figures, not a published metric, and it mixes a forward multiple with a forward growth rate. Still, it shows why bulls read the selloff as a buying chance. We covered the earlier swing in Nvidia’s slide after a 32% surge, which framed the same tension between momentum and value.
Broadcom and Alphabet on the Same Scale
Broadcom trades at 15 times this year’s earnings, and the article cites 66% projected EPS growth for 2027. The base year differs from Nvidia’s multiple, so the two numbers do not line up cleanly. Alphabet sits at 18 times next year’s earnings, the highest multiple of the three, and the fact sheet gives no 2027 growth projection for it. Alphabet also differs in kind. It buys AI chips and sells AI services, so it sits on both sides of the spending trade.
The mixed bases are a reminder to compare carefully. A 15 times multiple on this year’s earnings is not directly comparable with a 9 times multiple on next year’s. Anyone building a trade off these numbers should recompute them on a single basis first.
The 2027 Earnings Growth Assumption: 72%, 66% and 118%
Every bullish case in this debate rests on one assumption, that 2027 earnings arrive roughly as forecast. The three growth numbers in the Seeking Alpha piece are large. Nvidia at 72%, Broadcom at 66% and AMD at 118% describe profit growth of a kind that only shows up when a spending wave is still building.
Compare those figures with the broader market. Allianz forecast roughly 8% U.S. EPS growth for 2027 and about 8% for Europe. Axios reported that Wall Street expected around 35% EPS growth for 2026. The chip names are forecast to grow earnings many times faster than the market, which explains both the premium they commanded and the sharpness of the drop when doubt crept in.
Morningstar offered a revenue view that fits the picture. It estimated Nvidia revenue growth of 46% in 2026 after an estimated 55% gain in 2025, and Broadcom revenue growth near 124% in 2026 after roughly 50% in 2025. Revenue growth is not EPS growth, but the direction is consistent. Both firms ride the same spending curve, and both lose if it flattens.
The risk is asymmetric. When a stock is priced for 72% growth and delivers 40%, the shares can fall even though the business grows. When a stock is priced for very little, as the 9 times figure suggests, good news has more room to lift it. The market is currently betting on the first outcome for the sector and the second for Nvidia.
AI Capex: The Number Behind Every Chip Forecast
Chip earnings are other companies’ capital spending. When Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle build data centers, Nvidia, Broadcom and AMD book the revenue. That makes hyperscaler capex the single most important input to the AI bubble bear market debate, and it is the number analysts watch hardest.
Business Insider reported that Alphabet kept its 2026 capital expenditure outlook at $195 billion to $205 billion. Its third-quarter 2026 capex came in at $51.36 billion, below the $55.09 billion analysts expected. The gap of about $3.73 billion works out to roughly 7% under the consensus figure. A miss of that size would normally pass without comment, but it landed while investors were already nervous.
Axios sharpened the worry on November 23. It reported that analysts covering the five biggest spenders did not raise their forecasts for further capital investment, and it warned that slower capex would curb profits for AI infrastructure suppliers. Morningstar framed the long arc, noting that hyperscaler capex more than doubled from 2023 to 2025 and was expected to nearly triple again by 2029. The question is whether that second leap still looks realistic.
Dell’s results gave one counterweight. Our report on Dell lifting its forecast as its AI backlog hit $132.3 billion showed that orders for AI servers remained strong at that company. Demand today and capex plans for 2027 are different questions, and the market is pricing the second one.
Debt-Funded Spending and the Financing Risk
A second pressure point sits beneath capex. Business Insider reported that investors were increasingly concerned about the sustainability of the AI trade amid rising debt-funded spending by some companies. When a company funds data centers from cash flow, it can slow spending at will. When it funds them with borrowed money, the debt stays no matter how the projects perform.
Morningstar listed financing among the risks that could cut AI spending, alongside energy constraints, weaker-than-expected AI benefits and technological breakthroughs that make AI more efficient. The last item cuts against the bulls in a counterintuitive way. A breakthrough that lets models run on less hardware is good for users and bad for chip vendors.
Morningstar also noted that lower hyperscaler spending could reduce accelerator revenue for Nvidia, Broadcom and AMD, making it harder for those firms to meet elevated expectations. That sentence captures the whole mechanism in one line. Spending falls, supplier revenue falls, and valuations built on growth reset.
Energy adds a physical limit that money cannot solve quickly. Data centers need power, and a company can place a chip order far faster than it can secure the electricity to run the chips. If that bottleneck tightens, it would slow capex even when financing stays cheap.
Allianz Calls It a Correction, Not a Popped Bubble
Not every analyst agrees with the Seeking Alpha framing. In a November 25 note titled “Black Friday before Black Friday: Stocks at a discount?” Allianz Research described the retreat as a broadening, sentiment-driven correction rather than a deterioration in fundamentals or a popping of the AI bubble. The firm said strong Nvidia earnings and the Anthropic IPO failed to stop the AI selloff, which then spread to other sectors.
That detail is telling. A strong earnings report and a high-profile public listing normally lift the sector. This time neither worked, which suggests the market was reacting to something other than quarterly results. It was reacting to the future, and to positioning. The Anthropic IPO also rattled Asian markets, as our coverage of SoftBank’s 14.48% fall showed.
Allianz’s roughly 8% 2027 EPS growth forecast for the U.S. is a sober anchor. It does not describe an economy about to collapse. It describes ordinary growth, and against that backdrop a 20% drop in tech looks like a reset of expectations, not a verdict on the economy. The two views can both be right in part. Prices can overshoot downward even when the underlying story stays intact.
