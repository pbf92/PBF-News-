TITLE: Amazon's CTO says the 20-watt brain will shape the next computers
OUTLET: PPC Land

Amazon chief technology officer Werner Vogels published his yearly list of technology predictions on November 24, 2026, naming five shifts he expects in 2027 and the years that follow: computing modelled on the human brain, software attacked by its owner's own AI agents before it ships, data sovereignty treated as a design requirement, medical cures found by scientists working with AI agents, and brain interfaces that need no surgery. Amazon ran a summary of the list on its corporate news site, written by Amazon staff, and posted a short video of Vogels reading it on the Amazon News YouTube channel. The full essay sits on his personal blog, All Things Distributed.
In Short
Amazon's chief technology officer has released his annual list of five predictions, this time covering energy-saving computers inspired by the brain, software that is tested by automated attackers before release, control over where data is stored, faster medical research and brain-reading devices that need no surgery. For marketers, two of the five matter most: the cost and power behind the computing that runs AI tools, and the growing pressure to prove where customer data lives and which AI model is allowed to use it. Nothing changes on a set date, but Vogels says the main question he now hears from IT chiefs is about geopolitics rather than cost, and that shift reaches the cloud contracts that ad tech and marketing data depend on.
Five predictions, in two versions
Amazon published the list in two forms on the same day. The written summary carries the headline "Amazon CTO Werner Vogels' 5 tech predictions for 2027 and beyond" and the subtitle "From computers built on living neurons to brain interfaces that need no surgery, Amazon's CTO shares what's coming in 2027 and beyond." The video, titled "Werner Vogels' Tech Predictions for 2027 and Beyond," has Vogels speaking each prediction to camera. Its description says he "shares five predictions shaping 2027 and beyond, from computers built on living neurons and systems that defend themselves."
The two versions share the same order but not always the same words. Two headings differ, the number of test attacks differs, and the time frame for brain interfaces differs.
Vogels opens the video in broad terms. "The world around us is changing rapidly. How we learn, how we work, how we interact with one another. At the center of all of this is technology," he says. "Multiple golden ages are happening at once. In biotech, in space, and of course, AI. What excites me the most is the possibility to use these advances for good."
According to Amazon, robotics, AI, space exploration and biotech are converging into what Vogels calls "a golden era of innovation." The summary describes him as a self-declared techno-optimist who "also acknowledges that technology can be used for malicious acts, and that organizations need to prepare for bad actors and an unpredictable geopolitical landscape."
He splits the list by horizon. "Some of this year's predictions are important to think about right now," Vogels says. "Others challenge you to think of what is possible, not only in the next year, but in the decade to come."
A computer that runs on 20 watts
The first prediction starts with a problem as old as the modern computer. For most of computing's history, according to Amazon, memory and processing "have lived in separate places, with data moving between them, consuming energy, and producing heat." The brain works differently. It "computes in memory, improves with use, and runs with the efficiency of a small lightbulb," the summary says.
In the video, Vogels puts numbers on the gap. "First, computing returns to biology. Where a GPU burns about 700 watts, your brain only needs 20 watts," he says. A GPU, or graphics processing unit, is the type of chip that trains and runs most current AI models. By a PPC Land calculation, 700 watts is 35 times the brain's 20.
"Brain-inspired systems are learning to do more with far less, making compute cheaper and more efficient," Vogels continues. "Neuroscience now belongs on every builder's reading list."
The summary gives two examples. Cortical Labs, based in Melbourne, Australia, "grows living human neurons on silicon chips that learn tasks through trial and error, using significantly less power than a rack of high-end graphics processors." Separately, chip designers are building neuromorphic processors, which store and process information in the same place, the way the brain does. Neither example comes with a power figure, a price or a date for commercial use.
Energy, according to Amazon, will drive much of the progress. Systems that compute where they store information and learn from fewer examples could also take computing to places where electricity and network access are limited, "including rural hospitals, schools, and farms."
Why should an advertiser care about the wattage of a chip? Every AI feature inside an ad platform, from automated bidding to generated creative, runs on banks of graphics chips in data centers, and the electricity and hardware behind each answer feed into what platforms charge and how widely they deploy those features. These running costs are known as inference costs. In April 2026, PPC Land reported on an investigation by Paligo into the hidden infrastructure cost behind every AI answer, which cited a projection of 945 terawatt hours of electricity a year for AI data centers by 2030 and described inference costs doubling every quarter. Vogels' first prediction is a bet on one way out of that curve. It is not a near-term one: the summary gives no year in which brain-inspired chips would run commercial workloads.
Software that attacks itself
The second prediction concerns security, and Amazon places it in 2027. Finding a software flaw and turning it into an attack, according to the summary, "once took expertise, weeks of work, and resources few organizations could afford, which gave defenders time to patch." That window has closed. "In the past year, that timescale has collapsed," the summary says. AI agents "now bring the expertise with them, run in swarms, and cost so little that an attacker can run hundreds of them in parallel."
Vogels puts it more bluntly on camera. "Second, every system defends itself. Cheap, capable agents have flipped the economics of cyberattacks," he says. "So before your software ever ships, your own agents will have attacked it thousands of times, turning security from a final gate into an intrinsic part of software development."
The written version is slightly more cautious on the count. According to Amazon, Vogels predicts adversarial testing will move into every phase of software development, so that "before software reaches the outside world, an organization's own agents will have attacked it hundreds or thousands of times, and those attacks will continue for as long as the system runs."
Amazon says it is already seeing the early stages of this work, naming AWS Continuum, which it describes as "a service that looks for risks in customers' code and proves which ones are exploitable." The summary gives no price, launch date or availability for Continuum; its link points to Amazon's coverage of the AWS Summit in New York in 2026. Agents, according to the summary, "will free security experts to concentrate on work that requires human judgment, and systems will get safer as a result."
The prediction arrives a day after another Amazon executive made a related argument. On November 23, AWS chief executive Matt Garman argued that AI safety cannot depend on an emergency brake, calling for controls built around models rather than trust in model behaviour. Amazon's predictions page promotes that essay alongside the list.
The marketing stack is an obvious target for the kind of cheap, automated attack Vogels describes. It is built from outside code: tags, pixels, software development kits in apps, plug-ins and, increasingly, agents connected to ad accounts. In February 2026, the Dutch data protection authority flagged open-source AI agents as a Trojan horse for hackers, and about one fifth of plug-ins for the OpenClaw agent appeared to contain malware that steals credentials or cryptocurrency. In August, GreyNoise found scanners posing as ClaudeBot hunting credential files from 824 addresses, borrowing the names of AI crawlers to look for cloud keys and credential stores. Vogels' prediction describes the defensive mirror image of those campaigns: the same cheap agents, pointed at a company's own code before anyone else gets there.
Sovereignty becomes a design rule
The third prediction is the one closest to the daily concerns of ad tech and data teams, and it rests on what Vogels says his clients ask him. "For the last few years, a main concern Vogels has heard from chief information officers (CIOs) has been how to control cost," according to Amazon. "In the past year, the number 1 question he hears is how to deal with geopolitical tensions that may affect where they store data, where they run workloads, and which services they rely on."
The summary heading calls sovereignty "a non-functional requirement." In software engineering, that phrase describes how a system must behave, such as how secure, fast or reliable it is, as opposed to what it does. In the video, Vogels uses plainer words. "Third, sovereignty becomes an engineering requirement. In 2027, we will see a rapid shift in how builders think about resilience," he says. "Sovereignty needs to be built into the full stack."
The written version is more specific about what that means. "In 2027 and beyond, builders will design for sovereignty the way they design for security and cost," the summary says. "Where data lives, who holds the keys, and which AI model touches it will be requirements a system proves on every deployment, because an AI agent can now add a replica in another region or swap a model in minutes."
That last clause carries the argument. A copy of a database in another country, or a switch from one AI model to another, used to require a project. If an agent can do either in minutes, according to this reasoning, then a one-off compliance review no longer holds, and the check has to run every time the system changes.
Vogels also warns against going too far. According to Amazon, he cautions that "isolation is not the same thing as control." The organizations that get this right "will decide, workload by workload, how much sovereignty each one needs, whether that means a dedicated environment such as the AWS European Sovereign Cloud or customer-held keys."
Amazon has a commercial interest in this prediction. The AWS European Sovereign Cloud, the summary's example of a dedicated environment, links to Amazon's January 2026 announcement of its launch. PPC Land reported in April that euNetworks joined the AWS European Sovereign Cloud as its first connectivity partner, noting the service reached general availability in January 2026 with its first region in Brandenburg, Germany, and investment of more than 7.8 billion euros in Germany by 2040.
The question Vogels describes did not start with AWS. In June 2025, Microsoft France's legal director told a French Senate inquiry that he could not guarantee French data in European data centers against access by US agencies, as PPC Land reported in July 2025. In October 2025, the European Commission's Cloud Sovereignty Framework scored providers on eight criteria, and PPC Land found it gave more weight to efficiency than to independence: supply chain counted for 20%, while data and AI sovereignty counted for 10%. In the Netherlands, regulator ACM's draft guidance under the EU Data Act says cloud providers lose switching fees in January 2027, which would make moving between providers cheaper.
