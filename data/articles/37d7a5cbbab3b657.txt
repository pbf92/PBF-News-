TITLE: PENG and Quadratus Lumborum Blocks Show Comparable Analgesia After Hip Surgery
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

In a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing regional anesthesia techniques after hip surgery, researchers found that the pericapsular nerve group block and the quadratus lumborum block provide comparable postoperative analgesia, opioid-sparing benefits, and short-term safety. The investigation directly compared head-to-head randomized controlled trials to evaluate how both nerve block approaches manage patient pain, recovery, and adverse outcomes following surgical interventions on the hip.
The review team systematically searched major electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, identifying relevant clinical studies published up to April 15, 2026. Eligible literature was restricted to head-to-head randomized controlled trials comparing pericapsular nerve group blocks directly against quadratus lumborum blocks. Ultimately, seven randomized controlled trials encompassing a total of 536 surgical patients met the eligibility criteria for quantitative synthesis.
Postoperative pain served as the primary clinical outcome, analyzed across eight distinct assessment windows covering four postoperative time points evaluated both at rest and during patient movement. Investigators pooled pain scores as standardized mean differences and converted the data to mean differences on a ten-point scale to benchmark against a one-point minimal clinically important difference. Across six of the eight evaluated assessment windows, pooled estimates fell within one point, demonstrating that neither block conferred a clinically meaningful advantage over the other.
Secondary outcomes demonstrated similar performance between the two regional techniques. Total twenty-four-hour opioid consumption and the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting were comparable between patients receiving either nerve block. Furthermore, a narrative synthesis of the trial data identified a preliminary, transient motor-sparing advantage favoring the pericapsular nerve group block in some randomized trials, though the researchers noted this signal requires definitive verification.
Despite the generally comparable performance, the authors highlighted critical evidence limitations regarding two specific pain assessment windows: early pain during movement and resting pain at twenty-four hours. Outcomes for these two periods remained inconclusive due to high statistical heterogeneity and very low certainty of evidence. Consequently, the authors emphasized that potential early motor-sparing benefits remain exploratory and warrant further confirmation through adequately powered trials using objective functional endpoints.
