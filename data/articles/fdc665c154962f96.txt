TITLE: Liver Resection Yields Lower Recurrence Than Microwave Ablation in Solitary Liver Cancer
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic review and meta-analysis comparing microwave ablation and surgical liver resection for solitary hepatocellular carcinoma measuring five centimeters or less found no statistically significant difference in overall survival between the two interventions, but identified significantly better recurrence-related outcomes among patients treated with surgical resection. The synthesis evaluated findings from nine retrospective cohort investigations comprising 1,961 individuals diagnosed with early-stage disease. While long-term overall mortality risks appeared comparable across the available data, the pooled risk of disease recurrence favored surgical removal over thermal ablation. The review authors stressed that because all included data originated from retrospective observational studies, the cumulative evidence provides very low certainty, does not establish therapeutic equivalence, and must be interpreted cautiously.
Solitary hepatocellular carcinoma measuring up to five centimeters falls within stage Ia under the China Liver Cancer Staging guidelines. For patients in this clinical category, liver resection represents the recommended first-line treatment, whereas microwave ablation serves as another potentially curative therapeutic modality. To clarify comparative efficacy, the researchers designed a systematic investigation comparing overall survival alongside recurrence-related outcomes, specifically evaluating disease-free survival, recurrence-free survival, and progression-free survival.
The review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook, with its protocol registered under PROSPERO identifier CRD420261323897. Systematic literature searches were initially performed across Embase, PubMed, and the Cochrane Library on September 23, 2025, and across Web of Science on October 6, 2025. All searches were comprehensively repeated on October 12, 2026, targeting randomized controlled trials and cohort studies comparing microwave ablation against liver resection for solitary tumors measuring five centimeters or smaller. The research protocol pooled overall survival and combined disease-free or recurrence-free survival, while separate analyses assessed disease-free survival, including eligible progression-free survival, and recurrence-free survival alone to examine the potential influence of outcome definitions.
The final quantitative synthesis incorporated nine retrospective cohort studies containing 1,961 patients, including 987 treated with liver resection and 974 treated with microwave ablation. In the primary overall survival analysis combining seven reporting studies, no statistically significant difference emerged between modalities, yielding a hazard ratio of 1.07 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.89 to 1.28 and a p-value of 0.48. This finding remained nonsignificant when evaluated using the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman method, which generated an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.07 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.89 to 1.29. The authors noted that the lack of statistically significant variance in observed survival does not demonstrate therapeutic equivalence.
For recurrence-related endpoints, the combined analysis of disease-free and recurrence-free survival across all nine studies statistically favored liver resection. The pooled estimate produced a hazard ratio of 1.31 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.07 to 1.61 and a p-value of 0.01. Analysis under the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman method likewise favored surgical resection, showing a hazard ratio of 1.31 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.02 to 1.69. However, the calculated prediction interval under this method crossed the line of no effect.
Subgroup analyses reinforced the recurrence advantage of surgery in specific patient cohorts. In studies restricted to propensity score-matched cohorts, liver resection demonstrated superior outcomes with a hazard ratio of 1.34, a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.13 to 1.60, and a p-value of 0.0009. Similarly, among patients presenting with tumors measuring three centimeters or smaller, surgical resection significantly reduced recurrence with a hazard ratio of 1.44, a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.13 to 1.83, and a p-value of 0.003. Despite these directional findings, formal tests examining differences between subgroups did not achieve statistical significance. The authors highlighted that residual confounding remains possible within the observational literature, warranting careful clinical judgment.
