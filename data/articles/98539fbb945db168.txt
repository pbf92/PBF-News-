TITLE: Recording Academy and SAG-AFTRA back DC cap holding ticket resales to 110%
OUTLET: PPC Land

Ten organizations of musicians, songwriters, performers, artist managers and fans filed amicus briefs in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia on November 13, 2026, asking Judge Carl J. Nichols to let Washington, D.C.'s new cap on ticket resale prices take effect. The briefs, both titled "Brief of Artist and Fan Organizations as Amici Curiae in Support of Defendant," were filed in two parallel lawsuits against the District: one brought by Ticket Policy Forum, Inc. together with SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, Gametime and TickPick, the other by the National Association of Ticketing Professionals and two broker members, ASC Ticket Company and Stage Front Tickets, Inc.
The coalition includes the Recording Academy, SAG-AFTRA and the American Federation of Musicians. Both sets of plaintiffs want a preliminary injunction blocking the Restricting Egregious Scalping Against Live Entertainment (RESALE) Amendment Act of 2026 before it begins to apply on January 1, 2027. The law limits resale prices to the initial ticket price plus 10%, caps secondary exchange fees at 10% of that initial price and requires the original price to be disclosed. Judge Nichols granted leave to file the briefs on November 16. Three days later, on November 19, he heard oral argument on both injunction motions and took them under advisement, according to minute entries on the two dockets. No ruling had been entered at the time of writing.
In Short
Ten music industry and fan groups have asked a federal judge in Washington to reject attempts by ticket resale companies and brokers to block a District of Columbia law that caps resale prices at 10% above the original ticket price. The fight affects the online ticket exchanges, including SeatGeek and Vivid Seats, and the professional brokers who sell through them, along with fans buying tickets to concerts and shows in the District. The judge heard arguments on November 19 but has not decided whether the law can start on January 1, 2027, so the caps, fee limits and price disclosure rules remain in limbo for now.
Two lawsuits, one judge
The two cases were filed two days apart in October and are both assigned to Judge Nichols. They attack the same statute from slightly different positions in the market. The Ticket Policy Forum case represents the exchanges, the marketplaces where tickets change hands. The National Association of Ticketing Professionals case represents the brokers, the professional sellers who list inventory on those marketplaces. According to the amicus brief in the brokers' case, the association was formerly called the National Association of Ticket Brokers.

According to the coalition's statement distributed on November 18, the plaintiffs bring Commerce Clause, due process and equal protection challenges. The exchanges' own motion adds a First Amendment claim against the original-price disclosure requirement, and it asks for a ruling by December 10 so that the companies can seek appellate review if needed and make compliance decisions before January.
What the RESALE Act requires
The briefs describe the statute through its codified sections. The D.C. Council's stated purpose, quoted from the preamble in both briefs, was to "protect artists, venues, and consumers ... from unfair and deceptive practices in live event ticket purchases." According to the coalition's statement, the Council passed the law unanimously.
The brokers' motion, filed on the public docket, adds detail the briefs do not repeat. It says the law exempts movie screenings and sporting events organized by teams or associations located in the District, treats violations as Class 2 civil infractions carrying fines of $1,000 to $8,000 per offense, and classifies them as unlawful trade practices open to enforcement by the Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection, the D.C. Attorney General and consumers.
How the caps would apply to a resale
Pieced together from the two briefs and the brokers' motion, the statute sets out the following path for a ticket to a covered event once the law applies:
- A fan or broker buys a ticket at its initial price from a venue or primary ticketer. The cap is measured against the total price of that initial ticket, according to the brokers' motion.
- When the ticket is listed for resale, the original price paid has to be disclosed, under § 28-5506(a)(2).
- The resale price cannot exceed the initial ticket price plus 10%, under § 28-5506(b).
- The secondary exchange handling the sale cannot charge fees above 10% of the initial ticket price, under § 28-5506(c).
- Selling below the original price remains unrestricted. According to the brokers' brief, the cap "caps resale above the original price, not below it."
- From January 1, 2028, the Mayor can raise or lower the caps by rulemaking, under § 28-5507.
On a ticket originally priced at $65.80, the figure the briefs cite for a sold-out show at the Black Cat club, those rules would put the ceiling at $72.38 for the resale price and $6.58 for the exchange's fees (PPC Land calculation). Resale listings for that show, according to the briefs, ranged from $156 to $1,047.02 on Ticketmaster, SeatGeek and StubHub. The top listing works out to a markup of roughly 1,491% (PPC Land calculation); the briefs describe it as "nearly sixteen times face value."
Who filed and what they say
The ten amici, as listed in the corporate disclosure statements, are a mix of labor unions, trade groups and nonprofits:
All ten are advocates for the law, and several testified for it before the Council's Committee on Public Works and Operations, according to the briefs. Katherine Van Dyck of KVD Strategies PLLC wrote and filed both briefs. Her certification of counsel states that no party or party's counsel wrote any part of them or paid for their preparation.
The coalition's statement carried quotes from leaders of eight member organizations. "The RESALE Act is the gold standard for event ticketing policy," said Tino Gaglardi, President of the American Federation of Musicians. Todd Dupler, Chief Advocacy & Public Policy Officer at the Recording Academy, said: "Music fans shouldn't have to compete with bots and speculators to see their favorite artists, and artists shouldn't watch their fans get priced out." Kevin Erickson, Director of Future of Music Coalition, said the brokers and resale platforms challenging the law "profit when tickets meant for fans are bought up in bulk and resold at massive markups." Nathaniel Marro, Executive Director of the National Independent Talent Organization, called the lawsuit "a last-ditch effort to stop progress for fans and artists across the country and before common sense ticket reforms hit the halls of Congress."
Van Dyck framed the law as an old idea rather than a new one. "Laws regulating and capping ticket resale and speculation have existed for over a century, and judges, including the Supreme Court in 1965, have rejected nearly every constitutional challenge they have faced," she said.
The central claim: brokers, not fans
Both briefs open their argument the same way. The plaintiffs describe the fan who falls ill or cannot attend and needs a safe way to sell a ticket. The coalition argues that this fan is not the plaintiffs' principal customer. "Plaintiffs are not fans. They are professional ticket brokers," the brief in the brokers' case says, describing businesses "that buy tickets, often months before an event, and resell them at a markup."

The coalition also quotes the Council committee's own report, which said fans who pay inflated resale prices "subsidize a reseller who provided no value to the artist, the venue, or the event."
From there the briefs build a chain of argument. Artists often price tickets below what the market would bear, for reasons the GAO listed in 2018: to avoid being "perceived as gouging fans," to sell out shows, and to draw "the most enthusiastic fans ... rather than just those able to pay the most." The committee report, as quoted in the briefs, called "this gap between face value and market value" what "creates the scalping opportunity." Uncapped resale, in the coalition's account, lets brokers capture that discount through arbitrage between the artist's price and the market-clearing price.
The coalition makes the same point about bots. New York banned ticket bots in 2010, according to the briefs, yet six years later its Attorney General found that bot use had "reached epidemic proportions in the ticketing industry." The briefs argue that bans alone did not work because "the profits bots generate are simply too high," and that caps remove the incentive.
The exchanges' fees, and the sealed numbers
The brief in the Ticket Policy Forum case contains a section absent from the brokers' version, aimed squarely at how SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, Gametime and TickPick earn money. The exchanges charge fees calculated on the resale price. According to the brief, the companies' own declarations state that their ordinary rates will exceed 10% of the initial price and violate the cap without any change in the platforms' pricing.
The GAO, the brief says, found that most secondary exchanges set their buyer fee as a percentage of the ticket's list price, averaging 31% and reaching as high as 56%, and that most also charged sellers a second fee, "typically 10 percent of the ticket's sale price." Exchanges also charge download and delivery fees for electronic tickets, according to the brief, while primary sellers such as Ticketmaster charge for delivery only by mail or courier. Vivid Seats sets fees based on factors including "the time remaining until the event," "seller economics," and "partner economics," the brief says, citing the company's declaration. SeatGeek operates in both the primary and secondary markets, allowing it, the coalition argues, to collect fees on multiple transactions involving a single ticket. Exchange fees make up roughly a fifth of all spending in the $18 billion secondary market, according to the Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator figure the brief cites.
"This is a business model that rewards markups," the brief says. "The higher the markups, the more Plaintiffs earn, on both sides of the sale, with no change in the service Plaintiffs provide or the costs Plaintiffs incur."
What the coalition cannot see is the exchanges' actual take rate. The plaintiffs filed their declarations with cost and fee figures redacted, and Judge Nichols granted their motion to seal on October 19. In a footnote, the coalition asks the court to reconsider. "Because the motion to seal these records is also under seal, it is impossible for Amici to respond to Plaintiffs' sealing arguments in any meaningful way," the brief says, citing the "strong presumption of public access" to judicial records in the D.C. Circuit.
The brief also takes on the exchanges' argument that the original price is often unknowable. According to the coalition, Gametime receives a face-value figure through sellers' inventory feeds for a share of its District sales, SeatGeek has the original price in its own records whenever it was the primary ticketer, and both Vivid Seats and Gametime already require sellers to supply a ticket's face value where a jurisdiction's law requires it. A declaration filed by Van Dyck adds screenshots showing that Vivid Seats displays a separate fee line item at checkout for Maryland concerts but only an all-in price for California and D.C. concerts, which the coalition offers as evidence that the exchanges already tailor their sites to local law.
