TITLE: YouTube adds estimated TV co-viewers to public view counts
OUTLET: PPC Land

YouTube today said it is rolling out a change to the number printed beneath its videos. Public view counts will include co-views, which the company describes as "the estimated number of people watching together on TV," according to a Short posted on Creator Insider, YouTube's official channel for creator updates. The company said the reason was scale: viewers now watch more than 1 billion hours of YouTube on televisions every day.
In Short
YouTube is adding its estimate of how many people watch together on a TV to the public view count, so a single playback on a television can now register as more than one view. That changes the most visible number creators show to brands and the count advertisers see accumulate on their own video ads, while the measures YouTube uses to pay creators stay as they were. The public counter is now partly a modelled figure, and YouTube has not published how the model works.
What YouTube said today
The Short, titled "New! Co-Views Included in Public YouTube View Counts," went up on the Creator Insider channel shortly after 19:00 Berlin time. Its description sets out the change to YouTube's view counts in one sentence: "YouTube is rolling out an update to include co-views - the estimated number of people watching together on TV - in the public view counts for videos." The underlying idea is co-viewing, more than one person in front of a single screen, which a count of devices or playbacks cannot see.
The justification follows. "YouTube is doing this because viewers are now watching over 1 billion hours of YouTube on TVs every day," the description reads, adding that the inclusion of co-views in the public view count will "Credit creators true reach with a platform-backed metric to better inform everything from channel strategy, to brand pitches." A second aim is to "Provide a more complete picture of how audiences are engaging" with content, "aligning with TV industry standards," a passage that points to how "traditional broadcast and streaming networks estimate" their audiences.
No rollout schedule, list of countries or methodology appears in the description. About 24 minutes after publication, the Short itself showed 335 views, 72 likes and 28 comments. It links to an earlier Creator Insider video, "New! Analytics is Now Insights in YouTube Studio," published on November 13, 2026, when YouTube renamed the Analytics section of YouTube Studio.
What the Help Center now says
Two YouTube Help Center pages carry the new definition. "Understand your YouTube content performance" (answer 12220281) and "How engagement metrics are counted" (answer 2991785) both open with the same sentence: "Beginning November 2026, YouTube public view counts on video watch pages incorporate co-views by estimating the number of individual viewers watching together on TV screens."
Both pages keep the first-frame rule introduced earlier in the year. "Views are counted the moment a video starts to play across all formats, including Shorts, long-form videos (VODs), and live streams," the text reads.
On money, the pages say the update "won't impact" YouTube Partner Program earnings or eligibility, which continue to run on engaged views and qualified views rather than on the public count. Their wording differs. The content performance page says earnings "will still be based on "engaged Shorts views" and "engaged watch hours"" and eligibility on "qualified Shorts views" and "qualified watch hours." The engagement metrics page names "engaged views" and "engaged watch hours" for earnings and "qualified views" for eligibility. Neither page explains the difference.
The co-view estimate does not land in every metric. The content performance page marks, line by line, which numbers include it.

Two notes on the separate Views (Co-viewed) metric give its end date in slightly different words. One says "This metric is available in YouTube Insights Advanced Mode until May 2027." The other says "This metric will be available until May 2027." The co-view rate entry, meanwhile, describes a way back to the old number: total views divided by the rate give "baseline views without co-viewers," in the wording of the section, or "without co-views," in the wording of the metrics table.
How the public count works, according to the Help Center
Taken together, the two pages describe the counter in six steps.
- A view is counted "the moment a video starts to play," in every format: Shorts, long-form videos and live streams.
- When playback happens on a TV screen, YouTube estimates "the number of individual viewers watching together" and adds them to the count. The device examples given are YouTube TV and the main YouTube app on TV.
- The result is the public view count on video watch pages. For live streams, concurrent viewers carry the same estimate.
- Ad views that contribute to a video's public count, which advertisers know as "YouTube public views," are counted the moment a video ad starts to play across Shorts and in-stream ads, and they carry the same TV estimate.
- Inside YouTube Insights, the Co-view rate shows how far co-viewing moves the total, and dividing views by that rate returns a baseline without co-viewers. The separate Views (Co-viewed) metric stays in Advanced Mode until May 2027.
- Engaged views and unique viewers exclude co-views, and Partner Program earnings and eligibility continue to run on engaged and qualified measures.
From a private metric to the public counter
None of this arrived without warning. YouTube introduced a creator-only metric called views co-viewed on September 5, 2026, in a Creator Insider video presented by creator liaison Rene Ritchie. Until then, every playback on a television device had counted as one view. The new figure sat in YouTube Analytics, in advanced mode, needed up to 48 hours to process, and left public watch-page counts untouched. Creators were told they could use it in reporting and in pitches to brands.
Brand partners had no way to check it. The metric was visible only to the creator who owned the channel, with no published model and no external audit. Today's change removes the visibility problem by putting the estimate in the number everyone sees. It does not remove the audit problem.
The building blocks were assembled over several months. Unique Reach arrived in Advanced Analytics on May 22, 2026 as an estimate of individual people rather than devices, accounting for shared viewing on TVs. On the advertising side, the Google Ads API moved seven reach and frequency metrics to a Total Co-view definition on June 2, 2026, counting people who share a connected television screen. That followed a much older step: in April 2022, Google brought YouTube co-viewing metrics into Google Ads and Display & Video 360, citing a Nielsen finding that multiple adult viewers watched YouTube together on the television screen 26% of the time, against 22% for linear TV.
What changes today is the audience for the estimate. Co-viewing has been part of how advertisers buy YouTube on television for more than four years. Now it is part of the number on the watch page, read by viewers, brands and competitors alike.
The estimate behind the number
Neither Help Center page describes how the estimate is made. There is no mention of surveys, panels, models, error margins or update frequency. The only description is the phrase "by estimating the number of individual viewers watching together on TV screens."
The September video was slightly more forthcoming, describing statistical modelling based on demographic patterns, video genre and viewing times, with no sensor counting people in the room. Tubefilter, which covered the same video on September 9, 2026, reported that a YouTube representative pointed back to the video when asked how YouTube decides that several people are watching one screen.
Google's advertising documentation is more specific about its own method. According to Google Ads Help, Google measures co-viewing on TV screens using a combination of its connected TV panel data and real-time, census-level surveys covering more than 100 countries in more than 70 languages, with statistical methods applied to remove bias and a dynamic reach model that weighs factors such as day of week, time of day, country and user demographics. The same page says Nielsen DAR guarantees remain available to advertisers. Whether the public view count uses the same approach as the advertising products is not stated anywhere in the material YouTube published today.
Television as the reason
YouTube's case rests on the living room. The 1 billion hours figure is not new: in February 2024, YouTube was already citing more than 1 billion hours of daily viewing on TVs globally alongside Nielsen data naming it the top streaming service in the United States. The phrasing in today's Short is effectively unchanged nearly three years later.
The share has grown since. Nielsen's August 2026 Gauge put YouTube at a record 15.8% of US television time, more than double Netflix's 7.7%. Yet the money has moved more slowly than the minutes. In the third quarter of 2026, YouTube ad revenue grew 9% to $11.2 billion, its slowest growth of the year, even as Alphabet chief executive Sundar Pichai described YouTube as the leader in the living room.
Co-viewing is also where rivals have pressed their case against YouTube. Research by the Video Advertising Bureau and TVision put co-viewing at 60% of impressions on premium streaming platforms against 45% on YouTube on connected television. The VAB repeated the comparison in an October infographic claiming 33% stronger co-viewing for premium video. A public counter that credits YouTube videos with co-viewers is, among other things, YouTube's own number entering that argument.
The "TV industry standards" line in the Short refers to a real practice. Television ratings have long counted people rather than sets, and networks report audiences that include everyone in the room. The difference lies in who produces the estimate. In television, audience figures come from third-party measurement companies such as Nielsen. YouTube's co-view figure is produced by YouTube.
What changes for advertisers, creators and brand deals
Advertisers. The ads section of the engagement metrics page says that "YouTube public views," which represent ad views contributing to the public view count, now incorporate co-viewing estimates. The content performance page adds that "the methodology for counting ad views across all content (referred to by advertisers as "YouTube public views") has also been updated." Neither page mentions billing. Google's documentation has long treated billable ad views and public views as separate figures that are not expected to reconcile. Nothing published today says that billable views or cost-per-view charges change. What does change is the public count on an advertiser's own video when it runs as an ad on television screens.
Creators. The public count, watch time and live concurrents now include the estimate, while engaged views and unique viewers do not. In PPC Land's reading, that arithmetic has a consequence: any ratio built from engaged views over total views will fall for channels with a large television audience, because co-views enter the denominator and not the numerator. Partner Program earnings and eligibility stay on engaged and qualified measures, which matters ahead of February 1, 2027, when YouTube doubles its Partner Program entry thresholds to 8,000 qualified watch hours or 20 million qualified Shorts views. A higher public count will not move a channel closer to either bar.
Brand deals. YouTube names "brand pitches" as a purpose of the change. A brand deal priced on views now rests on a figure that mixes counted plays with modelled people.
