TITLE: Google's AI Mode cuts clicks to websites by 18.8 points, field test finds
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google users whose searches were switched entirely to AI Mode for a week clicked through to outside websites 18.8 percentage points less often than people using standard Google Search, according to a preregistered field experiment with 1,100 US participants that researchers at the University of Pennsylvania and Northeastern University posted to arXiv on August 18, 2026. The same participants visited news sites, Reddit and Wikipedia less often, rated Google as less trustworthy, useful and satisfying, and were more likely to run searches on Bing, DuckDuckGo or Yahoo. A second condition, which tried to remove AI Overviews from ordinary results, produced an estimated 8.8-point rise in click-through, but that estimate depends on a statistical adjustment the authors had to make after a change to Google's page code broke their filter partway through the study.
In Short
In March 2026, researchers used a browser extension to change what about 1,100 US Google users saw for a week, and those pushed entirely into AI Mode clicked out to websites 18.8 percentage points less often than people on normal Google. This matters for publishers and advertisers because AI Mode also cut visits to news sites, Reddit and Wikipedia, lowered trust in Google, and sent more users to rival search engines, while removing AI Overviews appeared to raise clicks in an estimate weakened by a filter that stopped working mid-study. The paper is a preprint that has not been peer reviewed, it tested a forced switch with a young, left-leaning sample over seven days, and it says AI Mode carried no ads at the time, a statement that sits awkwardly with Google's February 2026 introduction of a sponsored shopping format there. What changes is the evidence base: together with an earlier 2026 experiment by researchers at the Indian School of Business and Carnegie Mellon, there are now two randomized studies pointing to fewer outbound clicks from Google's AI search features.
The paper and its authors
The paper is titled "AI in Search Reduces Publisher Referrals Without Improving User Experience: Experimental Evidence." It carries the identifier arXiv:2608.18352v1 and is filed under Information Retrieval (cs.IR), with cross-listings in Computers and Society and Human-Computer Interaction. Its arXiv page records a single version, submitted on Tuesday, August 18, 2026 at 22:08 UTC by Stephanie Wang, and as of November 17, 2026 no revision had been posted. Every page of the 46-page PDF carries a "Draft" watermark.
Five researchers are listed. Stephanie T. Wang, the corresponding author, and Danaé Metaxa are with the Department of Computer and Information Science at the University of Pennsylvania. Jeffrey Gleason and Christo Wilson are at Northeastern University's Khoury College of Computer Sciences, and Yakov Bart is at Northeastern's D'Amore-McKim School of Business. Wang and Gleason are marked as having contributed equally, and the author contributions section credits the two of them with the software, formal analysis and data curation.
arXiv is a preprint server. Papers posted there are checked for basic suitability but are not peer reviewed, and no journal reference or venue appears on the paper's page. The findings below are therefore the authors' own, as submitted, and have not been through independent review.
The paper discloses its funding and its tools. The work was "supported in part by the National Science Foundation under award IIS-2442711," and Gleason was supported by the NSF's Platforms for Exchange and Allocation of Resources traineeship. "The authors declare that they have no competing interests," according to the paper. It also states that "Generative AI (GPT-5.5-mini) was used to lightly edit and rephrase author-written text for clarity and fluency," and that "All analyses, interpretations, and conclusions were developed and verified by the authors." The Institutional Review Boards of both universities approved the study. The authors preregistered their hypotheses on the Open Science Framework and say aggregated data and code are on Harvard Dataverse.
What the researchers set out to test
The question at the center of the paper is one that publishers and Google have argued over since AI Overviews arrived in May 2024: do AI answers on the results page send people onward to websites, or do they replace the visit? The authors frame the dispute directly. Publishers "view AIO and AI Mode as substitutes for their content and report falling referral traffic from Google Search," while "Google disputes these claims, arguing that AI features are complements that have kept overall referral traffic steady, improved click quality, and expanded opportunities for websites to be surfaced through longer, more complex queries."
Google's position has been on the record for some time. In August 2025, Google's head of Search, Liz Reid, said total organic click volume had stayed relatively stable year over year and that Google was sending slightly more quality clicks to websites than a year earlier. Senior vice president Nick Fox wrote on July 17, 2026 that Google was sending billions of clicks to websites every week through AI features in Search, without giving a denominator.
The researchers preregistered three primary hypotheses. The first predicted that AI Mode would lower trust in Google's results and that removing AI features would raise it. The second predicted that AI Mode would lower click-through to external sites and that removing AI features would raise it, based on publisher reports and observational data. The third, built on Google's own claims that AI features increase engagement, predicted that AI Mode would increase the number of search sessions. Four secondary hypotheses covered perceived usefulness, satisfaction, agency and personalization; clicks to news sites, Wikipedia, Reddit and ads; time and searches per session; and switching to other search engines.
How the experiment worked
According to the paper's methods section and supplementary materials, the study ran in eight steps.
- Screening. Participants confirmed four statements on the consent page: "I am living in the United States," "I am 18 years old or older," "My main web browser is Google Chrome," and "My main search engine is Google Search."
- Pre-survey. Before anything changed, participants answered questions on their familiarity with and feelings about "LLM applications such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini," and rated how believable, trustworthy, accurate, biased and complete they found information on Google, along with agency, personalization, usefulness and satisfaction. The survey included an attention check ("Please select 'Slightly' for this question").
- Installation. Participants signed in to the study's web application through Google social authentication, then installed a browser extension from the Chrome Web Store. According to the paper, the extension works in "all Chromium-derived browsers, including Google Chrome." The web application was built with Django and ran on a Northeastern University server backed by a PostgreSQL database.
- Baseline. For three days, every participant used unmodified Google Search while the extension logged searches, outbound clicks and copies of the results pages. This produced pre-treatment values for each behavioral outcome.
- Random assignment. After the baseline, each participant was assigned to one of three conditions "with equal probability and no constraints imposed on group balance."
- Treatment. For seven days, the extension enforced the assigned condition inside the browser. In No AI Search, it watched the page for changes and set the display property of any AI Overview container to "none", whether the Overview sat at the top of the page, in the middle or inside a People Also Ask box. It also hid the AI Mode tab and the AI Mode prompt on the Google home page, and redirected AI Mode searches to standard results. The paper notes that "browser extensions cannot hide the 'AI Mode' prompt in the Chrome browser's address bar." In AI Mode Search, the extension redirected every Google search to AI Mode through the URL parameter udm=50 and hid the "All" and "Web" tabs. News, shopping and image searches stayed available, and participants saw a one-time notice that they would be using AI-driven search for the rest of the study. Current Search received no changes.
- Post-survey. After seven days, participants repeated the trust and experience questions. They also compared an AI Mode response with a standard Google response for three news queries, drawn at random from ten trending queries taken from Google Trends the day before the survey went out.
- Debrief. Participants were paid and shown a statement explaining the purpose of the study, the three conditions and why those details had been withheld until the end.
The ten news queries give a sense of the period: "tsa agents government shutdown," "iran israel war ceasefire," "laguardia plane crash," "us treasury insolvency," "us army enlistment age," "meta social media addiction trial," "retirement benefits social security," "no kings protest near me," "covid 19 cicada variant" and "ice at airports."
How the outcomes were defined
The definitions matter for anyone comparing this study with traffic data from analytics tools. The paper defines click-through rate as "The total number of external clicks originating from Google divided by the total number of Google searches. The numerator excludes clicks to google.com (e.g., follow-up searches). The denominator excludes searches on Google's vertical search pages (e.g., images, news, shopping). Each turn of a conversation in AI Mode is counted as a separate search. This is a user-level metric."
That last rule has consequences. A participant who asks AI Mode three follow-up questions has run three searches under this definition, which lowers the click-through rate of a long conversation. The authors tested the point: counting each AI Mode conversation as one search instead of each prompt, the estimated fall in click-through rate is 14.9 points rather than 18.8, and still statistically significant.
Searches were grouped into sessions using a five-minute rule: "if the inter-arrival time between two searches exceeds 5 minutes, the second query is considered a new session." Checked against 100 manually labelled pairs of queries from a pilot, the rule scored 0.84 on the F1 measure. Ad clicks were "identified using the gclid URL parameter," the click identifier that Google Ads attaches to each ad click. News sites were identified using a curated list of news domains, and Reddit and Wikipedia were singled out because they were the only domains to receive at least 5% of clicks from Google during the pilot.
Who took part
Recruitment ran in waves from March 17 to March 19, 2026, through the research platform Prolific and among Northeastern University students in a work-study program with one of the authors. Prolific participants were paid $2 for screening, the first survey and installing the extension, and $8 for finishing the study. Northeastern students received $17 an hour.
Two numbers in that table frame everything that follows. First, AI Mode was almost unused before the experiment began: 0.6% of searches. The AI Mode condition therefore moved people from near-zero use to near-total use overnight, which is not how most people would meet the product. Second, the sample does not look like the US population on age, education or politics. The authors say race, ethnicity and gender were similar to US Census figures.
The researchers checked that the three groups were balanced on demographics, baseline attitudes and baseline behavior, and found no evidence of imbalance (p = 0.305). Dropout was somewhat higher in the AI Mode group: 36.6% did not complete the final survey, against 32.5% for No AI and 32.3% for Current Search, and 26.5% ran no searches at all during the treatment week, against 24.0% and 20.7%.
