TITLE: ADHD Medication in Pregnancy: BMJ Study of 3,643,376 Nordic Births Finds No Added Autism Risk
OUTLET: Tech Times

Children whose mothers took ADHD medication during pregnancy were no more likely to be diagnosed with any neurodevelopmental disorder, among them autism, ADHD, intellectual disability and learning disorders, once researchers adjusted for maternal ADHD, smoking and other factors, according to a registry study of 3,643,376 births in Denmark, Norway and Sweden published Nov. 25 in The BMJ. For women who take ADHD medication and are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, the result speaks to a decision many face with little evidence: whether staying on treatment puts a child's brain development at risk, weighed against what stopping can do to a woman's mental health and daily functioning and family life.
The paper, "Prenatal exposure to drug treatment for attention deficit / hyperactivity disorder and risk of neurodevelopmental disorders: Scandinavian multinational cohort study," carries DOI 10.1136/bmj-2026-101236. Claudia Bruno of the University of Sydney is the lead author, and the work was done with the Norwegian Institute of Public Health. Bruno's own conversations with women from ADHD Norway, the country's national ADHD advocacy and support organization, inspired the project.
The team followed children born from 2000 to 2021, for a median of about nine years in Sweden and 10 years in Norway and Denmark. Of the 3,643,376 children, 7,595 (0.2%) were exposed to ADHD medication at some point in pregnancy, and 2,389 (0.1%) were exposed in the second or third trimester. Methylphenidate accounted for 69% of exposure, amphetamines for 20% and atomoxetine for 7%, and only 4% of exposed pregnancies involved more than one type. The outcomes examined included ADHD, autism, intellectual disability, learning disorders, speech and language disorders, tic disorders, epilepsy, and hearing and vision impairments.
Raw Diagnosis Rates Were Higher Until Researchers Adjusted for Maternal ADHD and Smoking
Exposed children first appeared more likely to receive a neurodevelopmental diagnosis. That gap disappeared after the analysis accounted for the child's sex and birth year, and the mother's age, country of birth, education, smoking in pregnancy, ADHD diagnosis and use of other medications. The University of Sydney says the raw difference was largely explained by maternal ADHD, other maternal mental health conditions, smoking and other medication use.
That pattern matters because ADHD runs strongly in families. Sydney puts the estimated heritability of maternal ADHD at 64% to 75%, and general reviews place ADHD heritability at roughly 70% to 80%, with siblings of affected children three to four times more likely to develop it. A child of a mother with ADHD is therefore more likely to be diagnosed with ADHD whether or not the mother took a pill. The raw gap points to the mother's underlying condition, not the prescription, as the main driver. Because that inherited component is not something stopping a medication would be expected to change, the adjusted result carries the study's main practical message.
Neither exposure at any point in pregnancy nor exposure in the second or third trimester was linked to higher risk of any neurodevelopmental disorder, and the same held for methylphenidate, amphetamines and atomoxetine examined separately. The release also reports no association with behavioral and emotional disorders or with disorders of psychological and intellectual development.
Nordic Registers Link Pharmacy Fills to Diagnoses Through Personal ID Numbers
The scale comes from infrastructure. Each Nordic country maintains nationwide health registers and assigns every resident a unique personal identity number, which lets researchers link a mother's pharmacy record to her child's later diagnoses without new data collection. Five Nordic countries record drugs dispensed at pharmacies, and those records can be linked to health outcomes in other registries. Exposure in registry studies of this kind is typically based on dispensing records, which show what a mother collected, not confirmed doses taken.
Sydney's announcement draws a contrast with Australia: Scandinavian countries have long collected data to investigate medication safety in pregnancy, while Australian researchers face difficulties linking pregnancy records with national dispensing data. Bruno says improving access to linked health data would help answer medication-safety questions.
The design still has a known weak point. In observational studies of treatments, confounding by indication arises when the reasons a patient is treated also influence the outcome, and adjusting for known factors may reduce the problem but leaves it in place if an unknown factor is missed. Here the indication, maternal ADHD, is itself highly heritable, which is why the comparison group matters.
Mothers Who Stopped Before Conception Gave Consistent Results; Earlier Sibling Estimate Was Imprecise
Sydney says the results were consistent when mothers who continued treatment in pregnancy were compared with mothers who stopped before conceiving. Both groups have ADHD and sought treatment, so the comparison strips out part of the difference between women with and without the condition.
An earlier Swedish study, published in Molecular Psychiatry in 2025, used the same comparison. It followed 861,650 children born from 2008 to 2017, with 2,257 exposed in utero, and pooled its result with an earlier Danish study (3,155 exposed children in total) to reach a pooled hazard ratio of 1.00 for any neurodevelopmental disorder, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.83 to 1.20. Its sibling comparison returned an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.24 with an interval of 0.72 to 2.13, which the authors read as showing no association but which is too wide to rule out a modest increase or decrease. The authors said some subgroup analyses were underpowered and that evidence for amphetamines and atomoxetine rests on small samples.
Does This Study Settle Whether ADHD Medication Is Safe in Pregnancy?
No. It addresses neurodevelopmental diagnoses in children, and it is observational, so it cannot establish cause and effect. The authors list unmeasured factors such as ADHD severity or genetics as possible influences, and low exposure rates and small event counts limited firm conclusions for some conditions.
One wrinkle remains. Some elevated risks appeared for second- or third-trimester exposure to certain individual drugs, which the authors suggest may be due to chance given the small number of exposed children. The release does not name the drugs or outcomes, and neither it nor Sydney's announcement reports hazard ratios or confidence intervals, so readers wanting effect sizes need the paper itself.
Pregnancy complications are a separate question with separate evidence. A claims-based U.S. study of 24,200 stimulant-exposed pregnancies from 2013 to 2021 found that continuing stimulants into the second or third trimester, compared with stopping after the first, carried higher risk ratios for pre-eclampsia (1.33), placental abruption (1.78), preterm birth (1.86) and small-for-gestational-age infants (1.47), plus stillbirth (3.54) with a wide interval of 1.48 to 8.44. First-trimester-only exposure was not tied to higher risk for most outcomes and was linked to lower rates of spontaneous abortion, preterm birth and small-for-gestational-age infants. That study is also observational, and its limitations include confounding by indication, since women who continue treatment may have more severe ADHD or other conditions. The CDC separately reports that about 1 in 100 women took ADHD medicine during pregnancy, says early-pregnancy use is associated with some birth defects though the absolute risk is very low, and calls for more research.
Australian Dispensing to Women 25 to 44 Rose From 4 to 24 per 1,000 in Five Years
Use is climbing. Sydney reports that Australian dispensing of ADHD medication to women aged 25 to 44 grew from 4 to 24 per 1,000 people between 2018-19 and 2023-24. Bruno says the findings suggest concerns about a child's future neurodevelopmental health "should not be a reason to automatically stop ADHD medication during pregnancy."
Authors and RACGP Expert Back Individual Risk-Benefit Review Over Blanket Rules
The authors write that concerns about future neurodevelopmental risk "do not justify discontinuation" of ADHD medication in pregnancy, and say the findings can support shared decisions between patients and clinicians, including when planning a pregnancy.
Associate Professor John Kramer chairs the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners' specific interests group on ADHD, ASD and neurodiversity, and weighs in on the findings. He calls it "new knowledge" that will have to be incorporated into recommendations. He advises careful risk-benefit assessment for every individual and a full review of non-drug measures such as sleep, diet, routine, exercise, counseling and extra support, and cautions against generalizing. Australia's current ADHD guidelines, issued in January 2026, contain no specific advice on managing ADHD in pregnancy and list it as an area needing more research.
A linked BMJ editorial (DOI 10.1136/bmj-2026-101506) is opinion and was not externally peer reviewed, unlike the research paper. The study was funded by the ADHD Research Network, Oslo University Hospital and the Research Council of Norway. Several co-authors report pharmaceutical-funded research or consultancy, paid to their institutions or as honoraria, and the article states these are unrelated to the submitted work.
For anyone pregnant or planning a pregnancy while taking ADHD medication, the evidence now includes more than 3.6 million Nordic births in which neurodevelopmental diagnoses did not rise after adjustment, and a clearer view of what is still open: later-trimester signals the authors attribute to chance, limited precision where exposed numbers are small, and a separate and mixed body of evidence on pregnancy complications. Whether to continue, switch or stop is a decision for a prescriber who can weigh symptom severity against those open questions.
This article is not medical advice. Anyone who is pregnant or planning a pregnancy should talk with a prescriber before changing or stopping any medication.
Frequently Asked Questions
Is ADHD medication safe to take during pregnancy?
This study answers only part of that question. It found no higher risk of autism, ADHD, intellectual disability or learning disorders in children after adjustment, but it is observational and does not address pregnancy complications. A U.S. claims study found higher risk ratios for several complications when stimulants continued into the second or third trimester, though it also cannot prove cause. A prescriber can weigh those findings against an individual's symptoms.
Did ADHD medication cause the higher raw diagnosis rates in exposed children?
The data do not support that reading. The raw gap was largely accounted for by maternal ADHD, other mental health conditions, smoking and other medication use, and results held in a comparison with mothers who stopped before conception. Heritability of maternal ADHD is estimated at 64% to 75%, so inherited liability is a plausible driver of the raw difference.
How reliable is an observational study of this kind?
It can show whether a link appears after adjustment, but it cannot prove the absence of cause and effect. Unmeasured factors such as ADHD severity or genetics could still shape results, and the authors note that low exposure rates limited conclusions for some conditions. The earlier Swedish study's sibling comparison, for example, produced an estimate with a wide interval of 0.72 to 2.13.
Which ADHD medications did the study cover?
Methylphenidate (sold as Ritalin and Concerta), amphetamines and atomoxetine. Methylphenidate made up 69% of exposure, amphetamines 20% and atomoxetine 7%.
