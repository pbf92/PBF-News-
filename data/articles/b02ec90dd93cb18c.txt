TITLE: Content creator name
OUTLET: PPC Land

Content creator name is a dimension in Amazon Ads unified reporting that labels each row of a report with the name of the creator whose channel carried the advertisement. Amazon's dimensions reference, as quoted by PPC Land on November 12, 2026, defines it as "the name of content creator on whose channel an ad is shown." The field exists because a growing share of the video inventory Amazon sells is not produced by studios or broadcasters but by individuals streaming live to their own audiences, most obviously on Twitch, which Amazon has owned since 2014. A report that says an ad ran on Twitch says little about what a viewer actually saw. A report that names the channel does.
What the field records
Content creator name is a categorical attribute, not a metric. Placed beside impressions, total cost or video completions, it splits each metric into one row per creator. A brand that bought Twitch inventory through Amazon's display, video and audio buying product - sold as Amazon DSP until September 29, 2026 and as DVA since - can see which channels absorbed its impressions and at what price.
The field returns a name, which is a display label. Amazon's published list of dimensions does not include a matching creator ID. Channel names can be changed by their owners.
As of November 2026, Amazon's dimensions reference could not be read in full outside the Amazon Ads console. No public Amazon page lists which inventory populates the field, how names are normalised, or what value appears when an impression runs on content with no identifiable creator. Those points remain unconfirmed.
Where it sits in a unified report
Unified reporting, which Amazon launched in beta on November 11, 2025 and made generally available on June 8, 2026, puts every Amazon ad product into one report builder. PPC Land counted 156 dimensions in 11 categories after Amazon updated its reference on November 11, 2026. Content creator name belongs to the Supply category, the largest block reserved for display, video and audio buying, where it sits in a content sub-group alongside content genre, content rating, content type, content title and content language. Nine live-event attributes form a further sub-group.
The same article counts 35 supply attributes, with its table marking 34 for DVA+, two for Sponsored Products and one each for Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Display. It does not mark products field by field for the content group, and its text, which says 32 apply to DVA+ alone, does not match its own table. The working assumption, consistent with the field's wording, is that content creator name applies to DVA and DVA+ campaigns. Amazon's reference says that when a dimension does not apply to an ad product, "its value appears as null." A mixed report broken down by creator will therefore show blanks for sponsored ads rows.
How the data reaches the report
On Amazon's own properties, the creator is known at the source. Twitch records which channel is live, which game or category the streamer has chosen, and the content classification labels creators must apply to sensitive streams. Amazon's Twitch brand safety guide says game ratings come from the Entertainment Software Rating Board (ESRB) and describes "content-level reporting, which provides impression-level detail of the content your ads appeared in." Content creator name surfaces the channel element of that detail as a reporting column.
On inventory bought from outside sellers through supply-side platforms (SSPs), Amazon depends on what the bid request carries. OpenRTB, the IAB Tech Lab protocol that governs those requests, has long included a Producer object, described in the 2.6 specification as the "producer of the content; not necessarily the publisher (e.g., syndication)." OpenRTB 2.6, released for public comment on December 9, 2021, added Network and Channel objects "to support CTV inventory description," according to the IAB Tech Lab announcement. The specification defines a channel as "the entity that curates a content library, or stream within a brand name for viewers," giving a local television station as its example. Those fields are optional, and sellers fill them unevenly. Whether Amazon maps any of them into content creator name is not documented.
A worked example
Consider an illustrative DVA campaign for a headset brand, spending US$30,000 on Twitch over a month and delivering 2.5 million impressions at a US$12.00 CPM (cost per thousand impressions). A breakdown by content creator name might show 340 channels. The top ten account for 1.1 million impressions; one channel streaming a mature-rated shooter takes 180,000 impressions on its own. Adding content rating and content title shows the game and rating behind each row. Whether the concentration is acceptable is a judgement for the brand; the field supplies the evidence for it.
Origin and evolution
Amazon bought Twitch in August 2014 for about US$970 million. For years Twitch ads were sold mostly through direct deals. On December 14, 2022, Amazon made Twitch display inventory available in Amazon DSP as open-auction supply, with 728x90 units below the video player and 300x250 units across browse pages.
Creator-specific selling followed. On May 30, 2025, Amazon Ads launched Twitch Creator Sponsorships, templated formats including channel skins and streamer readouts across 17 markets, which it said could be activated "in as little as two weeks," according to ChannelX. Krishan Patel, director of sales for EMEA at Amazon Ads, called it "a great way for brands to test advertising on Twitch for the first time."
Reporting moved in steps. On August 25, 2025, Amazon extended its Streaming TV content reports to Twitch, letting advertisers see "which Content Type, Rating and Title their Twitch ad impressions were delivered on," for impressions on or after June 16, 2025. That announcement named no creator field. In December 2025, Amazon added content exclusion categories for brand suitability covering Twitch and third-party web and mobile inventory in 43 markets. Content creator name appears in the dimensions reference of the unified reporting system, which renamed order to campaign and line item to ad group so that DVA rows could sit beside sponsored ads rows.
Why it matters
Creator inventory is where context is least predictable. A broadcaster schedules programmes in advance; a streamer decides what to play, and say, minute by minute. Brand suitability controls that work at the level of a domain or an app treat every channel on a platform as one environment. On Twitch, Amazon's guide says DoubleVerify and Integral Ad Science are accredited for invalid traffic, viewability and domain- or app-level brand safety. A creator-level report fills part of the space between that and what a viewer actually watched.
Scale gives the question weight. Streams Charts data covered by PPC Land put Twitch at 4.51 billion hours watched in the third quarter of 2026, 14.2% of tracked livestreaming hours. Amazon Ads, in its June 9, 2026 TwitchCon recap, claimed Twitch "commands 60% of the global livestreaming landscape" and said Twitch combined with Prime Video adds 74% incremental reach. The two shares cannot both describe the same market; Amazon has not published its method.
Limitations and disputes
The first limitation is coverage. Amazon has not said which inventory populates the field, and Prime Video, IMDb and most third-party sites have no "creator" in the Twitch sense. On those rows the field is likely to be empty or to carry a studio or channel name, but that is an inference.
The second is identity. A name without an ID breaks when a streamer rebrands, and Amazon has not published how it handles renamed or duplicate channels. Year-on-year comparisons by creator may need manual matching.
The third is control. A report shows where ads ran; it does not, on its own, stop them running there. Amazon's public documentation describes inventory tiers and content exclusion categories for Twitch, applied "on a best-effort basis," but no creator-level exclusion list in DVA. Sponsored Products, by contrast, lets advertisers exclude individual creators from its creator placements.
The fourth is independence. Amazon owns Twitch, sells its inventory and reports on its delivery. On August 31, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission and 22 states sued Amazon over alleged undisclosed auction surcharges, alleging advertisers paid their full bid 79% of the time; Amazon denies the claims. The case does not concern creator reporting, but it frames how much buyers rely on Amazon's own numbers.
Commonly confused terms
Content title. Names the programme, film or game being shown. On Twitch, the title and the creator are separate facts: one streamer plays many games, and one game is streamed by thousands of channels.
Publisher name and publisher owner. Identify the site or app operator selling the inventory, such as Twitch itself. Content creator name sits one level below, inside the publisher.
Creator placements in Sponsored Products. A separate programme in which members of the Amazon Influencer Program feature advertised products in their own content. Amazon began serving Sponsored Products ads through creator placements on August 10, 2026, reported through the placement report rather than this dimension.
OpenRTB producer and channel. Protocol fields that describe who made content and which channel carried it. They are inputs that sellers may send; content creator name is Amazon's reporting output.
Recent developments
On November 11, 2026, Amazon updated its unified reporting dimensions reference, the published source of the field's definition. A day later, Amazon set the end of legacy reporting: old report pages go read-only on December 17, 2026 and close on January 31, 2027, after which unmigrated reports will be deleted. Creator activity across Amazon has widened in parallel, from Sponsored Products creator placements in August to Amazon products becoming taggable by YouTube creators later the same month.
