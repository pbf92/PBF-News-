Anthropic's Unreleased Claude Model Surfaces in User Tests, Turning Code Into Animation
Anthropic appears to be quietly testing a more capable version of its flagship Claude model, and the evidence is showing up less in benchmark charts than in animated films, interactive dashboards and 3D games built entirely from code.
Users who say they are subscribed to Opus 5.5 have reported that their outputs suddenly look and behave differently, a shift that TestingCatalog flagged on Nov. 25 as a possible staged rollout. The AI community has taken to calling the unreleased version Claude Opus 5.6.
What makes the results unusual is that Claude is a language model. It cannot generate video frames the way dedicated video tools do. Instead, the animations circulating online were produced by Claude writing code, from character movement and camera cuts to lighting changes and background music, which a browser then renders in real time.
The most widely shared example is a presentation titled "The Last Boring Slide." It opens like an ordinary deck and then pulls the viewer into a three-dimensional scene, with flat charts gaining depth and slide transitions staged like film cuts. The entire file runs to roughly 5 megabytes across 13 scenes and renders live in a browser. A post sharing it on X has drawn more than 7 million views and close to 30,000 likes.
Other demonstrations include a 65-second hand-drawn-style short about a red fox in a sleeping library, rendered from 1,560 frames of Python code, and an 82-second 3D sequence in which a rider crosses a market on the back of a giant pelican before flying into a lantern-lit sky. That piece contains 159 figures and six kinds of animals, with lantern sway and steam above a teacup synced to music. Its author says no external images or prebuilt 3D models were used, and the whole scene fits in a single HTML file powered by the Three.js graphics library.
One creator assembled six AI agents, each handling cityscapes, characters, sound or effects, to produce a 78-second fight scene. Another built a first-person shooter set on a storm-battered offshore platform in about three hours.

The aesthetic gap may matter more than raw capability. When the same prompt was given to Opus 5.5 and the suspected new model for a front-end developer's portfolio site, the newer version organized the design around the CSS box model, the building block of front-end layout, placing a floating three-dimensional box on the landing page and turning the skills section into a pressable keyboard. Side-by-side comparisons of a Chinese-style floating island produced three markedly different worlds from the same brief.
That distinction is central to how Anthropic sells its models. The company has been pushing Claude beyond conversation into production tools, and its November release cycle added a native spreadsheet, shared whiteboards, meeting notes and a personal-finance feature that reads bank transactions without moving money. Those products depend on the model reliably generating working interfaces, not just plausible text.
The stakes extend to Anthropic's competitive position. Rival labs have emphasized video and image generation as separate products, while Anthropic has argued that coding ability is the path to general-purpose software creation. If a model can turn a written brief into an interactive 3D scene or a functioning application, the boundary between a chat assistant and a design tool narrows considerably.
Anthropic has not confirmed the existence of Opus 5.6 or described what changed between versions. The company's published roadmap for November centers on Fable 5.2, which it bills as up to 30 percent faster than its predecessor, alongside beta features including Claude Sheets, which supports more than 250 functions and 500,000 populated cells, and Claude Personal Finance, which connects to bank accounts through Plaid with read-only access to as much as two years of transactions.
For developers and investors tracking the AI model race, the lesson from the past week is that capability gains are increasingly judged on output quality rather than benchmark scores. A model that writes better code produces better-looking software, and the difference is visible to people who have never opened an editor. Whether the suspected upgrade reaches general availability, and at what price, remains the open question.
