TITLE: Jesus Christ would be criminalised under Northern Ireland conversion therapy bill, minister warns
OUTLET: Premier Christian News

A church minister from Northern Ireland has told politicians he believes Jesus Christ would be criminalised under a proposed law to ban "conversion therapy".
The Conversion Practices (Criminalisation) Bill would outlaw actions “with the intention that the sexual orientation or gender identity of the victim would be suppressed”.
Spokespeople from Protestant Churches spoke on fears the new law would be weaponised against the Christians.
Public affairs officer of the Presbyterian Church in Ireland, Dr Rebecca Stevenson, voiced concerns about a clause which criminalises causing "distress".
Dr Stevenson said: “We are concerned that these provisions could be weaponised through complaints against ministers and church workers in respect of their preaching and teaching.
"Even where no prosecution followed, this could have a significant chilling effect. This is of great significance to Presbyterians. Preaching is central to Presbyterian worship, and Bible teaching plays a significant role in Bible studies, youth groups, Sunday schools, and in the home.
"The bill places limits on the freedom to conduct preaching and teaching, and consequently on the freedom of hearers to accept it.”
There would be no defence to argue that a person had consented to such a “conversion practice", because the bill says that an individual cannot give informed consent due to power imbalances. The new law would be punishable by up to two years imprisonment and a fine.
A minister from the smaller Free Presbyterian Church argued that the law "would actually criminalise the Son of God himself if he lived in Northern Ireland".
Rt Rev Mike Darbandi, of Six:Eight Church belonging to the Church of Ireland, revealed that he had sought counselling due to same-sex attraction.
He warned that the law could create a taboo where other Christians, like himself, would be scared to speak to their church like he did, and that churches would be scared to comment on sexuality for fear of criminal punishment.
"I speak today as both someone who has wrestled personally with attraction towards both sexes and my Christian faith, and as a member of clergy who has spent walking years walking alongside many others trying to do the same,” Rev Darbandi said.
"Over the years counselling, talking therapy, pastoral care, prayer, and honest conversations with trusted friends had been among the most life-giving experiences of my life. They helped me understand my sexuality, examine what I believe, make my own decisions, and work out how I wanted to live.
“Sexuality can already feel like a taboo subject. This bill will only serve to vastly increase that fear and encourage further an atmosphere of silence and isolation. This bill risks making the safest response: ‘I care about you, but I cannot risk talking to you about this’. This will not protect vulnerable people. It will only isolate them.”
The Democratic Unionist Party, Ulster Unionist Party and Traditional Unionist Voice oppose the proposed law. The bill has been moved by Eóin Tennyson MLA, deputy leader of the cross-community Alliance Party and member for Upper Bann.
Tennyson, who is openly gay, said during a September debate that "conversion practices" are "cruel acts" and are not a "relic of some distant past". He believes the bill seeks to ensure people aren't "shamed and humiliated because of who they are or who they love".
In England and Wales, the proposed Conversion Practices Bill is throwing up similar questions over freedom of religion.
