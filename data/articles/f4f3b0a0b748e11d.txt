TITLE: Review: ‘The Chosen’ Season 6
OUTLET: Christianity Today

It wouldn’t be fair to say that The Chosen has never dealt with the supernatural. From the beginning, the hit television series has shown Jesus (played by Jonathan Roumie) performing healings and other miracles.
But it would be fair to say that, until now, The Chosen has largely avoided dealing with the supernatural realm. In its early Christmas episodes, characters talked about angels, but we never actually saw any. The show has never depicted (or even alluded to) the revelation of the Trinity at Jesus’ baptism, the temptation by Satan in the wilderness, or the Transfiguration, the relevance of which series creator Dallas Jenkins famously questioned when he asked on Facebook, “What purpose would it serve our story?”
But eventually, the series was going to have to go there. Eventually, it was going to have to deal with the death and resurrection of Jesus—and that, in turn, meant it was going to have to deal with the glorified spiritual body of Jesus and the angels at the empty tomb, and maybe with the angels at the Ascension as well.
The series hasn’t gotten to that part yet. But season 6 (the first four episodes are out now on Prime Video), which follows the eight hours or so between the arrest and the crucifixion of Jesus, finally gives us our first good look at the angels in this universe—and they’re not exactly what previous episodes led us to expect.
For one thing, they’re dark and monochromatic, and they don’t throw off the colorful lights that we saw in the Christmas episodes. For another, these angels never speak, not even when the Bible gives them dialogue. In previous episodes, people discussed what the angels had said to them. As far as this season is concerned, the angels communicate only through hand gestures and tilts of the head.
More puzzlingly, the angels are introduced running and leaping across the fields, racing to get to Gethsemane before Jesus is betrayed (why don’t they just appear there the way angels often appear and disappear in the Bible?)—and then, just before the betrayal itself, Jesus, the only person who can see them, gives them a shake of the head and basically tells them not to intervene.
This raises all sorts of questions. For example, Luke’s Gospel says an angel appeared to Jesus and “strengthened” him while he was praying in Gethsemane (22:43), but The Chosen didn’t depict that in season 5. So why are the angels suddenly showing up now? Soon after the angels arrive, Jesus tells the disciples he could ask his Father to “send” legions of angels to come to his defense—but it looks like they’ve already been sent. Did the Father give them one order, only for the Son to countermand it? Or did the angels rush to action without getting authorization first, like Peter when he swung his sword?
In the November 14 livestream that launched the season premiere, Jenkins admitted that some of these questions were valid, but he added, “The main reason why we showed it this way is because it was cool.”
And that, in a nutshell, is the tension that runs through The Chosen. The show has pitched itself as a dramatization of the Gospels that gives its audience the “authentic” Jesus, and it is on this basis that it solicits the tax-deductible donations that keep the show funded. But the series is also frequently motivated by what would be “cool”—by what would please the fans or keep them on their toes.
As its budget and fanbase have grown, The Chosen itself has changed from the gritty, earthbound drama that it was in the first couple of seasons—when it was modeled after shows like Friday Night Lights and The Wire—to something that feels more and more like a Marvel series. Fans get excited by the appearance of anonymous new characters and speculate about which figures from the Bible they might be. Characters who’ve been absent for years are reintroduced with surprise end-of-episode reveals. Show-stopping miracles are followed by quippy Whedon-esque punchlines. And now, this season lays the groundwork for The Chosen’s “shared cinematic universe.”
Thankfully, with all this change, there has also been growth, and this season doesn’t shy away from the seriousness of what follows Jesus’ arrest. Jesus himself is largely silent while his accusers and defenders argue around him, and that gives some of the supporting actors a chance to really stand out. Luke Dimyan, in particular, has never been better; as Judas, his character’s reasons for betraying Jesus—a confused, passionate, increasingly frustrated mix of hopes, doubts, and rationalizations—are basically the invention of the show’s writers, but he absolutely sells them as Judas spirals toward his own self-destruction.
Likewise, the flogging of Jesus is a truly painful affair, and it’s striking to see Roumie not just emotionally drained—his Jesus has always been deeply emotional—but so physically weakened. The scene in which Jesus is mocked by Herod Antipas (Paul Ben-Victor) gets an extra layer of emotional complexity when Herod uses the occasion to embarrass his wife Herodias (Shereen Khan), possibly as payback for her role in making him kill John the Baptist. Even Pontius Pilate (Andrew James Allen)—who comes across all too often as a pampered, petulant adolescent, not least when he washes his hands of Jesus’ death—gets a moment of real stillness when he speaks to Jesus for the first time.
Like season 5, which devoted eight episodes to the five days before Jesus’ arrest, season 6 takes its time going through the hours leading up to his death. It uses this opportunity to integrate the Gospels in interesting ways. Most notably, The Chosen harmonizes the Gospels’ very different accounts of the trial and interrogation of Jesus by treating them as separate incidents and giving each one its own episode, though it moves some of the details around.
First, Jesus is interrogated by the former high priest Annas (Shaun Duke), as per John’s Gospel. Then he is tried at a secret hearing before sunrise, as per Matthew and Mark—and in The Chosen, this is an unofficial meeting that only the elders hostile to Jesus have been invited to. And then, Jesus is tried before the Sanhedrin after sunrise, as per Luke’s Gospel. This last trial is supposed to be the official trial, but when Nicodemus (Erick Avari) and Yussif (Ivan Jasso) arrive to mount their defense of Jesus, they learn that he has already been condemned and the Sanhedrin is expected to rubber-stamp the secret hearing’s verdict.
The show’s creative blending and reworking of Bible stories has always been one of its strengths. Alas, one of its weaknesses remains its dialogue, which is as full of overexplanation as ever. Herod, for example, can’t just make fun of Jesus like the Gospel says he did; first he has to have a private conversation with someone to explain precisely why he’s going to make fun of Jesus. And in this case, the dialogue actually gets the biblical account wrong. Luke 23:12 says Pilate and Herod became friends because Pilate kept Herod in the loop, but the Herod of the series resents the fact that Pilate sent Jesus to him.
For all its innovations, The Chosen often has moments that feel like callbacks to earlier Bible movies. Jesus is beaten so badly that his right eye swells up, just like Jim Caviezel’s in The Passion of the Christ. One person receives a message from God through moving silhouettes on a wall, just like the animated hieroglyphics in The Prince of Egypt. And when Herod asks Jesus to perform a miracle, he jokes that Jesus could cure him of his baldness, which is basically straight out of Monty Python’s Life of Brian. (If you’ve seen the trailer for The Chosen: The Three Kings, a Christmas special coming to Fox on December 14, then you know we’ll get to see a younger version of Herod with a full head of hair soon enough.)
The biggest callback comes in the final episode, as Jesus carries his crossbeam through Jerusalem. We suddenly see him walking not through the Jerusalem set in Utah that the show has been using since season 2, but the Italian city of Matera, which has been featured in many Bible movies over the past 60-plus years. It’s as if the characters have walked into a completely different movie. It makes you wonder how the upcoming film, The Chosen Crucifixion, will compare to Mel Gibson’s The Resurrection of the Christ: Part One (also shot in Matera!) when they come to theaters just a couple months apart next year.
But even as the series moves towards the pivotal, climactic moment of Jesus’ death, it throws a few curveballs into the mix. Major plot twists are introduced in the last two episodes (episode 5 will be released on November 29, and episode 6 on December 6) that are fan-servicing and completely fictitious. I can only assume they’ll distract from the Crucifixion movie coming this spring, either because they’ll be part of the movie and draw attention away from the Crucifixion itself, or because they won’t be in the movie and fans will be left wondering how they will resolve when season 7 comes out in 2028.
And so the tension continues, between a show that is faithful to the Gospels and a show that pumps up the drama for drama’s sake. The Chosen has always had difficulty with mystery and transcendence. It has always veered toward action scenes, fictitious context, and dialogue that explains what Jesus “really” meant. Hopefully it can find its way there in time for the story’s spiritual climax.
Peter T. Chattaway is a film critic with a special interest in Bible movies.
