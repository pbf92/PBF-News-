TITLE: More carbon, but not more earthworms
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Solitary oaks in traditional Romanian wood pastures create islands of carbon-rich soil around their trunks. However, a research team led by the Universities of Göttingen and Hildesheim found this does not mean more earthworms. In traditional wood pastures in Romania, the topsoil near the trunk held about a third more organic carbon and a quarter more nitrogen than the open grassland, yet earthworm numbers were no higher. As such trees decline across Europe, it is important to understand what they contribute to the soil. The results were published in the journal Agroforestry Systems.
The researchers studied 30 solitary oaks in traditional wood pastures, with trunks up to 1.75 metres in diameter. At each tree, they took samples at four distances from the trunk: 1 and 3 metres (under the tree crown), 7 metres (at the edge of the crown) and 24 metres (in the open grassland). At each point, they analysed the soil to 45 centimetres and hand-sorted earthworms from a soil block dug at that spot, comparing conditions near the trees with those in the open grassland.
Carbon enrichment was confined to the topsoil near the trunk; by the edge of the crown, carbon levels had fallen back to those of the open grassland. Earthworms help maintain healthy soils by breaking down organic matter and recycling nutrients. On average, the researchers found more earthworms in the open grassland than near the trunks, but this difference was not statistically significant. "We expected more carbon to mean more food, and so more earthworms," says Sachin Bhattarai, first author and PhD researcher at the Universities of Hildesheim and Göttingen. "For earthworms, the amount of carbon in the soil does not seem to be the factor that affects their ability to flourish in the environment”. Understanding these relationships is important as traditional wood pastures decline across Europe. Future research could examine what earthworms feed on and how tree and grass litter decompose in these landscapes.
The study received funding from the Dr Musat V. Bodnarescu Foundation.
