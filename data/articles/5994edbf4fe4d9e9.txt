TITLE: Continuous Passive Motion Fails to Improve Knee Extension After Knee Arthroplasty
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Routine continuous passive motion therapy does not significantly improve the restoration of knee extension following uncomplicated primary total knee arthroplasty, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis published in the Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research. The analysis evaluated eleven randomized controlled trials encompassing 1,200 patients to determine whether mechanical passive movement provides measurable therapeutic value beyond conventional rehabilitation protocols.
Continuous passive motion devices have seen widespread clinical application aimed at enhancing postoperative recovery after knee replacement procedures. However, the study authors observed that previous systematic reviews predominantly concentrated on knee flexion angles or cumulative range of motion metrics. While restoring complete knee extension is critical for normal gait mechanics, quadriceps muscle function, and overall long-term physical recovery, its trajectory under mechanical passive motion remained poorly defined in prior clinical evaluations.
To resolve this uncertainty, researchers conducted a comprehensive review adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, with prospective registration in the PROSPERO database under identifier CRD420251242151. The investigators systematically queried PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, the Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and the Wanfang Database from their inception dates through May 18, 2026, applying no language restrictions. Eligible trials compared continuous passive motion protocols, implemented with or without conventional rehabilitation programs, directly against conventional rehabilitation alone in patients recovering from primary total knee arthroplasty.
The primary outcome assessed across the pooled trials was postoperative knee extension deficit. Random-effects meta-analyses categorized by temporal follow-up revealed no statistically significant differences in knee extension between patients receiving continuous passive motion and those receiving conventional rehabilitation alone. During early follow-up under six weeks, the pooled mean difference was -0.28 degrees with a 95 percent confidence interval ranging from -0.69 to 0.12 degrees. Mid-term evaluations between six weeks and six months showed a mean difference of -0.16 degrees with a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.95 to 0.64 degrees, while long-term assessments past six months yielded a mean difference of -0.64 degrees with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning -1.39 to 0.12 degrees.
Although the intervention yielded statistically significant increases in knee flexion at the mid-term mark, showing a mean difference of 1.65 degrees with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.06 to 3.23 degrees, and at long-term follow-up, with a mean difference of 3.94 degrees and a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.66 to 6.23 degrees, these modest mechanical gains failed to generate broader clinical advantages. Secondary evaluations demonstrated no corresponding benefits in patient-reported pain scores measured on the visual analogue scale, functional scores assessed through the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index, incidence of manipulation under anesthesia, postoperative complication rates, or overall length of hospital stay.
Methodological quality across the eleven included trials was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. To verify the stability of the primary and secondary effect estimates, the investigators conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. These iterative analyses confirmed that the pooled findings remained statistically robust, even when individual influential studies were removed and statistical heterogeneity was reduced, reinforcing the consistency of the observed outcomes across the analyzed literature.
Based on the synthesis of available randomized trials, the authors concluded that the routine application of continuous passive motion following uncomplicated primary total knee arthroplasty provides limited clinical utility beyond standard contemporary physical therapy regimens. Because modest improvements in passive joint flexion do not translate into enhanced functional capability or superior patient-reported recovery, the researchers suggest that postoperative rehabilitation programs should prioritize strategies that actively develop quadriceps activation, neuromuscular coordination, and functional recovery over passive movement. The authors also noted that future high-quality randomized controlled trials with standardized continuous passive motion protocols will be necessary to determine whether specific patient subgroups might still achieve meaningful therapeutic gains from the technology.
