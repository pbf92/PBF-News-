TITLE: Meta explores “extend to CTV” buying
OUTLET: MarkHub24

Meta is reportedly exploring an “extend to CTV” buying option that would let advertisers using Facebook and Instagram extend campaigns into connected-TV advertising through Meta’s existing ad-buying interface. The report describes early discussions with TV manufacturers and free ad-supported streaming-TV platforms; it is not an announced or confirmed Meta product.
What “extend to CTV” means
Connected TV, or CTV, generally refers to television content delivered through internet-connected devices such as smart TVs, streaming sticks, game consoles, and set-top boxes. The reported proposal would add a CTV extension to Meta’s ad-buying platform, allowing a Facebook or Instagram advertiser to buy streaming-TV inventory alongside—or as an extension of—their existing social campaigns.
The core idea is not that Meta would necessarily own every TV screen or streaming service. Instead, it would act as a front door for advertisers: they could use familiar Meta campaign tools, audiences, and measurement systems while accessing CTV inventory supplied by participating TV original-equipment manufacturers and FAST platforms. FAST stands for “free ad-supported streaming TV,” a category that includes free streaming channels and services funded by advertising.
What has actually been reported
The claim originates with Mike Shields’s Next in Media newsletter, published on November 17, 2026. Shields wrote that, according to sources familiar with the discussions, Meta is “actively talking” with TV OEMs and various FAST platforms about adding an “extend to CTV” option to its existing ad-buying platform.
Ad Exchanger repeated the report the following day, framing it as a possible route for Meta to enter the living room more substantially. It also emphasized that Meta has already been testing Instagram for TV, built around longer-form creator content, while the reported CTV ad-buying discussions concern a separate advertising opportunity.
The important distinction is between a report and a launch. No Meta announcement, product name, launch date, pricing model, named OEM partner, or named FAST-platform partner has been publicly confirmed in the available reporting. Shields explicitly described the plan as something Meta is discussing, not something it has launched.
Why Meta would pursue it
Meta’s advertising business is built around performance marketing: advertisers can target audiences, optimize campaigns toward actions such as purchases or sign-ups, and measure results through Meta’s attribution tools. Extending that model to CTV would let Meta bring a large base of existing Facebook and Instagram advertisers into television advertising without requiring them to learn an entirely separate buying system.
Shields describes the potential proposition as bringing “millions” of Facebook and Instagram advertisers to TV through the platform they already use, then connecting TV campaigns to outcomes through Meta’s measurement and attribution tools. That could make CTV more accessible to smaller businesses that have historically found television advertising complicated, expensive, or difficult to measure.
The strategic logic is also competitive. Shields argues that Meta may want a share of the roughly $60 billion U.S. TV-advertising market and may be responding to YouTube’s growing role in TV viewing; he cites a 15.8% share of TV viewing for YouTube. These figures are presented as context in his analysis, rather than as confirmed details of Meta’s internal plans.
What Meta would need from partners
For the concept to work, TV manufacturers and FAST platforms would need to make inventory available to Meta. Shields notes that various OEMs would have to be willing to give Meta access to CTV inventory, which he says is currently plentiful.
That creates a practical business question: TV platforms and streaming services may want Meta’s advertiser demand, but they may also be cautious about giving a major platform access to their inventory, audience data, and commercial relationships. The report does not identify which companies are involved, what commercial terms are being discussed, or whether any agreement is close.
Meta would also need to integrate CTV inventory, targeting, delivery, reporting, and measurement into its ad platform. The reported idea suggests Meta would use its own identity, targeting, and attribution capabilities, but the technical and privacy-related details have not been publicly specified.
The measurement opportunity
A major attraction for advertisers would be Meta’s ability to connect TV exposure with downstream outcomes. In social advertising, Meta can measure actions such as website visits, app installs, purchases, and conversions within its own ecosystem. If similar measurement could be applied to CTV campaigns, advertisers could potentially see whether TV exposure contributed to business results.
That is especially relevant because CTV advertising has often been viewed as more brand-oriented and harder to connect directly to sales than social or search advertising. The reported proposal would therefore position Meta not merely as another CTV inventory seller, but as a performance-oriented layer across TV advertising.
However, measurement across different TV environments is not automatically simple. Advertisers would still need clarity on frequency capping, household-level targeting, cross-device identity, viewability, fraud prevention, and how Meta would attribute conversions when a TV ad is seen on one device and a purchase occurs on another. Those issues are implied by the broader CTV-advertising challenge discussed in the report, but the reporting does not claim Meta has solved them.
Why it may not be easy
Industry comments included in Shields’s analysis suggest that CTV is not necessarily “plug-and-play” for social advertisers. Dan Larkman, CEO and founder of performance-CTV company Keynes, told Shields that marketers often want hands-on control and that advertiser churn in CTV can be high. He also argued that Meta would need to work with many third parties if it does not own the underlying TV properties.
That points to a central limitation: Meta’s strength is its enormous advertiser base and performance-marketing technology, but it does not own the full TV distribution stack. Unlike a company that controls a streaming service, TV operating system, or large CTV inventory pool, Meta would depend on partners to supply inventory and grant access.
Ross Benes, an analyst at eMarketer, similarly suggested that Meta’s best route into TV may be targeting and audience extension rather than trying to make Instagram itself a major TV destination. He noted that YouTube Shorts works on TV partly because viewers already use the YouTube app heavily on television, whereas consumers may be less likely to download a separate app solely for Reels.
The broader Meta TV strategy
The reported “extend to CTV” option appears to be one part of a wider question: how Meta can become more relevant on television. Shields outlines two broad paths—scaling Instagram and Reels on CTV through distribution deals or acquisitions, and bringing Meta’s identity, targeting, and attribution data into TV advertising. He suggests Meta could pursue both.
Meta has reportedly been testing Instagram for TV with longer-form creator content. Ad Exchanger describes this as an existing test, while the “extend to CTV” discussions concern advertising infrastructure rather than the consumer-facing Instagram TV experience.
Other possibilities discussed in the analysis include Meta paying for distribution of Instagram or creator content, building hardware, acquiring a TV or ad-tech company, forming data partnerships with supply-side platforms, or following an Amazon-like approach of enabling its data to target audiences across other platforms. These are analytical scenarios and industry speculation, not confirmed Meta plans.
What advertisers should take from it
For advertisers, the most concrete takeaway is that Meta may be preparing to make CTV buying more self-serve and more closely tied to social campaign workflows. If realized, that could lower the barrier for businesses already comfortable buying Facebook and Instagram ads to test streaming-TV campaigns.
But the appropriate response is caution. The report is based on unnamed sources, Meta has not confirmed the initiative, and no commercial terms or launch timeline are public. Advertisers should therefore treat “extend to CTV” as a potential development worth monitoring, not as an available Meta advertising product.
