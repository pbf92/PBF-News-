TITLE: Severe Traumatic Brain Injury Review Evaluates Sedative Strategies and Clinical Outcomes
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

In neurocritical care units treating patients with mechanical trauma, controlling raised intracranial pressure remains the central actionable target for preventing secondary neuronal destruction and improving long-term recovery. Severe traumatic brain injury, clinically defined by a post-resuscitation Glasgow Coma Scale score ranging between 3 and 8 following mechanical impact, affects millions of individuals globally each year and accounts for roughly 30 percent of all injury-related deaths worldwide. Following the primary mechanical insult of direct tissue damage, an intricate cascade of secondary injury pathways unfolds across hours and days, involving cerebral edema, impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation, excitotoxicity, and severe neuroinflammation. Clinical investigations consistently demonstrate that sustained intracranial pressure levels exceeding 20 millimeters of mercury are independently associated with a two- to three-fold increase in the risk of mortality and severe neurological disability. To counter these detrimental pathways, pharmacological sedation serves as a cornerstone of standard intensive care management, intended to reduce cerebral metabolic stress, blunt sympathetic hyperactivity, and facilitate mechanical ventilation.
To evaluate the comparative physiological efficacy and safety profiles of commonly utilized sedatives, neurosurgeons Xiaoyu Yang, Tong Sun, Yuekang Zhang, Junwen Guan, and Yanming Ren from the Department of Neurosurgery at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, conducted a comprehensive narrative review of clinical and translational evidence published through mid-September 2026. The researchers reviewed international medical databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar, synthesizing findings from randomized controlled clinical trials, prospective observational cohorts, experimental animal models, and authoritative clinical guidelines. The authors emphasized that the primary rationale for administering sedative agents lies in interrupting maladaptive systemic and cerebral responses to routine intensive care interventions, such as endotracheal suctioning, patient positioning, and surgical procedures. In unmedicated patients, noxious stimulation triggers robust sympathoadrenal surges of circulating catecholamines, systemic hypertension, and increases in cerebral blood flow that directly escalate intracranial pressure and worsen vasogenic edema when vascular autoregulation is impaired.
A critical neuroprotective mechanism conferred by sedative agents is the direct, dose-dependent suppression of the cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen. Because injured brain tissue exhibits acute mitochondrial vulnerability and limited cellular energy reserves following traumatic mechanical disruption, metabolic reduction helps balance cerebral oxygen delivery with consumption. Preclinical investigations reviewed by the authors indicate that intravenous hypnotics such as propofol and inhaled volatile anesthetics can suppress global and regional cerebral oxygen consumption by up to 50 percent, transitioning metabolically active neural tissue into a quiescent state that conserves adenosine triphosphate reserves needed for essential structural repair. Furthermore, continuous deep sedation, targeted to Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale scores between minus 4 and minus 5, frequently serves as a crucial pharmacotherapeutic bridge to surgical interventions such as decompressive craniectomy, or as a temporizing measure during acute intracranial pressure plateau waves when first-line medical therapies like head-of-bed elevation, cerebrospinal fluid drainage, and hyperosmolar infusions fail.
Among intravenous hypnotic options, propofol remains the most widely selected first-line sedative in modern neurotrauma protocols due to its rapid distribution half-life of 2 to 4 minutes, which allows intensive care clinicians to rapidly interrupt sedation to perform serial neurological examinations. Propofol acts mechanistically through the potentiation of gamma-aminobutyric acid type A receptors, promoting neuronal hyperpolarization and reducing metabolic demand. Multiple randomized trials have demonstrated that propofol outperforms benzodiazepines in suppressing acute intracranial pressure spikes and shortens the overall duration to mechanical extubation. However, these clinical trials have observed no statistically significant reduction in six-month neurological mortality, and clinical administration remains constrained by dose-dependent vasodilation, baroreflex blunting, and the life-threatening hazard of propofol infusion syndrome. Characterized by severe metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and fatal cardiac arrhythmias, this syndrome is predominantly linked to prolonged infusion rates exceeding 4 milligrams per kilogram per hour beyond 48 hours, mandating routine laboratory surveillance of serum lactate, creatine kinase, and triglycerides.
Midazolam presents an alternative gamma-aminobutyric acid agonist with an extended elimination duration between 2 and 6 hours, rendering it less adaptable for frequent clinical reassessment but hemodynamically stabler in patients vulnerable to sudden systemic hypotension. A meta-analysis of four randomized trials cited by the research team indicated that midazolam achieves intracranial pressure control comparable to propofol; however, its clinical utility is often limited by progressive drug accumulation, tachyphylaxis, and prolonged awakening times, particularly in polytrauma patients exhibiting concurrent hepatic impairment. Consequently, midazolam is generally reserved as a salvage option or short-term adjunct when propofol is strictly contraindicated. Meanwhile, the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonist ketamine, historically withheld in neurotrauma due to concerns that it might elevate intracranial pressure, has experienced clinical re-evaluation. Observational studies demonstrate that when ketamine is co-administered as a continuous infusion alongside background GABAergic agents in mechanically ventilated patients, it does not elevate intracranial pressure, while its sympathomimetic profile supports mean arterial pressure in hemodynamically unstable individuals, though it carries an increased relative risk of intermittent hypotensive episodes reported at 1.47 in meta-analyses.
Alpha-2 adrenoceptor agonists and ultra-short-acting barbiturates occupy distinct specialized niches in neurocritical care algorithms. Dexmedetomidine provides arousable, cooperative sedation with minimal respiratory depression by reducing central sympathetic outflow without uncoupling cerebral blood flow from metabolic demand. Despite preclinical suggestions of anti-neuroinflammatory actions, clinical guidelines restrict dexmedetomidine to an adjunctive role during prolonged or lighter sedation phases because its modest effect on intracranial pressure, extended elimination half-life, and propensity to cause dose-dependent bradycardia and systemic hypotension complicate acute intracranial pressure emergencies. Conversely, the barbiturate thiopental is positioned strictly as a late-stage rescue therapy for medically refractory intracranial hypertension. Thiopental profoundly suppresses both cerebral oxygen consumption and cerebral blood volume, but its implementation to induce barbiturate coma requires continuous electroencephalography targeting burst suppression. Systematic reviews highlight that thiopental carries severe risks of prolonged mechanical ventilation, drug-induced immunosuppression, and profound systemic hypotension requiring heavy vasopressor dependency, while offering no proven improvement in long-term functional recovery.
The review also detailed the emerging application of volatile inhaled anesthetics, such as sevoflurane and isoflurane, administered via specialized miniature vaporizers like the AnaConDa system in patients with refractory intracranial pressure elevations unresponsive to standard intravenous infusions. Volatile agents offer exceptionally rapid pulmonary elimination and continuous titratability monitored through end-tidal concentration measurements, preventing the tissue accumulation common to prolonged intravenous sedatives. In preclinical traumatic injury models, sevoflurane reduces biochemical markers of oxidative stress and improves post-injury behavioral metrics. However, the authors emphasize that volatile anesthetics possess complex cerebrovascular properties: while they suppress cerebral metabolic demand, concentrations at or exceeding 1 minimum alveolar concentration induce direct cerebral vasodilation, increasing cerebral blood flow and blood volume. In patients suffering from severely disrupted cerebral autoregulation, this vasodilatory mechanism can paradoxically elevate intracranial pressure while lowering systemic mean arterial pressure, causing cerebral perfusion pressure to collapse from both ends and restricting volatile anesthetic deployment to highly specialized units equipped with expert neurohemodynamic monitoring.
Effective analgesia represents an indispensable component of neurotrauma sedation, as unmitigated nociceptive input reliably triggers systemic arterial hypertension, psychomotor agitation, and sudden spikes in intracranial pressure. Continuous opioid administration remains the standard foundational analgesic therapy, with remifentanil and fentanyl favored for their predictable pharmacokinetic profiles and neutral impact on intracranial hemodynamics. Remifentanil, an ultra-short-acting mu-opioid receptor agonist metabolized by non-specific blood and tissue esterases, provides a key operational advantage by clearing within 5 to 10 minutes of cessation regardless of infusion duration, thereby allowing reliable neurological assessments without metabolic accumulation. Nevertheless, because prolonged continuous opioid administration fosters rapid pharmacodynamic tolerance and hyperalgesia, clinicians often require non-opioid analgesic adjuncts such as acetaminophen or intravenous lidocaine. Conversely, the authors note that morphine should be avoided in acute severe traumatic brain injury due to its histamine-releasing potential, which can induce systemic hypotension and cerebral vasodilation that exacerbates intracranial hypertension.
Transitioning from drug pharmacology to clinical deployment, the review underscores the critical limitations of conventional sedation titration based exclusively on subjective clinical scores like the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale. Because clinical examination findings are obscured by pharmacological suppression, contemporary neurocritical care increasingly relies on multimodal physiological monitoring. Clinicians target an intracranial pressure below 20 millimeters of mercury and a cerebral perfusion pressure between 60 and 70 millimeters of mercury, interpreting these thresholds alongside real-time indices of cerebrovascular autoregulation and continuous electroencephalography. Electroencephalographic monitoring proves vital for detecting non-convulsive electrographic seizures and titrating deep sedation to specific burst suppression ratios. Rather than relying on isolated numerical values from processed indices like the bispectral index, neurointensivists assess qualitative waveform transformations to track dynamic neurophysiological responses within the broader context of ongoing resuscitation goals.
