TITLE: Australia bans wagering ads for logged-out users from January 1, 2027
OUTLET: PPC Land

Australia's media regulator on November 12, 2026 published the first guidance on how streaming services, social networks, news sites and other online platforms can keep showing wagering advertising once a new prohibition takes effect on January 1, 2027. The 23-page document from the Australian Communications and Media Authority (ACMA), titled "Triple lock exception guidance" and dated November 2026, sets out the steps the regulator expects before a service may carry promotion for licensed betting operators: every person who sees the ads must be logged in to a registered account, must have had their age checked as 18 or over, and must be able to switch the ads off.
In Short
Australia will ban wagering ads on websites, apps, streaming and social media services from January 1, 2027, unless the service checks that viewers are adults, makes them log in and lets them opt out. The ACMA's new guidance explains what it will look for when deciding whether a platform did enough, and it leaves the choice of age-check technology to each provider. A service with no opt-out system in place on January 1, 2027 cannot rely on the exception and must stop showing wagering ads to every user unless another exception applies.
A prohibition with one way around it
The rules come from the Interactive Gambling Amendment (Gambling Reform) Act 2026, which the Parliament of Australia enacted on August 26, 2026. According to the guidance, the Act "introduces a new Part 7C containing restrictions on wagering advertising into the Interactive Gambling Act 2001 (Interactive Gambling Act), commencing on 1 January 2027."
Two sections carry the weight. According to the ACMA, sections 62P and 62Q "will prohibit online content service providers from providing wagering advertising content on their online content services, and from delivering such content to restricted users." The first prohibition sits at the level of the service. The second sits at the level of the individual.
Section 62H then defines who counts as a restricted user. According to the guidance, it is "an individual in Australia who either":
- "is under 18 years of age"
- "has not confirmed their age"
- "is not using a registered account in relation to the online content service (that is, the individual must be logged-in)"
- "has opted out from accessing or receiving wagering advertising content provided on the online content service, whether through a provider's opt-out mechanism or the Wagering Advertising Opt-Out Register (the register) (once commenced)."
The definition is wide. An adult who simply has not signed in is a restricted user. So is an adult who has an account but has never been asked for a date of birth.
The exit from the service-level ban is in subsection 62P(2). As the guidance sets it out, the prohibition "does not apply if the online content service provider takes reasonable steps to:
- prevent restricted users from accessing or receiving wagering advertising content; and
- ensure that individuals in Australia accessing or receiving wagering advertising content are doing so using a registered account in relation to the online content service; and
- ensure that individuals in Australia with a registered account in relation to the online content service may opt out of accessing or receiving wagering advertising content."
The ACMA calls these three conditions the triple lock exception. Subsection 62Q(2) offers a narrower version for the individual-level ban: it falls away if the provider "has taken reasonable steps to prevent restricted users from accessing or receiving wagering advertising content", which is the first limb alone.
The guidance is blunt about timing. "Online content service providers that do not have an opt-out system in place from 1 January 2027 will not meet the triple lock exception and, as such, will not be able to provide wagering advertising content on their service until they meet the requirements of this or other relevant exceptions," it states. Elsewhere it repeats the point: providers that are not compliant "must not deliver wagering advertising content to any of their users from the commencement of Part 7C of the Interactive Gambling Act, unless another relevant exception applies."
Which services are covered
The prohibition attaches to an "online content service", which subsection 10C(1) of the Interactive Gambling Act defines as a service that delivers content over an internet carriage service, or allows end-users to access content that way, and that "is provided to the public (whether on payment of a fee or otherwise)."
According to the guidance, four categories within the meaning of the Online Safety Act are taken to be online content services: on-demand program services, with subscription services (SVOD) and broadcaster services (BVOD) given as examples; social media services; relevant electronic services, which take in email, text messages, chat rooms and "online multiplayer video games"; and designated internet services, described as "any website or app that lets users access or receive online material via the internet, provided it is not already classified as a social media, messaging or gaming service."
The territorial test is light. A service falls outside the definition only if "none of the content on the service is accessible or delivered to one or more end-users in Australia." The ACMA adds that "end-user" carries its ordinary meaning and "is intended to have the broadest reach of coverage capturing Australian end users."
That reach is what makes the guidance relevant well beyond betting companies. Australian SVOD and BVOD services, global social platforms, sports highlights sites and news publishers all sit inside the category, and any of them that sells inventory to wagering operators now faces a choice: build the three locks or drop the category.
What counts as wagering advertising
Wagering advertising content, according to the guidance, "means any advertising, sponsorship or promotional content that relates to a licensed interactive wagering service." Under subsection 62C(2), content is caught if it "has the purpose or effect of, promoting the licensed interactive wagering service and is by, on behalf of or for the benefit of the licensed interactive wagering service provider (LIWSP)."
Two clarifications in the document have direct consequences for media owners. First, in-program content is covered. "Yes, all content that is advertising, sponsorship or promotional content that relates to a licensed interactive wagering service is considered to be 'wagering advertising content' unless a specific exemption applies," the guidance states. Second, payment is not the test. "The definition does not turn on whether content has been paid for but, rather, whether it is 'by, on behalf of or for the benefit of' an LIWSP," it says, while noting that the accidental conduct exception in section 62ZB may still apply.
The phrase "for the benefit of" is meant to keep ordinary conversation out. According to the ACMA, citing the revised explanatory memorandum, it is "intended to exclude private communications about wagering services between individuals, discussions on chat forums about wagering services and other similar user-generated content (where there is no involvement with a LIWSP)." The guidance gives an example of a person discussing their football team's chances on social media with reference to two betting operators, neither of which was involved; that post "is unlikely to be wagering advertising content." Incidental mentions are also excluded, "such as advertising a horse-racing event that is sponsored by a LIWSP or advertising a venue that offers wagering services."
Section 62D carves out further material, which the guidance lists as content relating solely to government or political matters, content appearing as part of ordinary business matters such as invoices or online profiles, content shown in premises owned or occupied by an LIWSP that are not used in conjunction with a live sporting event, and content "discouraging the use of wagering services."
Two routes that skip the triple lock
Not every service needs the three locks. According to the guidance, "Possible exceptions include simulcast content and dedicated wagering or racing services and content."
Simulcast content. Subsection 62P(1) does not apply to online radio or television simulcast services where a notice under section 62SB is in force. Those services follow "the alternative treatment prescribed in Division 3A." The ACMA says it is "proposing to publish a list of services or parts of services for which a notice has been provided," and under subsection 62SB(4) the simulcast provider must tell potential end-users that Division 3A applies before the arrangements start.
Dedicated wagering or racing services. A website or app of a licensed interactive wagering service provider, or one "that is wholly or principally dedicated to wagering or wholly or principally provides racing content", may display wagering advertising without satisfying either 62P(2) or 62Q(2). The exception works "at a service level only." The ACMA draws a firm line on social media: "the exception does not apply to social media profiles or pages of racing or wagering companies or organisations on a social media service, as these profiles or pages do not fall into the meaning of an online content service."
How the triple lock works, according to the guidance
Taken together, the guidance and the ACMA's November 12 announcement describe a sequence of nine stages for a provider that decides to keep carrying wagering advertising after January 1, 2027. The document frames these as reasonable steps the provider "might take", not as a fixed checklist.
- Establish coverage. The provider determines whether its service is an online content service under subsection 10C(1) and whether any content reaches end-users in Australia.
- Check for another exception. Simulcast services with a section 62SB notice in force and services dedicated to racing or wagering follow separate rules.
- Run and document a risk assessment. Because providers "bear the evidential burden" for the exception, the guidance says they "should ensure they have undertaken and documented a risk assessment process" that identifies "reasonably foreseeable risks", identifies the people who may be affected, assesses likelihood and severity, documents mitigation measures, and records the expected effectiveness of those measures and how long they are expected to work.
- Check ages (first limb). The provider takes reasonable steps to identify users under 18 and users who have not confirmed their age. Under section 62T it must offer "a reasonable alternative to government identification" for age checks. No wagering advertising may appear on a "kids" profile.
- Require a registered account (second limb). Users must establish an account and be logged in wherever wagering advertising may appear, through either a "global gate" or a "feature gate".
- Offer an opt-out (third limb). An account-based opt-out mechanism must be live from January 1, 2027, with prominent information about how to use it and a direct reminder "at least annually".
- Notify the ACMA. According to the announcement, "Providers seeking to rely on the exception should notify the ACMA, including so they can be kept directly informed about the development of the Wagering Advertising Opt-out Register."
- Keep records. Section 62ZD requires providers to retain sufficient records "for 12 months after the relevant wagering advertising content is displayed."
- Connect to the register once it starts. Providers must make users aware of the national register and check it "at least every 14 days or any other period prescribed by the register rules."
