TITLE: Two JAMA studies link blood marker to advanced CTE
OUTLET: streamlinefeed.co.ke

Two studies published in JAMA found that a blood marker developed for Alzheimer's disease was raised in former football players with advanced chronic traumatic encephalopathy, but neither establishes a test that can diagnose CTE in living patients.
That distinction is the whole story. CTE can currently be confirmed only at autopsy, so a reliable antemortem marker would reshape research and, eventually, care. The evidence so far comes from 32 brain donors across two small cohorts, most of them with late-stage disease.
What the two studies measured
A team led by Boston University researchers, working with Banner Sun Health Research Institute, tested stored plasma from 21 male former football players who later donated their brains. Eighteen had CTE, 16 of them at stages III or IV, and three had none.
Median concentrations of endogenously cleaved microtubule-binding region tau were 0.019 pg/mL in CTE cases against 0.001 pg/mL in those without. The highest readings, 0.082 pg/mL, came from the five men who also had Alzheimer's neuropathology. High-stage CTE without that copathology sat at 0.009 pg/mL.
Separately, Washington University researchers analysed samples from the University of California San Francisco brain bank, including 11 autopsy-confirmed CTE cases and 20 controls. Median plasma eMTBR-tau243 was 1.93 fmol/L in CTE against 0.29 in controls, while clinically diagnosed Alzheimer's cases reached 11.25 fmol/L.
The cohorts were narrow in ways that matter. All 21 Boston participants were men, 12 of the 18 CTE cases had played professionally, and the median age at blood draw was 68 for CTE cases against 49 for those without. Blood was taken a median of one year before death in the CTE group.
Where the signal fails
Discrimination depended heavily on stage, and the published confidence intervals show how little the early-stage result can carry.
Measurement limits compound this. In the Boston cohort, 10 of 21 samples fell below the assay's lower limit of quantification, including all three men without CTE, both early-stage cases and five stage III cases. Separation at the top of the range is therefore clearer than any ability to rule disease in or out at the bottom.
Specificity is the second problem. The Washington University study found elevations across mixed 3-repeat and 4-repeat tauopathies, including Alzheimer's and some genetic frontotemporal degenerations, but not in predominantly 3-repeat or 4-repeat diseases. The marker appears to track a class of tau aggregation rather than one diagnosis.
The verdict from outside the research teams
In an accompanying JAMA editorial, Carla Palleis and Günter Höglinger of Ludwig Maximilian University Munich wrote that the marker is "not a validated diagnostic test for CTE". They noted that an association with regional tau burden does not prove the assay detects the distinctive perivascular, sulcal pattern that defines the disease.
They also flagged selection: the Boston cohort was 21 men, all football players, with only three comparisons, drawn from research participation and brain donation. The Washington University paper discloses patents licensed to C2N Diagnostics, and one of its senior authors co-founded that company.
Clinical criteria already exist without resolving the problem. The editorial notes that traumatic encephalopathy syndrome criteria cannot confirm underlying pathology during life, and that tau PET tracers lack specificity for CTE. A third JAMA paper, covering 728 participants in Alzheimer's cohorts, supports the immunoassay used in the Boston study as a measure of tau burden.
Why it matters for former players
The pressure for a test is measurable. A separate study in JAMA Network Open estimated that 74.5% of former NFL players who died between 2008 and 2023 had CTE neuropathology, after adjusting for the fact that families of symptomatic players were about five times more likely to donate brains. Among 387 donated brains, 362 showed CTE.
Mass General Brigham, which announced that work in October, stressed the estimate applies only to deceased players and does not predict how many living former players are affected. It also noted CTE appears in under 1% of community brain bank donors.
For now the practical position is unchanged: no clinical blood test exists for CTE, and the researchers themselves call for larger, independent, prospectively collected cohorts. The next milestone is validation, not a launch.
