TITLE: My grandmother was a seal
OUTLET: Aeon

In October 1948, in Country Kerry, Ireland, Seán Ó Muircheartaigh met with Seosamh Ó Dálaigh to tell him about the woman who came from the sea. She had appeared, said Ó Muircheartaigh, one night on the magharees, those bright spits of sand that occasionally pop up amid the rain-lashed rocks of the Dingle peninsula. Alone on the beach, she settled herself on a rock and began combing her hair.
Also on the beach was a farmer, who – much to the surprise of the woman herself – would by daybreak become her husband. He’d come to gather seaweed, seeing her before she saw him. Beautiful as she was, the woman was also strange: the creature he saw stagger onto that Dingle beach was not a woman at all. It was a seal.
By the time Ó Muircheartaigh told Ó Dálaigh about the seal woman, he was already 80. He had been born in the apocalyptic aftermath of the Great Hunger that divested as much as a third of Ireland’s population to death or migration, and he had survived two great wars. Most of the disappeared were – like Ó Muircheartaigh himself – small farmers and native Irish, or Gaeilge, speakers. The famine had been not just a physical genocide: it had been a linguistic and cultural one, too.
Both Ó Muircheartaigh and Ó Dálaigh were veteran contributors to the Irish Folklore Commission: the former as a renowned storyteller; the latter, an equally renowned collector. The IFC is now the National Folklore Collection at University College Dublin – a behemoth of 3 million pages of texts and recordings. But in 1948, following eight centuries of colonialism, the loss of 99 per cent of the island’s forests, a staggering emigration, and the decline of the Irish language, the IFC was in a race against time and the fragility of memory. As it turned out, an awful lot of people had heard stories about seals that came to land as humans.
Nowadays, these shapeshifters are known as ‘selkies’. Selkie is a Scots term that came into widespread use via a Joan Baez cover, ‘Silkie’ (1961), of a traditional Scottish folk song. Yet the phenomenon it describes – of creatures who shapeshift between human and seal forms by shedding a skin – appears consistently in folklore spanning the Celtic world and beyond, even as far as Sweden. Before Baez insinuated the term into international parlance, selkies were identified in Irish and English by a constellation of descriptors. In Irish, the most common is mhaighdean mhara, or ‘sea maiden’, referring to selkies and mermaids. Even in English, many storytellers use ‘mermaid’ for both the phocine and the piscine. Sometimes the two are differentiated with context (fish tails, seal skins), but what matters more than phenotyping is that the mermaid or selkie is neither quite human nor animal. What defines the selkie is transformation.
Much of the folklore retains the quality of the best gossip, hyper-local and yet vague. Storytellers name names, offer precise locations for key scenes in the story. And several admit something startling: that they themselves are the descendants of human-seal pairings.
After the farmer spotted the woman on the beach, Ó Muircheartaigh said, he saw something else. A sealskin cloak lay beside her: her ticket to transformation and a return to the sea. Then he made a choice that would ricochet throughout the generations, and leave the selkie with no option but to follow him home. He stole the selkie’s cloak and secreted it away, thus securing her for a bride. These rough beginnings aside, the couple produced a brood of children and went about the gruelling lives of any impoverished Irish fishing family. The only thing disrupting the fantasy of a normal human wife, acquired in normal human ways, was the webbing between the children’s toes. That, and the fact that the woman never smiled, but kept looking out towards the sea.
What does it mean if your grandmother was a seal? The folklore attests that marriages between humans and selkies were common enough to generate a host of human-selkie lineages – Conneely, Gallagher, Dowd, O’Kane, O’Shea, MacDuffie, McPhee, Flaherty, Glavin – whose members were predisposed towards webbed digits and an ineffable longing for the sea. (‘My mother was one of them O’Sheas,’ the railway worker Séamas MacMathghamhna confided to a folklore collector in 1949, after explaining the selkie lineage of the O’Sheas.) The Celtic studies scholar Gregory Darwin has identified nearly 50 family groups whose members carry selkie blood.
Now is a good time to have a seal in the family tree. As our planet hurtles through climate catastrophe, everyone from environmental activists and TikTok influencers to academics and New Age pilgrims is turning towards animals-as-family rhetoric. Such gusto stems from a cultural exhaustion with extractionism and with narratives of domination and human exceptionalism that have dogged the culture and geopolitics of the Global North since the 19th century. Yet the naivety with which the zeitgeist tends to approach an expanded view of interspecies kinship presents its own problems. For one, it doesn’t quite ring true – and it is hard to fight for a worldview that you can’t see clearly. For another, if we reframe our relationship with the more-than-human around intimate familial connection, then we need ways of understanding what happens when that connection becomes fraught.
The selkie story is one of family dysfunction par excellence. After all, the gutting truth at the centre of selkie families is that one’s origins lie in theft, violence, rape and unimaginable loss. The children of human-selkie pairings face a mottled inheritance, at best. Writing for The New York Times in 2008, Bryan Patrick Miller recounted how his great-great-great-grandfather’s kidnapping of a mhaighdean mhara cursed the family, causing the couple’s half-human children to flee across the Atlantic. Some coastal communities saw seals as fallen angels or the souls of drowned fishermen: mating with them was dubious to say the least.
Mottled or not, a selkie genealogy had to be reckoned with. Having a selkie in the family forced an acknowledgement of a mixed inheritance that was dark and disturbing, and in which successive generations continued to be implicated. Only by considering these uncomfortable reverberations of what it means to be family do we move closer to reimagining our relationships with the more-than-human world.

Among folklorists, the trope of the selkie is generally known as the Seal Wife – a designation that speaks more to the gendered hold that the selkie story has on our imagination than to the actual folklore topos. The selkie’s allure is such that men will risk curses and commit theft and kidnapping to possess her, regardless of the certain tragedy that forced marriage augurs. Yet the selkies of folklore are not necessarily wives, women or even seals (note the mermaids, above). Male selkies occasionally make cameos as the kidnapped selkie wife’s jilted husband, father of her aquatic children, keening for her release from outside the human husband’s house. At other times, they appear as her brother, or as characters in the quotidian interactions between humans and their environment: they are accidentally killed, kidnapped as pets, steal fish, issue prophesies, plead and bargain, proffer advice and opinions.
Male selkies also arrive as lovers. Sometimes their affair with a human woman is star-crossed – the paramours may meet only once every seven years, say – but generally sex with a male selkie is an occasion for straightforward joy. A woman who is sexually unsatisfied in her marriage or a maiden hoping for a youthful adventure may cry into the sea and receive a selkie who is, apparently, an absolute dynamo in bed.
What is fascinating about these stories is that they centre female pleasure in a way that the selkie wife stories never do (or male pleasure, for that matter). Instead, the selkie wife – despite the sensual appeal that incited her husband to theft – is noted for her competence in housekeeping and childrearing. But even the male selkie stories occasionally take dark turns. Sometimes, the human woman steals her lover’s skin to compel him into marriage; other times, the human woman is raped by the male selkie.
The hybrid relationships described in the folklore are multilayered and fraught, but they are also interwoven with the rhythms of rural Irish life. The Irish tendency to link themselves to the more-than-human world – including through creatures such as the merrow, or mermaid, the shapeshifting púca, and, of course, the aos sí, the supernatural, fairy analogue to humans – has been noted for hundreds of years. Even the Normans, frustrated with their new subjects’ disinterest in distinguishing themselves from animals, set about reforming the Irish Church and ‘civilising’ the population.
In their paper ‘Slipping Off the Sealskin’ (2015), the literary critic and research artist Clair Le Couteur points out that early selkie stories had far more gender parity than later ones: not only were male selkies better represented, but their female counterparts were more empowered. The change towards the comparably flat Seal Wife trope, they suggest, echoes mankind’s changing view of our ecological place. Male selkies have ‘been largely erased [and] replaced by a simplified Seal Wife story in which gender and species are cross-linked … the female, the animal (seals), and indeed the natural and supernatural realms are conflated into a simplified, exploitable identity formed in opposition to Man’s agency as protagonist.’
Yet if the Seal Wife stands for gendered and environmental exploitation, she does not submit without a fight. The would-be husbands who steal her skin are met with unhappiness, rebellion and sometimes violence. In one story, a mhaighdean mhara retrieves her skin by setting her kidnapper’s house on fire and following the smell of singed skin (in spite of the lore, as Le Couteur warns, that destroying a mhaighdean mhara’s skin may lead to her death).
One particularly difficult aspect of the selkie story is the repeated insistence that the marriage is a happy one. It’s a fair claim that this is patriarchal whitewashing: in one story, the teller describes the marriage as happy, only to admit two lines later that the woman laughed just three times during her spell on land – the last being at her departure. Yet the story raises a thorny truth: sometimes love is problematic. You can love someone who hurts you, and you can still leave.
The notion that humans and animals are kin falls into the broad bucket of relational ontologies known as animism, although the word occupies an uncertain place in Celtic studies. Edward Tylor, a British anthropologist and a towering figure in the nascent field, coined the term in his book Primitive Culture (1871). Here, he divided the world population into stages of evolution: ‘animism’ was reserved for savages so undeveloped they could grasp only the crudest expression of religion. It took more than a century for pioneering scholars like Nurit Bird-David to reclaim and refresh the term as ‘new animism’ and argue that animism did not describe a ‘failed epistemology’, but a relational one.
Initially, the Victorians were thrilled to equate so-called ‘backwards’ rural Europeans with those cultures abroad that they deemed uncivilised. Yet, as Natalia Schwien Scott, a herbalist, wildlife rehabilitation apprentice, and PhD candidate in religion at Harvard University told me, once colonialism became racialised, the language quickly changed: now animism as genealogical kinship between humans and animals was delegated to Black and brown cultures as a means of othering them. The Irish themselves, sick of British dehumanisation, wanted nothing to do with the term. Such leeriness is understandable – and, if history is anything to go by, completely warranted.
