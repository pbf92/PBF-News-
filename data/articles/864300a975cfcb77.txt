TITLE: Measles Costs U.S. A Status It Held For Decades As RFK Jr. Makes A Vow
OUTLET: The Daily Wire

The United States has lost its measles elimination status, ending a quarter-century run in which the virus couldn’t gain a permanent foothold in the country.
The Pan American Health Organization confirmed on November 17 that America no longer qualifies. The country earned the designation in 2000, meaning no single strain had spread nonstop within its borders for a year or more.
The downgrade comes during the worst measles comeback in decades. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention had counted 5,838 confirmed cases this year as of November 19, more than double the 2,290 recorded in all of 2025.
Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — who has previously questioned the safety of the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine — has vowed to regain America’s measles-free status, and he now recommends the MMR vaccine.
“We will stop measles outbreaks, protect American families, and work to regain U.S. measles elimination status. I continue to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles,” Kennedy said last week, adding, “We are working to close vaccination gaps and rebuild public trust. We will answer families’ questions honestly about vaccine benefits and risks and work with trusted health care providers and community leaders to prevent measles, limit its spread, and support timely treatment.”
A recent survey found that only 9% of adults in the United States know that roughly 1 in 1,000 children who catch measles die from it, according to a poll from the University of Pennsylvania’s Annenberg Public Policy Center. The CDC puts the toll at one to three deaths per 1,000 infected kids, from complications affecting the lungs and brain.
A whopping 59% admitted they didn’t understand the danger, and 23% said they underestimated it.
On the MMR vaccine, nearly half (49%) of respondents didn’t know how many doses kids need, and only about a third (34%) correctly said two.
The CDC recommends the first MMR jab, which also protects against mumps and rubella, at 12 to 15 months, followed by a second dose between ages 4 and 6. One dose is 93% effective against measles; two raise protection to 97%.
The theories linking the vaccine to autism also refuse to die.
While most respondents (64%) rejected the idea that childhood vaccines like MMR cause autism, 17% believed the theory and another 19% weren’t sure, so more than a third of respondents either accept the autism link or can’t rule it out. Earlier Annenberg surveys going back to 2024 found nearly identical results.
The vaccine-autism link traces back to a 1998 paper by British doctor Andrew Wakefield that looked at just 12 children. The Lancet retracted the paper in 2010. Wakefield lost his medical license months later and the medical journal BMJ later branded the work a fraud.
Since then, study after study has come up empty:
- A 2019 Danish study of more than 650,000 children found that vaccinated kids were no more likely to be diagnosed with autism than unvaccinated ones, even among children at higher risk.
- A 2020 Cochrane review of 138 studies covering 23 million children reached the same verdict.
- So did the National Academy of Medicine in 2004 and the World Health Organization’s vaccine safety advisers last year.
