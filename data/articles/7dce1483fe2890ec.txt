TITLE: Higher Sugary Drink Consumption Linked to Pediatric Myopia Across Observational Studies
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Higher consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages is positively associated with myopia among children and adolescents, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis published in the journal Frontiers in Medicine. Conducted by researchers at the Eye Hospital of the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences in Beijing, China, the investigation synthesized available observational studies to assess the epidemiological link between sugary drink intake and nearsightedness. However, the study authors emphasize that substantial statistical heterogeneity and a wide prediction interval indicate considerable uncertainty regarding the true magnitude and consistency of this pooled relationship, and the observational evidence does not establish causality.
The stated objective was to systematically synthesize the available observational evidence evaluating sugar-sweetened beverage consumption alongside myopia among youth recruited from school-, community-, or healthcare-based settings, while assessing the robustness of pooled statistical findings.
The authors searched multiple electronic databases to comprehensively identify relevant observational literature. Searches of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were completed on October 10, 2026, supplemented by searches of Chinese academic databases, including CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, and SinoMed, on October 21, 2026. Search parameters captured literature published through October 10, 2026, without language restrictions. Two reviewers independently screened the identified studies, extracted research data, and evaluated study-level risk of bias utilizing design-specific Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools.
The systematic review identified 15 observational studies meeting all predefined eligibility criteria, encompassing a cumulative sample of 99,364 child and adolescent participants. Within this broader evidence base, nine studies involving 78,679 participants provided extractable, beverage-specific adjusted odds ratios for quantitative meta-analysis, while the remaining six eligible studies were evaluated within a qualitative synthesis.
For the primary quantitative synthesis of nine studies, adjusted odds ratios were pooled using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation and the Knapp-Hartung adjustment. Under this statistical modeling framework, higher sugar-sweetened beverage consumption was positively associated with myopia, yielding a pooled odds ratio of 1.84 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 1.17 to 2.87 and achieving statistical significance at P = 0.014. Formal assessment of small-study effects was not performed because fewer than ten individual studies contributed to the primary meta-analysis.
Alongside the pooled odds ratio, the quantitative evaluation revealed substantial between-study heterogeneity across the included literature. Statistical testing showed a Cochran’s Q statistic of 134.51 with 8 degrees of freedom (P < 0.001), accompanied by an I-squared value of 96.34 percent and a between-study variance of tau-squared equal to 0.2905. Furthermore, the calculated 95 percent prediction interval spanned from 0.47 to 7.11. Because this prediction interval is wide and includes the null value of 1.00, it reflects substantial dispersion in potential true effect sizes across different observational settings.
The researchers performed a series of sensitivity analyses to test the stability and robustness of their pooled findings.
In their conclusions, the authors emphasized that current observational evidence suggests a positive association between higher sugar-sweetened beverage consumption and myopia among children and adolescents, but the observational data do not establish causality. Marked between-study heterogeneity and the wide prediction interval highlight considerable uncertainty regarding the true magnitude and consistency of the association. The authors concluded that further large-scale prospective studies with standardized exposure definitions, cycloplegic refraction, and consistent adjustment for key confounders are warranted.
