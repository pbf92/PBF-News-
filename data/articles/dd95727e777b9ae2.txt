TITLE: Platforms face fines up to 1% of global turnover under French press pay law
OUTLET: PPC Land

France's Parliament on November 19, 2026 definitively adopted a law that lets the media regulator Arcom order digital platforms to hand over the data behind press publishers' remuneration, and to fix that remuneration itself when three months of negotiation produce no agreement. A platform that disregards Arcom's injunctions faces a penalty capped at 1% of its worldwide turnover. On the same day in Luxembourg, Advocate General Maciej Szpunar advised the Court of Justice of the European Union that national laws of this kind are compatible with EU copyright law only inside firm limits: publishers must remain free to license their content for nothing, and platforms must remain free not to use it at all.
Those two events, and the commercial terms Meta paused on November 5, are the subject of an analysis Geradin Partners published today on its news page under the title "France's New Press Publishers' Rights Law Meets EU Copyright Law: The Meta Terms of Service Dilemma." The competition law firm argues that the three developments describe a division of labour between copyright, sector regulation and competition law, and that a royalty-free permission written into a dominant platform's standard terms could take a use outside the publishers' right altogether, beyond the reach of any regulator's price-setting power.
In Short
France has passed a law that lets its media regulator decide what big platforms pay news publishers when talks fail, and fine platforms up to 1% of their global turnover for ignoring its orders. An adviser to the EU's top court said the same day that such laws are allowed, but that publishers must keep the right to give their content away free and platforms must keep the right not to use it. Lawyers at Geradin Partners argue this leaves a gap: if a publisher accepts a free permission buried in a platform's standard terms, as Meta's now-paused terms would have granted over business accounts, there may be nothing left for the regulator to price. Meta has put that clause on hold rather than withdrawn it, so courts and competition authorities in France, the UK, Norway and Brazil are, for now, the main check on its return.
Three developments, one question
The Geradin Partners analysis carries no byline. Its page is dated 23/11/26, while the text itself carries the date November 20, 2026, the day the paused Meta terms had been due to take effect. A French version is offered as a download.
It frames the French statute and the Advocate General's Opinion as answers to the same problem. According to Geradin Partners, both "bear on a question that has become the central one in this field: how far may a national legislator intervene to secure fair remuneration for press publishers without undermining the exclusive and contractual nature of their rights?"
The right at stake is the one created by Article 15 of Directive (EU) 2019/790, the 2019 copyright directive for the digital single market. It gives press publishers an exclusive right over online uses of their publications by information society service providers. France transposed it by the Law of 24 July 2019, and, according to the analysis, publishers there have faced "persistent difficulties" in obtaining payment from major digital platforms ever since. Belgium transposed it by the law of 19 June 2022. Italy's version, built around the communications regulator AGCOM, was tested at the Court of Justice earlier this year, when the Grand Chamber backed Italy's publisher pay rules in a challenge brought by Meta in a judgment dated May 12, 2026.
Each country has tried a different way to turn an exclusive right into actual money. The Italian judgment set limits, and the Belgian case is testing them again. The French statute pushes further. And Meta's terms, in the firm's reading, show how a platform with market power could make the whole exercise moot.
What the Balanant law changes
The French law takes its name from Erwan Balanant, a member of the National Assembly. The trade publication CB News, reporting with AFP, said the final vote took place in the Senate on November 19, with Michel Laugier as the Senate's rapporteur. The text, a proposition de loi "visant à renforcer l'effectivité des droits voisins des éditeurs et des agences de presse" (aimed at strengthening the effectiveness of the neighbouring rights of press publishers and press agencies), has completed its parliamentary passage but awaits promulgation. CB News reported that the President of the Republic should promulgate it within a maximum of 15 days unless the Conseil constitutionnel is asked to review it.
Not every group in the French press welcomed the result. The SNJ journalists' union told CB News that many professional journalists have yet to receive a single euro from neighbouring rights, and described the bill as remarkably empty for journalists. The Senate rapporteur asked the government to study whether part of future Arcom fines could be returned to publishers and agencies, according to the same report. That is a request, not a provision of the law.
How the French mechanism works
According to Geradin Partners, the mechanism operates at two stages of a negotiation, with enforcement and cooperation provisions around it. The analysis describes six elements.
- Data transmission. Platforms must transmit the data needed to calculate the remuneration owed to publishers, and Arcom may order them to do so.
- Three-month deadline. "Where three months of negotiation produce no agreement, either the publisher or the platform may ask Arcom to determine the remuneration itself," according to the analysis. The request can come from either side, not only from the publisher.
- Penalty cap. A platform that disregards the regulator's injunctions faces a penalty capped at 1% of its worldwide turnover. CB News, reporting with AFP, described the same cap as applying where platforms fail to comply with Arcom's orders to provide the necessary information.
- Visibility during talks. The analysis refers to "the statutory restrictions on changes to the visibility of press content during negotiations." CB News described the rule as a ban on platforms reducing the visibility of publications while negotiations are under way.
- Referral to the competition authority. The law provides for cooperation between Arcom and the Autorité de la concurrence, including "referrals from Arcom to the French Competition Authority where potentially anticompetitive practices arise."
- Automated data collection. A separate provision allows Arcom to collect publicly accessible data by automated means notwithstanding platform terms of service. The analysis draws a clear line around this power: "That is an investigative power, not a statutory invalidation of contractual licences granted by publishers, and the distinction matters for what follows."
That sixth point is the hinge of the whole analysis, and the text makes it twice. Arcom can scrape a platform's public pages to check what is happening, whatever the platform's terms say. But nothing in the statute, as the firm reads it, cancels a licence a publisher has already signed, or clicked through.
The analysis does not give the law's article numbers, the date it would enter into force, or whether an Arcom determination of remuneration can be appealed and before which court.
The Advocate General's limits in Streamz
The Belgian case, Streamz and Others (Case C-663/24), concerns the law of 19 June 2022 that transposed the same directive. According to Geradin Partners, that law established a negotiation mechanism between press publishers and information society service providers, and granted authors and performers who have assigned their exclusive rights a non-waivable, non-transferable right to remuneration. It is contested by subsidiaries of Google, Spotify, Meta, Streamz, Sony Music Entertainment, Universal Music and Warner Music. The Belgian Constitutional Court referred questions to Luxembourg, and the Grand Chamber heard the case on July 6 and 7, 2026.
In the Opinion of November 19, according to the analysis, Advocate General Szpunar considers that Article 15 "does not, in principle, prevent Member States from establishing administrative procedures to supervise negotiations between press publishers and digital platforms." Nor, in his view, does EU law preclude unilateral disclosure obligations on platforms, "provided the information is necessary to determine remuneration and commercially sensitive information remains confidential."
Then come the limits. Drawing on the Court's May 12 judgment in Meta Platforms Ireland (Case C-797/23), the Advocate General restates three, as the analysis summarises them.
The Court's own press release, No 168/26 of November 19, adds detail on the second strand of the Belgian law, which goes beyond press publishers. According to the Court, the Advocate General considers that EU law does not in principle preclude the residual remuneration right for authors and performers, provided rightholders keep the possibility of authorising exploitation free of charge. For online content-sharing services, the residual right may cover only content they were licensed to use; without authorisation, a provider must block access and cannot be required to pay. Double payment must be avoided and total remuneration must stay appropriate and proportionate. In one respect the Opinion goes against the Belgian scheme: according to the press release, the freedom to provide services precludes the mechanism insofar as it prevents multi-territorial licences covering online uses in Belgium.
An Advocate General's Opinion does not bind the Court. The Grand Chamber's judgment in Streamz has no announced date.
For France, the firm singles out two provisions. "These qualifications are particularly significant for the French framework, notably Arcom's new power to determine remuneration and the statutory restrictions on changes to the visibility of press content during negotiations," according to Geradin Partners. The analysis stops short of saying either provision is incompatible with EU law. It does say the measures are "broadly consistent" with the approach the Advocate General endorsed for Belgium. The tension is easy to see, though. A rule that a platform must remain free not to use press content sits awkwardly next to a rule that restricts how a platform changes the visibility of that content while talks are running.
Competition law as the second flank
If copyright cannot force a deal, something else has to stop a dominant platform from setting the terms. Geradin Partners points to three recent competition cases.
On July 8, 2026, the Autorité de la concurrence imposed interim measures requiring Meta to negotiate with publishers within 15 days, ordering it to resume good-faith negotiations with press publishers and disclose information needed to assess the economic value of their content. The Paris Court of Appeal upheld those measures on October 29, 2026, in Case Nos 26/12580 and 26/12581, when Meta lost its appeal and was told to share revenue data with French press groups. In October 2026, Poland's competition authority raised objections against four companies of the Google group, suspecting an abuse of dominance in remuneration negotiations that, it alleges, were conducted without the information required by law, leaving publishers unable to assess the rates proposed. Reuters reported the Polish case on October 5.
The firm reads the second Paris judgment as the important one. According to Geradin Partners, in Case No. 26/12581 the Court of Appeal "observed that the existence of Meta's terms of service did not preclude a finding that the platform might be imposing unfair trading conditions through its market power."
