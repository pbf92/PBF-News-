TITLE: GTA 6 Review: Rockstar's Vice City Is a Masterpiece I Never Want to Leave
OUTLET: PCMag

When I first started playing Grand Theft Auto VI ($79.99) a few weeks ago, before I even did anything at all in this next-gen vision of Vice City, I just tried to be present. I wanted to get the lay of the land in this previously hypothetical video game that had finally become real. The developers at Rockstar aren’t just the originators of the open-world game; they remain among the best in the genre. GTA 6 soon reminded me why that’s the case.
Rockstar’s games understand that an open world isn’t just an overgrown level, a giant box to fill with content. Rather, open-world games should be captivating interactive spaces to exist in for their own sake. The Vice City of 25 years ago understood this. And now that I’ve played all of GTA 6, frequently losing myself throughout my 100-hour playthrough, I can say it understands this lesson, too.
I never want to leave Grand Theft Auto VI because it knows the value of a vital vacation.
Vice City and Leonida: My Virtual Dream Vacation
When you’re a kid, how you travel is basically up to your parents. But as an adult, I’ve tried to become more aware of my own vacation style and actively refine it into something that serves me. I like some structure, but not too much. Give me an itinerary with some unmissable landmarks and plenty of free time to roam. I’ll bail on any plans that become boring. Send me to a place with a nice downtown and an energizing urban atmosphere. Bonus points if it has a boardwalk on the water. I also won't say no to a nice nature hike. I want to live like a local, not a tourist, and walk around where real people are. I love seeing how fast neighborhoods change from one to the next.
It’s remarkable how much of this philosophy I brought into my GTA 6 play sessions, without even realizing it. I treated Vice City like any other exciting new place I’ve visited, and my wanderlust was similarly rewarded. I could fill pages and pages just by recounting all the little moments that emerged whenever I decided to head off in a random direction with only a vague plan. By default, the minimal UI isn’t littered with distracting icons. Instead, you can choose when to reveal any potential points of interest nearby. The story could wait; my own curiosity gave me all the motivation I needed to simply keep putting myself in new scenarios.
I walked up to a food truck to see what they had. It was po' boys. I ordered and ate the sandwich, pressing the button a few times to take a few bites, and my stamina was restored. There was a shopping center close by, and hopping the fence was faster than going around the corner. I noticed I was near an electronics store and wondered what meaningful items it would sell in this game. New cases for my phone? A neat aesthetic item but not essential. The headphones, however, allowed me to listen to Rockstar’s famously well-curated radio stations even while walking, not just while driving. My wife insisted we listen to the radio station that featured mostly Spanish acts, so I strolled down the block enjoying the Latin island groove of Cocoteo FM co-hosted by Bad Bunny himself.
Was that the most action-packed anecdote in the world? Of course not, but I’m obsessed with how many details I can use to describe the experience. It’s like recounting a real trip I took. That’s the case with so many of my experiences in GTA 6. I could talk about heading down to Vice City’s Little Cuba and being forced to take the bus back because the van I tried to steal was broken. I should’ve realized there was a reason the busted vehicle was just abandoned on the side of the road. One time I robbed a waffle restaurant, grimaced in embarrassment when a mission forced me to return to the same waffle restaurant, and cackled once I realized I had to rob it again. Drive down enough highways, and you’ll be challenged to impromptu street races. Sometimes I accepted the challenge, sometimes I didn’t. Sometimes I left the race halfway through because something else was more important. But regardless of the outcome of a single throwaway mini-game, those were all stories I remembered, and promises of more possibilities.
These moments stick out in my mind, even more vividly than many of GTA 6’s blockbuster third-person cover shooter missions, because I made them happen. Many of the gameplay enhancements add not just mechanical depth but an increased feeling of agency. I put more thought into which cars I stole because I had to think about filling up the tank or disabling an alarm. I made the choice when to rob a backwater convenience store versus a well-protected bank. I turned down most offers to do coke, but not all. A vacation is also an expression of agency, a temporary version of life you’ve decided to live just for the sake of fun. All open-world games claim that more freedom will give you more investment in your entertainment. GTA 6 fully proves that.
Graphics and Performance on PS5: Wastin’ Away Again in Margaritaville
Key to selling this overwhelming illusion is the fact that Grand Theft Auto VI is simply one of the best-looking video games I’ve ever seen. Those fearing a Cyberpunk 2077-style technical disaster at launch should rest assured that’s not the case here. Multiple delays likely helped here. Aside from maybe some occasional blurriness from image upscaling, the game looked great and ran without issues on my standard PlayStation 5. Any screenshot will show you how many elements are packed into each frame. In motion, I was especially impressed by the moody shadows and striking lighting, impossibly perfect orange sunsets with a neon purple color palette that’s spiritually '80s without being totally anachronistic. It’s proof of how realism and art direction don’t have to be mutually exclusive.
And as for how it plays, the awkward driving and clunky shooting of past games are nowhere to be found here. Even punching people and dodging counterattacks, while basic, has smooth rhythm and flow. Just as a graphically impressive and mechanically sound video game, GTA 6 delivers, but it's also so much more.
Still, while often aiming for nothing less than a simulation of life itself, GTA 6 is still a video game. It has fail states and graphical settings and dialogue that awkwardly repeats if you interrupt the speaker. It’s not reality. But really, neither is a vacation. Everything is a little unreal on vacation. The money you spend, the things you accumulate, the vehicles you enter, the food you eat? None of it really counts. Time itself doesn’t matter. All you have to show for it is the memories and whatever you can fit in your suitcase.
I’ve had plenty of vacation moments that felt downright hallucinatory. One time, I was on a boat with some bros having a bachelor party, and one of them got attacked by a shark while diving to explore some wreckage. Another time, on another boat, our captain casually talked about his side gig as an illegal octopus hunter, and I’m pretty sure this Australian couple was into us. One of these tales is from GTA 6, and the other is from my real life, but it’s amazing how well they pair.
Just as Control Resonant benefited from its depiction of freaky New York City, GTA 6 captures the liminal vibe of a good vacation especially well by recreating a version of Florida, here called “Leonida.” And we’re talking about Florida today, not some 80s throwback. We’re talking about a place so innately cartoonish it’s the perfect unspoken context justifying typical GTA shenanigans. Whether you’re slapping a civilian as you drag them out of their car or shooting a rocket launcher at a helicopter, it feels diegetic here. It’s not just inexplicable video game nonsense; it’s something you got up to while waiting for your next flight out of this hellhole. Just steal your own plane and fly away if you want. Vice City isn’t fake, Florida is, and I mean that as a compliment.
Accepting that GTA 6 is very much not real also makes it all the more astonishing how much the game is very real. Again, I like to live like a local when on vacation because I need to see visible humanity as a counterbalance to tolerate even the most attractive artifice. And GTA 6 does a great job validating that pretentious mindset of mine. By being highbrow, middlebrow, and lowbrow, the state of Leonida feels tremendously well-rounded and believable.
Your journey through the criminal underworld is essentially a journey through class strata. By the end, I was lounging on tropical sandy beaches in designer clothes, people-watching at the fanciest hotel’s nicest pool with cocktail in hand. These were the perfect spots to take selfies with my phone before browsing posts on the game’s fake social media site. My feed was full of similarly vapid influencers, either flaunting actual wealth or just cosplaying it for this one unreal afternoon.
But before that, my accommodations were much more modest. I spent much of the game feeling like a Jimmy Buffett fan, enjoying these substitute Florida Keys as an early retiree in a margarita-soaked paradise for the irony-pilled. Sunny and kitschy heaven on plastic earth. Each green parrot and pink flamingo I spotted was another breezy good omen to cherish. I recalled them fondly as the game inevitably transitioned into the Miami Vice-style gangster tale of city sleaze and corrupt cops most players probably expected from the beginning.
GTA 6 is also a game about absolutely slumming it like a dirtbag. I fell out of a fan boat, swam underneath my pursuers on jet skis like an amphibious predator, and got into a shootout with drug pushers in the swamp set to twangy rockabilly music. Any one of us could’ve been eaten by an alligator at any minute. I trudged back to my musty motel room, where I had left the TV on for background noise. All I could think about was the Orlando shack, full of fake leopards and real snakes, I once stayed in during a family vacation to Disney World.
    Accepting that GTA 6 is very much not real also makes it all the more astonishing how much the game is very real.
    
From its glossy cutscenes to its scripted missions, GTA 6 spends a lot of time operating as this slick and expensive cinematic experience that most AAA games aim for these days. But for every high-speed car chase and quick montage, the game will also switch to the deliberate and relaxed pacing of real life. It has a confidence to be slow, bordering on dangerously close to intentionally dull, that feels like a follow-up to Red Dead Redemption 2’s methodical cowboy melodrama. Time periods may change, but crime stays the same. Some may find the downtime tedious, but I derived a lot of meaning from these relaxing resets and opportunities to explore.
GTA 6 forces you to socialize with your friends from all walks of life in all sorts of places. I hopped from rural dive bars and strip joints to inner-city hip-hop house parties and bougie nightclubs where you might spot a drunken celebrity. Everything was so hazy and smoky, another semi-real space where time stops mattering. The way every party lasted a little too long, with me entertaining a few too many friendly conversations that went nowhere, felt so true to life. Although, granted, I’ve never been to a club where I suddenly needed to find the best location for sniping mercenaries (smoking a blunt helps you chill out and better target enemy weak spots).
After leaving one party, without violence for a change, I decided I didn’t want to steal a car and ruin the night. I just stumbled out onto the sidewalk’s dirty pavement, the scene lit only by streetlights. My companion said, “Oh, so we’re walking back?” All at once I felt the aftermath of so many delirious college ragers on nights where tomorrow’s classes might as well not even exist. GTA 6 is a video game; it’s not real. But also, it is.
