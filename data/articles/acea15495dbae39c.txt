TITLE: I Do Not Understand Why I Scroll
OUTLET: Christianity Today

We know that we are addicted to our screens and we know that this is bad. It is unclear whether this knowledge is having much affect on our behavior. We continue to check our phones while waiting at the bus stop, sitting through a sermon, standing in the checkout line, or dissociating at a family meal. We keep screens on our wrists and in our living rooms; in our offices, in our classrooms, and by our beds. We have theological, scientific, and sociological arguments for why we ought to do differently. No one can tell that from looking at us.
Most Americans report dissatisfaction with the amount of time they spend tethered to their smartphones; they also report difficulty changing their digital habits. We feel pessimism about AI; we are also increasing our reliance on chatbots. We are developing stronger feelings of suspicion toward big tech firms; we are also continuing to adopt their products. What, exactly, is going on with us?
There are structural reasons for why we struggle to extricate ourselves—the engineered addictiveness of our devices, the ruthless expansion of surveillance capitalism. But I am curious about the impulse that keeps us reaching for our devices even when our behavior fills us with ambivalence.
The hypothesis I am most compelled by comes from my friend, art historian Christian Gonzalez Ho, who explains our screen usage as a form of supplication: “Looking at media … is a longing for pilgrimage—it’s an engagement we undertake while desiring to go to another place.” Because of this, he says, “bending down to look at your iPhone or pulling it out of your pocket is a devotional act.”
The metaphor works. The sight of a group of people lost in their screens is reminiscent of a group of people lost in prayer. Everyone is silent and focused, tapping at dark, glossy windows, beseeching their devices for something.
And why not? Screens function as portals to our desired reality. They can show us consumer goods that we wish we had and then summon those goods to our doorstep. They can help us escape the people who surround us and put us in contact with the people we prefer. They can envelop us in images, sounds, or narratives of our choice, and insulate us from our immediate environment. To use Gonzalez Ho’s framing, each time we tap against a device, we are engaging in a form of prayer, asking to enter a world that is superior to the one we inhabit.
Where are we trying to go? Journalist Elise Hu gives a hypothesis in her book Flawless: Lessons in Looks and Culture from the K-Beauty Capital.
Hu points to the rise of new cosmetic procedures as evidence for how our preferences are being remade through our relationships with our screens. Already, many of us have grown accustomed to seeing humans “corrected” by dental prosthetics and buccal fat removal, or FaceTuned, or generated entirely by AI. Because these optimized images are the ones most likely to dominate algorithmic recommendations, looking at our screens is akin to gazing into a mirror that is determined to reflect back a distorted image.
Now, Hu observes, wealthy nations are producing services that can match our bodies to the images we see on our devices. This places a burden on all internet users, male and female, to interpret the world through the eyes of an algorithm and to align ourselves with the algorithm’s expectations.This may mean using photo editing software to perfect the images we post online. This may also mean surgically altering our bodies to meet the internet’s demands. Wealthy, postindustrial societies are beginning to normalize the latter. Hu calls this phenomenon the internalization of a “technological gaze”: a perspective reinforcing “an algorithmically determined set of ideal traits for our facial and body parts.”
Hu argues that this trend should be troubling for everyone. As she sees it, the underlying problem, relevant even to those of us who do not believe we will ever pay for cosmetic alterations, is the rate at which our societies acquiesce to the technological gaze. She is concerned that our tastes have become irrevocably divorced from the physical world, and that the market is rapidly evolving to accommodate a set of desires that are essentially shorn from reality. Hu warns that we have entered an era where “the virtual world leads the physical world … [where] how we exist on screens takes privilege over how we exist in real life.”
As I read Hu’s book, I googled cosmetic surgery “before and after” photos for the procedures she describes. It’s unclear if people look better in the “after” photos. However, what was immediately obvious to me was that the “after” photos appear less idiosyncratic than the “before” photos, with fewer aberrations that would give anyone reason to pause and look at one image longer than another. There is a smoothness to skimming the “after” images, an optical experience equivalent to the tactile one of running your hands across a surface that will never catch or snag. If scrolling is a form of prayer, Hu provides a vision of where these prayers are taking us—into a future where our technologically-enabled preferences can manifest in the flesh.
This is clearly a form of idolatry, and the church is not immune. Kelsey Kramer McGinnis argued in CT that the ubiquity of “Jesus glow-up” posts on TikTok and Instagram indicates that Christians, like the rest of the world, have grown accustomed to envisioning our lives through the algorithmic gaze. These posts, which are meant to depict the change that occurs once a person decides to follow Christ, also compare “before” and “after” photos, conflating a spiritual transformation with a physical one. Unsurprisingly, the “after” photos hew closer to an algorithmically rewarded ideal. Men are better groomed and more muscular; women grow longer, glossier hair. Everyone has clearer skin.
Let’s assume that all these posts correspond with sincere confessions of faith. Even so, McGinnis argues that their popularity evinces “a failure of imagination,” a definition of Christian life that has capitulated to “the constantly shifting, often-oppressive beauty standards determined by entertainment media, male desire, capitalism, entrenched racism, and ableism.” Like everyone else, Christians have acquired a taste for the algorithmically optimized version of reality. The growth of Christian-owned medical spas suggests that the theology behind the Jesus glow-up is convincing enough to attract a customer base that will pay to align their physical selves with their online avatars.
Glow-ups aren’t the only way the church is acquiescing to the artificial. The proliferation of sermon–generating AI platforms indicates an appetite for inhumanly polished, consistently delivered content from the pulpit. In California, one church has generated headlines with its creation of an AI pastor chatbot that “ministers to his flock when the real man cannot.” The church’s technical director told ABC News, “I can make an appointment with the pastor, but I can ask this question right now.” His description of the chatbot is arguably more descriptive of the demands congregants are placing on their leaders.
An endless scroll of attractive new converts, sermons that break down neatly into short reels, and instantaneous responses to the questions troubling us late at night—there is a gratifying concision to the kind of Christianity we access through our screens. Unmediated contact with reality cannot compare. We may have entered theologically problematic territory, but it seems that plenty of us understand its appeal.
Western Christendom has had a rough decade. Between celebrity pastor scandals and a distressing preoccupation with political dominance, it makes sense that heavily mediated interactions with the church are preferable to rawdogging it in the wild. The same goes for our planet in general, which continues to be ravaged by interminable wars, economic volatility, and the threat of environmental collapse. Regardless of our professed beliefs, our conflicted dependence on technology seems driven by the understanding that the world can be frighteningly unconcerned for our well-being. We wield our devices to help ourselves survive its unruliness.
Strangely enough, it is the apostle Paul, writing to the Colossians two millennia ago, who correctly assesses our predicament. The Colossian Christians, attempting to survive a landscape ridden with political hostility and religious syncretism, were adopting coping mechanisms similar to our own.
“Do not handle! Do not taste! Do not touch!” Paul writes, referencing the religious strictures observed by some of his hearers. “These have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh.”
The Colossian church treated an attenuated relationship to their environment as evidence of spiritual maturity. Like us, they used disembodiment as an exit strategy; where the world became malevolent or contaminated, they would choose not to experience it. Yet Paul depicts them as people who are helpless against the morbidity of their surroundings—powerless against “the indulgence of the flesh.” The Colossians labored over their precautions, but they remained vulnerable to decay.
Who are the Colossians’ contemporary equivalents? Is it those of us who retreat into our devices in search of a haven? Is it those of us who are persuaded by the algorithm’s rigorous curation, its “appearance of wisdom”? It is possible to argue that we are most like the Colossians when we are embedded in our technologies, awash in the fantasy that we can attain a good life once we are untethered from the world.
It is also possible to argue that we are like the Colossians when we restrain ourselves from participating in behaviors that may compromise us and then succumb anyway. We detect a threat in our environment and try, unsuccessfully, to defend against it. A majority of us report growing reliance on AI tools and challenges with curbing screen time, but we agree that this is bad for us. We wish we could monitor our habits more rigorously. Perhaps the Colossians’ contemporary equivalents are those of us who are trying to repress our longings even as they continue to evade our control.
Paul’s critique of the Colossians, and by extension his critique of us, cuts both ways. In either scenario, this is the common denominator: All of us struggle to reckon with the irreducible fact of our need. We gaze into our screens because we cannot stop longing for someplace better than where we are now. We indulge the tools that promise to harm us because we are trying to take some comfort where we can find it. Even as we construct necessary barriers against Big Tech’s overreach—litigating their abuses, legislating protections for our children—we still seem haunted by the sense that there is something missing in our environment and that we need to scroll in order to find it. Another Pauline quote to describe our contemporary malaise: “I do not understand what I do. For what I want to do I do not do, but what I hate I do.”
Christ understands the provisional nature of our choices and explains that we subsist on what we recognize as nourishment. “Your ancestors ate the manna in the wilderness,” he says, “yet they died” (John 6:31). To the Samaritan woman at the well, he observes that “everyone who drinks this water will be thirsty again” (John 4:13). He delivers his appraisal not to shame us, but to make an argument for what can actually satiate.
“I am the bread of life,” Christ says; “whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst.” Later, he issues a direct challenge to his hearers: “Truly, truly, I say to you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you.”
