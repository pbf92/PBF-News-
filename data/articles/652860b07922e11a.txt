Mesopotamia’s Primeval Waters
Louvre exhibit explores Mesopotamia’s relationship to water
Through March 15, 2027
Musée du Louvre
Paris, France
louvre.fr
An exhibition currently on view at the Louvre is dedicated to ancient Mesopotamia’s critical relationship to water, the source of all life and prosperous civilization. Titled Primeval Waters: Lessons from Mesopotamia, the exhibit explores early hydraulic engineering and how ancient Mesopotamian city-states revolutionized their interaction with land through canals, aqueducts, piping, bridges, and artificial lakes.
As the place where irrigation was first invented and developed, Mesopotamia offers lessons for the present. The exhibit draws from ancient manmade transformations of the natural environment to frame timely (and timeless) challenges resulting from wide-ranging human changes to the natural world—and their positive and negative long-term consequences.
Through more than 100 artifacts, the exhibit tells the story of the natural, human, and divine contexts of ancient water management, in which water was a source of prosperity, power, and conflict—a divine gift as much as a natural resource. On display is this Sumerian statue of Gudea, sculpted in 2120 BCE. It represents the prince of Lagash holding an overflowing vase filled with fish. It is about 24 inches tall, and the cuneiform text commemorates Gudea’s construction of two temples.
