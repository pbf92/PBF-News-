TITLE: Lean and Mathlib communities certify Levent Alpöge's Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis
OUTLET: MLQ.ai

Lean and Mathlib communities certify Levent Alpöge's Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis
- The Lean and Mathlib communities said they have "full confidence" that Levent Alpöge's proof of the Riemann hypothesis is correct and certified both its statement and its proof [1].
- The proof is a 17-million-line Lean library released on Nov. 20; dozens of people reproduced the same raw proof export, and several independent proof checkers accepted it [1][2].
- Two independent tools confirmed the theorem proved is exactly Mathlib's vetted statement of the hypothesis, the check that guards against a proof of the wrong statement [1][3].
- Clay Mathematics Institute rules still require publication in a qualifying outlet, two years of waiting and general acceptance before its $1 million prize can be considered [4][5].
The Lean and Mathlib communities have certified that Levent Alpöge's machine-checked proof of the Riemann hypothesis is correct. "We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him on this achievement," says a joint statement on the Lean community blog, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative [1].
Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, released the proof on Nov. 20 as a Lean library of 17 million lines of code, written with the company's Claude models (our report) [1][6]. The statement says the communities "rallied" over the following day to check it and "hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct" [1]. The Decoder first reported the confirmation in English-language news on Sunday [6].
What the community checked
The statement measures the proof against four criteria set out on Nov. 12 by Kevin Buzzard's Xena Project blog for judging AI-generated Lean code: whether the Lean statement matches the real theorem, whether the code compiles, whether it proves that statement and whether the proof itself is valid [1][3]. The communities say all four are met [1].
The statement of the hypothesis is not Alpöge's. It is the definition already in Mathlib, Lean's human-reviewed mathematics library, which the statement says has been "carefully vetted by experts in Lean and number theory" [1]. The community added a file that states the hypothesis importing only Mathlib, then compared it with the theorem in Alpöge's library using two independent tools, comparator and con-leche's compare mode; both found an exact match [1].
On compilation, "dozens of people" built the codebase and reproduced the same raw proof export file [1]. That export was then checked by Lean's official kernel, by the con-leche and con-ron checkers, and by several third-party checkers written in different programming languages [1]. The checks ran on an inert export, out of reach of any malicious code that might hide in the Lean sources [1].
What formal verification does and does not show
A Lean proof is only as good as its headline statement. The Xena post gives the example of a two-line Lean version of Fermat's Last Theorem that compiles but is trivially false, because Lean's natural numbers include zero [3]. That is why the statement check carries so much weight here: the theorem must be Mathlib's definition, not a lookalike [1][3].
The communities also addressed the worry that Lean's own logic could have a flaw a proof might exploit. The con-leche and con-ron checkers come with a formal proof, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof [1]. MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland, whose page lists the checks and says they were run with Claude Code, reports that the five headline theorems depend only on Lean's three standard axioms [2].
Those theorems go beyond the classical hypothesis. Sutherland's page lists versions for every Dirichlet L-function, including the form in which the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis is usually stated [2]. As of his last update at 04:35 UTC on Nov. 22, a comparator run and one Lean-kernel replay of the export were still in progress [2].
Machine verification settles correctness, not understanding. The statement closes by looking forward to work "that will help explain the new ideas in this proof" [1]. Alpöge's own note said an informal AI-generated write-up ran past 250,000 pages and that he chose to present only the Lean code (our earlier report).
Earlier than forecast, and the Clay prize
The result came far ahead of expectations. Forecasters on Metaculus had put the expected date at May 2035, and Terence Tao said last month he expected the problem to fall to "a combination of humans and AI around 2050 or so," The Decoder reported [6].
The Clay Mathematics Institute lists the Riemann hypothesis among its seven Millennium Prize Problems, with $1 million for each [5]. Its rules require that a solution be published in a qualifying outlet, that at least two years pass after publication, and that it win "general acceptance in the global mathematics community"; the institute does not accept direct submissions [4]. No Clay statement on the proof was found.
