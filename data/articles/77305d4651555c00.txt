What we learned when our Roundtables explored the quest to make minds immortal
To preserve our sense of self, some scientists are working on ways to keep our brains going—even beyond death. In their quest for cognitive immortality, these “mind savers” are exploring everything from head transplants and brain freezing to conscious software avatars.
In the latest MIT Technology Review Roundtables event, senior editor for biomedicine Antonio Regalado joined science editor Mary Beth Griggs to examine these ideas. After following the money, the science, and the philosophy, he came back part skeptical, part awestruck. Here’s a taste of what Antonio found—and what he made of it.
- Not all the projects are quite what they seem: A $190 million US government program to develop replacement brain tissue is officially about treating stroke, but “it’s also a fig leaf for the actual ambition”: living forever.
- AI could lead to convincing mind clones: Large language models are making it possible to build AI copies of people that act quite like the real thing. “This digital avatar might not be good enough for you, but what if it’s good enough for me?”
- Immortality can be a hard sell: One company, Nectome, wants to preserve brains within 12 minutes of medically assisted death. “If you’re selling a hot product, this is not it.”
- It’s not always clear what we’re trying to preserve: “I’m not even sure that I have a very good claim to be myself.”
- There’s an upside to life as a brain in a jar: “You can surf the internet. I do that pretty much 12 hours a day, anyway.”

One more thing
The “steroid olympics” were a circus—and a window into our culture
—Amit Katwala
A couple of weeks ago, at a $50 million arena built in a casino parking lot in Las Vegas, I witnessed a libertarian thought experiment come to life. The inaugural Enhanced Games were the first sporting competition where participants were encouraged to take performance-enhancing drugs.
For the event’s supporters, the Enhanced Games offered a glimpse of a future where medical advances push the human race to new heights—and they never have to get old. As I watched the games unfold, two questions bounced around my head: Were they right? And what does that mean for the rest of us?
