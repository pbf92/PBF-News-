TITLE: Korean AI developers face 5 safeguards to reuse personal data for training
OUTLET: PPC Land

South Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission on November 23, 2026 published draft rules explaining how companies and public bodies could use personal data they already hold to build artificial intelligence, even when that data was collected for something else entirely. The draft, a partial amendment to the Enforcement Decree of the Personal Information Protection Act, is open for public comment until January 4, 2027.
In Short
South Korea's privacy regulator has drafted the detailed rules for a new law that lets organisations train AI on personal data they collected for other reasons, as long as the regulator approves each project in advance. The draft matters to any business holding customer records in Korea, because it lists five safeguards every applicant must have and requires an extra risk check for projects that use sensitive data, involve children or build AI that predicts behaviour. Comments close on January 4, 2027, and the commission wants the rules settled before the law takes effect on March 9, 2027.
What the September law already allows
The draft decree does not create the new permission. That happened earlier in the year. According to the commission's regulatory impact analysis, the National Assembly passed an amendment to the Personal Information Protection Act in a plenary vote on August 20, 2026. It was promulgated on September 8, 2026 as Law No. 21910 and takes effect on March 9, 2027. The amendment inserted Articles 28-12 to 28-15 into the act, which together form what the commission's English material calls the AI Special Provision.
The idea is simple to state. Under Korean privacy law, data collected for one purpose generally stays tied to that purpose - the principle known in Europe as purpose limitation. The special provision opens a controlled exception. According to the commission's English press release, issued as an unofficial translation, data controllers "may use previously lawfully collected personal information for AI development beyond its initial collection purpose," provided three conditions are met:
- "The intended AI development is difficult to achieve using pseudonymized or anonymized data;"
- "Enhanced privacy safeguards are put in place; and"
- "The AI development serves the public interest and delivers social benefits."
Each use is subject to the commission's deliberation and approval. The regulatory impact analysis states the third condition slightly more fully than the press release does: the project must include public or social benefit, and the risk of "unfairly infringing the interests of data subjects and others" must be "remarkably low" (PPC Land translation).
That first condition is the important one for understanding the scope. Korea already allows data to be used for research and statistics after pseudonymisation, which replaces names and identifiers so the data can no longer point to a person without extra information kept separately. PPC Land reported in March 2026 that South Korea rewrote its pseudonymous data rules for AI, cutting required documents from 24 to 10. The new special provision goes further. It covers the original, unaltered data, for cases where stripping out identifiers would make the AI project unworkable.
The regulatory impact analysis describes it plainly. The special provision is "an exceptional system that permits the use of original personal information beyond its original purpose," and it brings "new risks, such as use outside the scope of deliberation and approval" (PPC Land translation). The law left the details to a presidential decree. That is the document now on the table.
What the draft decree changes, article by article
The draft adds a new Chapter 4-4 to the decree, titled "Special provisions on the processing of personal information for the development of artificial intelligence technology" (PPC Land translation), and touches two existing articles.
Five safeguards on top of the existing ones
Every organisation using the special provision would have to take five measures in addition to the general security duties that already apply to all personal data under Article 30 of the decree. The draft Article 29-13(1) lists them as follows (PPC Land translation):
- "Designation of a person responsible for processing personal information under the special provision, and the establishment, implementation and inspection of an internal management plan concerning safety measures and related matters"
- "Measures to prevent use beyond the processing scope of personal information that received the deliberation and approval of the Protection Commission"
- "Measures to restrict access rights to personal information under the special provision"
- "Measures to control access to personal information under the special provision"
- "Measures to destroy the personal information without delay when there is no longer a need to use it under the special provision, and to confirm and record the result"
The commission's English press release presents these as four bullet points because it merges access restriction and access control into a single line. The decree text itself has five numbered items, and the regulatory impact analysis also refers to "five measures."
Beyond the fixed list, the commission could attach additional conditions to an approval. Under draft Article 29-13(2), it may impose extra safety measures "commensurate with the degree of risk" where it considers them necessary, weighing five factors: the form, content, sensitivity and scale of the data and how identifiable the people in it are; the processing method and environment; "the form and scope of use of the results of artificial intelligence technology development"; the results of any risk-factor assessment; and any other risk to people's rights or interests (PPC Land translation). The concrete standards for both the fixed and the extra measures would be set later in a separate commission notice.
The regulatory impact analysis explains a design choice that may matter to engineering teams. The five measures are written as outcomes rather than prescribed methods. Its example: instead of forcing a "physically independent space" for access control, the rule requires access control that keeps the original data from leaving the designated processing scope. According to the commission, leaving the choice of method to each organisation accommodates "various development environments, such as the use of cloud and external models" (PPC Land translation).
The document does not claim the approach is free. In weighing the options, it lists a downside of the chosen design: every applicant incurs management costs, such as drawing up an internal management plan and carrying out "half-yearly inspections" (PPC Land translation). It then adds that the burden should not be large, because the measures can be built on existing protection systems. The draft decree itself does not fix an inspection interval, so that detail may land in the later notice.
Which projects need a risk-factor assessment
The act already requires a risk-factor assessment before approval in some cases and leaves it to the decree to say which. Draft Article 29-14 lists five triggers. Meeting any one of them is enough.
The full wording, in PPC Land's translation, reads:
- "Where sensitive information or uniquely identifiable information is used"
- "Where the characteristics of data subjects who require special protection due to age, disability, health condition or their relationship with the personal information controller, etc. are reflected"
- "Where artificial intelligence technology is developed that continuously or repeatedly observes or tracks data subjects, or that analyses personal information to evaluate, predict or infer the characteristics, tendencies or behaviour of specific data subjects"
- "Where, in light of the development purpose or the intended manner of use, it is reasonably expected that the results of the artificial intelligence technology will be used for judgments or decisions that significantly affect the rights, obligations or opportunities of data subjects"
- "Where the risk to the rights or interests of data subjects increases due to the processing structure or processing method, such as the collection, combination or analysis of personal information, and the case meets criteria set and publicly notified by the Protection Commission"
"Uniquely identifiable information" is a defined category in Korean law covering government-issued identifiers such as resident registration numbers. The third trigger describes what European law calls profiling, and it is the one most likely to catch recommendation and targeting models. The regulatory impact analysis explains why: continuous tracking or individual prediction "can generate inaccurate inferences," so the risks go beyond leaks (PPC Land translation). For the fourth trigger, it names hiring and screening as examples where errors and bias could turn into "real disadvantage."
The fifth trigger is open until the commission publishes its criteria. The impact analysis gives two examples of what it has in mind: combining personal data collected from different sources or for different purposes, and processing that involves transferring data abroad.
How heavy is the assessment? The commission says it is a self-assessment, not an external certification. According to the impact analysis, the applicant identifies risks it should already know about while planning the processing, reviews protective measures and attaches the result to the application. It is limited to the specific risk factors triggered rather than the whole processing operation.
How an application would be reviewed
Draft Article 29-15 and the change to Article 5 set out the path from application to decision. According to the draft, the process has six stages:
- Application. The applicant files a form set by commission notice, with documents covering four things: information about the applicant; "details of the artificial intelligence technology development"; material explaining how the case meets each condition of Article 28-12(1) of the act; and the results of any risk-factor assessment the commission has requested (PPC Land translation). Electronic documents are accepted.
- Supplementation. If something is missing, the commission may ask for more material within "a reasonable period."
- Return of the application. The commission may send an application back in three cases: where it is "clearly" written by false or other improper means, where it "clearly" does not meet the legal conditions, or where the documents are not filed or a request for supplementation is ignored.
- Expert pre-review. The commission may have a new expert committee examine the application before the formal decision. The draft adds "personal information processing for the development of artificial intelligence technology" as a new field to the commission's existing expert committees under Article 5. According to the commission's press release, the aim is to bring "relevant expertise in both AI and privacy protection" into its decisions.
- Deliberation and approval. The commission decides using five criteria, listed below.
- Conditions. An approval can carry additional safety conditions under Article 29-13(2).
