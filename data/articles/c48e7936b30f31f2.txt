TITLE: Turmeric is popular for joint pain, but does it actually work?
OUTLET: Tech Explorist

For years, turmeric supplements have been promoted as a natural remedy for arthritis pain. Backed by studies suggesting anti-inflammatory benefits, many people with knee osteoarthritis have turned to curcumin, turmeric’s main active compound, hoping for relief.
But a new Cochrane review is casting doubt on how effective these supplements really are. After reexamining the evidence using stricter standards for evaluating clinical trials, researchers concluded that turmeric supplements may provide little or no clinically meaningful pain relief for people with knee osteoarthritis, challenging the more optimistic conclusions of earlier reviews.
Osteoarthritis is the most common form of arthritis and affects millions of people worldwide. This is what happens when the cartilage that protects your joints wears away over time, causing pain, stiffness, and reduced mobility. Although exercise, weight management, and medications can help manage symptoms, there is currently no cure.
Turmeric has attracted considerable interest because it contains curcuminoids, including curcumin, compounds known to possess anti-inflammatory properties. Since inflammation contributes to osteoarthritis symptoms, researchers have long investigated whether turmeric could offer a safer alternative to conventional pain relievers.
The international team reviewed nine clinical trials involving 1,245 people with knee osteoarthritis. The studies compared turmeric supplements with placebos, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and diclofenac, and paracetamol (acetaminophen).
The majority of participants were 52-62 years of age, and the average duration of the studies was from four weeks to three months. The review indicated that there is no consistent evidence to support a significant clinical benefit for using turmeric over either a placebo or other conventional anti-inflammatory drugs.
Importantly, the researchers emphasize that the evidence remains uncertain because many studies were small, short-term, or had methodological limitations. The findings differ from several earlier reviews that reported beneficial effects of turmeric for arthritis pain and physical function.
One major reason is that the new Cochrane review included a formal assessment of trial trustworthiness. Researchers screened studies for potential concerns regarding data quality and reliability. During this process, 10 additional trials raised concerns and were not included in the final analysis because questions about their trustworthiness could not be resolved.
These studies remain under review pending clarification from their authors.
“This review highlights the importance of assessing the trustworthiness of clinical trials,” said lead author Dr. Ivan Shirinsky, a practicing rheumatologist. “Such assessments help identify studies that require further clarification before their findings can be used to inform conclusions about treatment effects.”
Dr. Shirinsky says the findings are consistent with a broader pattern in osteoarthritis research. “As a clinician and researcher, I am not surprised by these findings,” he said.
“Osteoarthritis is a complex and still incompletely understood disease. Despite intensive research, effective pharmacological treatment options remain limited, and many promising approaches have yielded limited or inconclusive evidence of clinical benefit.”
When asked whether he would recommend turmeric supplements to patients, his response was cautious.
“I would not routinely recommend turmeric supplements to my patients with knee osteoarthritis. However, if a patient asked me about them, I would explain what we know and what remains uncertain about their benefits and potential harms, and help them make an informed decision.”
The review did not show that turmeric had no effect. On the contrary, the review showed that there was insufficient evidence available to state that turmeric would be effective in providing significant pain relief for most individuals who have knee osteoarthritis. Future high-quality clinical trials may provide clearer answers.
For now, the findings remind us that natural supplements can appear promising in early studies, but rigorous evaluation is essential before determining whether they truly work.
As researchers continue searching for better osteoarthritis treatments, turmeric remains a possibility worth studying further, but not one that currently has strong evidence behind it.
