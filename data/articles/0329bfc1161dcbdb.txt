Sleep Tied to Economic Stability in New Study
A JAMA Network Open study of more than 12,000 participants links sleep patterns to financial security.
Updated on Nov. 27, 2026 in Sleep Disorders

A study published in JAMA Network Open in November 2026 found that sleep duration is strongly associated with economic stability. Participants with greater economic stability showed less variability in sleep and slept more total minutes per night.
Why it matters
The findings add to evidence that income and financial pressure may shape health outcomes, including sleep. Sleep is described as an opponent process to vigilance, and people may struggle to switch off at night when worried about basic needs.
The study used wearable devices, such as Fitbit, to track sleep amount, while many prior studies relied on self-reported sleep. A 2015 CDC analysis found 35% of people below the federal poverty level slept under six hours nightly, compared with 27% of those above three times the poverty level, but the causal direction remains unresolved.
The players
JAMA Network Open
An open-access, peer-reviewed medical journal published by the American Medical Association that features research across medical specialties.
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
The U.S. federal public health agency headquartered in Atlanta, Georgia, that tracks disease and health trends nationwide.
The details
Researchers analyzed sleep data from more than 12,000 participants collected through wearable technology. Participants with greater economic stability had less variability in sleep amount and slept more total minutes per night.
Timeline
- November 2026: JAMA Network Open publishes the sleep and economic stability study.
- 2015: A CDC analysis finds sleep disparities by income level.
Health Landscape
The 2015 CDC analysis documented that adults with lower family incomes were more likely to sleep under six hours per night. This new study extends that income-based pattern by tracking economic stability and sleep variability with wearable devices rather than self-reports.
Adults facing financial strain may find sleep harder to maintain, so tracking sleep consistency alongside daily stressors could be useful. Discussing persistent sleep problems with a clinician can help identify whether money-related worry or other factors are involved.
The takeaway
Consistent, adequate sleep appears linked to financial stability, so treating sleep as a health priority may matter regardless of income. Reducing nighttime financial worry and building a regular bedtime routine are practical steps readers can consider.
