TITLE: Gallup: 3 in 10 Black Adults Couldn't Afford Food in Past Year - News and Statistics

A Gallup survey released Nov. 23 found that Black adults are more likely than White, Asian and Hispanic adults to experience not having enough money for food, according to the source. The survey, reported by USA TODAY, indicates that three in 10 Black adults said they had a time in the past 12 months when they lacked enough money for food.
That rate was triple the 10% of Asian adults reporting food unaffordability and double the 14% among White adults. Food unaffordability was also higher in Black households earning under $90,000 a year, and grocery prices are at their highest level since 2020.
According to the latest Labor Department Consumer Price Index data, food prices have risen by more than 7% since January 2024. Annual inflation has exceeded the Federal Reserve's target for more than five years, with consumer prices spiking during the pandemic and remaining elevated since.
Walter Greason, an economic historian at MacAlester University in Minnesota, attributed the higher rates among Black people to several factors. He said working class and low-income African American communities have fewer options for accessing a variety of food on a daily or weekly basis, which tends to push prices up. He also said the quality of food differs in Black communities compared with other demographics, contributing to price increases, and that discrimination, limited access to higher paying jobs and layoffs are more prevalent in Black households.
Greason added that when households face contractions, inflation and higher layoffs, African American households are affected more severely than others in the United States.
The study, conducted through Gallup's National Health and Well-Being Index, found the South had the highest food unaffordability rates. Workers in hospitality and retail were also more likely to report being unable to afford food.
During the midterm elections, affordability ranked among the most important factors for voters. A Reuters poll found that 48% of registered voters identified the cost of living as a top issue. A post-election survey found that 78% of midterm voters with moderate or major strain on their budgets said household debt motivated them to vote, and that group backed House Democratic candidates by 25 points.
