TITLE: Cancel Thanksgiving? Plenty of Americans Are OK With That
OUTLET: SheKnows

Remember the “Cancel Thanksgiving” tweet that tarnished Wisconsin state assemblywoman Francesca Hong’s primary campaign for governor over the summer?
“Should have done this in 1621,” the democratic socialist’s unearthed six-year-old X post continued. It got her widely labeled a “grinch,” “woke,” and hater of America, and she quickly backtracked, saying she “actually loves” the holiday and prompting her, just this past week, to make light of it all by posting herself (several times) in a turkey costume promoting a food donation event.
Still, the idea of canceling Thanksgiving — or at the very least altering it significantly from the traditional sit-down, turkey-centric family feast — seems to resonate with plenty of Americans, for a variety of reasons. A Pew Research poll last year found that 9% of Americans do not celebrate. And a recent YouGov poll found that 84% of Americans plan to celebrate the holiday this year, down from the 90% who “usually” celebrate it.
Some just find it tiresome. “For our household, holidays — including Thanksgiving, Christmas, birthdays — are stress factories. Thanksgiving is 14 hours of cooking for 20 minutes of eating? No thank you,” says Richard, an editor. Instead, he and his household of three — including his best friend and that friend’s husband — will either hunker down at home with takeout and movies or go on a trip. “Anything but cooking,” he says.
Going on vacation is a popular alternative. And for some, that could be as simple as wanting to take advantage of international travel deals that come with the start of shoulder season in many places, using their extra days off from work to go to Mexico (Cancun is a top Thanksgiving destination), Thailand, Peru, or the Dominican Republic, all offering deep discounts this year.
For others, traveling instead of eating turkey alongside family members goes deeper.
“I decided I will have a new Thanksgiving tradition: traveling out of the country,” says Caroline, a D.C.-based marketing consultant whose elderly parents both died this past year. “I literally leave Thursday night by myself to Madrid, where I’ll see friends. It will be about exploring my emotions about being without my parents.” Being single and kid-free has given her the freedom to reinvent the holiday — one that she’s typically disliked anyway due to the forced family gatherings and inevitability of political arguments at the dinner table.
“Every year, I’ll go someplace else,” she says. “But just the idea of getting out on an adventure to someplace that doesn’t have a Thanksgiving is really attractive to me.”
Reddit has endless threads on the topic of no longer celebrating Thanksgiving, with people offering range of reasons — from dealing with grief and living far or being estranged from family to wanting to avoid political fights with family members or having ethical beliefs that clash with the holiday’s traditions. On the threads, people share a range of untraditional traditions — going out for Chinese food, cleaning the house, enjoying a day of solitude, hanging out with friends, hosting a “f— colonialism potluck,” or even getting work done so as to not fall too far behind.
Meghan, a small-business owner on Cape Cod, has co-hosted a queer vegan “friendsgiving” potluck for at least 20 years now. “My choice to be vegan is 100% an action-based lifestyle, and I put my money where my mouth is when it comes to not supporting mass farming,” she says. It’s why she and another vegan friend started the tradition, inviting a rotating cast (which has included yours truly) to share treats like slow-roasted homemade seitan, kale salad, olive-flecked stuffing, and a range of decadent, dairy-free pies.
“When people text me with ‘Happy Turkey Day!’ it does jab me, and I can’t help but be like, ‘Free the turkey day!’ or send a carrot image back,” she says. “Now, with more indigenous awareness, I feel torn that we’re even doing Thanksgiving at all. But by putting a vegan spin on it, we are at least being more aware of and connecting to the food we are ingesting.”
Leigh Beck, meanwhile, a social worker in Maryland who grew up in Cambridge, makes an annual pilgrimage to Plymouth, Mass., for the National Day of Mourning — an indigenous gathering and day of protest that’s taken place every year since 1970, drawing upwards of 1,000 (and also livestreamed). It’s a day to fight back on “the racism of the Pilgrim mythology,” according to the website of organizers United American Indians of New England (UAINE). And that strikes a chord with Beck, who recently learned she may be a Mayflower descendent.
“There are parts of our family that are very proud of that family legacy, whereas my sister and I look at it very differently,” she says. “Showing up for the Day of Mourning is the least I can do to make up for it.” The next day, she says, she gathers with friends.
