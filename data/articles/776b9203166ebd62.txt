TITLE: My Favorite Books of 2026
OUTLET: Christianity Today

This piece was adapted from Russell Moore’s newsletter. Subscribe here.
When my son asks me when we can put up the Christmas tree, I always say, “As soon as it’s not totally humiliating in the neighborhood.” We have options this year because about half the houses on my street now have lights up. And I’ve started playing Christmas playlists (don’t hate me).
So it works that this year, we would be a couple weeks early going through my favorite books of 2026.
The risk? I still have 2026 books to read, and I very well might say sometime in December, “Why didn’t I include this?” The Little Drummer Boy didn’t let risk assessment stop him, and neither should I.
So here they are—the top ten books that resonated especially with me in some way or the other, in alphabetical order by author.
Jennifer Keishin Armstrong, Parks and Rec: The Underdog TV Show That Lit’rally Inspired a Vision for a Better America (Dutton)
There are three television series I have watched so often that I can quote every line of dialogue as it happens, even if the sound was turned off. And I mean that, as Chris Traeger would say, literally. Parks and Recreation is one of the three. This book would be worth it for me if it had only been a backstage look at the making of this quirky, brilliant situation comedy—telling me, for instance, Ron Swanson really is Nick Offerman when it comes to woodworking (Offerman actually made that canoe in the last episode).
This book, though, is much more than that. It shows how the show is the product of producer Michael Schur reading David Foster Wallace (who in another mind-blowing anecdote turns out to be the reason for the name of “David Wallace” on Schur’s other show, The Office). More specifically, the book shows how DFW’s thoughts on sincerity and irony set the framework for the whole series.
In the early years of this newsletter, I wrote about that very thing—DFW’s warnings and how Parks and Rec leans into the vulnerability of sincerity—but I had no idea the connection was direct and intentional.
If you’re a fellow Parks and Rec fan, you will find much fun in here that you never knew before—from the origin stories of Li’l Sebastian and Jean-Ralphio Saperstein to Schur’s prognostications of how each character would have voted in the 2016 presidential election (Leslie Knope votes for Hillary Clinton; Jeremy Jamm, Tom Haverford, and April Ludgate vote for Donald Trump; and Ron Swanson writes in Johnny Cash).
I had many more thoughts, but I am waiting to be interviewed about it on Pawnee Today if Joan Callamezzo’s allergies are well enough by then.
Solvej Balle, On the Calculation of Volume IV (New Directions)
I can’t remember who recommended this quirky little series, translated from Danish, but I remembered they described it as “Groundhog Day if written by Kierkegaard.” I read the first volume just to see what that would be like and found myself trapped in the story, speeding through it and skipping other things I had to do in order to see what would happen next. And then the next volume … and then the next …
In a kind of chilling coincidence, the plot revolves around today’s date, November 18. The protagonist goes to sleep on November 18 and wakes up the next day to find that it’s still November 18, and the day repeats over and over and over. She starts doing different things each day, at first to try to force her way out and then just to survive. She meets others also trapped in the same time loop.
The series takes the reader from the initial expectations of the trope (“I Got You, Babe” on the radio) to the deeper questions of whether knowing how one single day would go should cause us to save lives or to work for incremental changes or to try to dream up big systemic ones.
As I am writing this, Amazon just delivered—on release date—the new fifth volume, and I will probably have read it by the time you read this. I might read it until midnight just to make sure the phone says “November 19.”
Wendell Berry, Dispatches from Rural America: Sixteen Essays (Counterpoint)
For decades now, I’ve said, “No living writer has influenced me more than Wendell Berry.” After this year, I can’t say that anymore, but only because Mr. Berry is no longer living. His influence is as strong, or stronger, than ever. And this book—published weeks after his death—is one more demonstration of why.
The first words of the book are “Soon enough, and somewhat to my surprise, I have become an aged man.” You might expect, then, that the final writings of a 90-something-year-old man would be curmudgeonly, and you would be right. But the curmudgeonliness is not because he’s old; it’s because he’s Wendell Berry. He’s the Mad Farmer who is sane in an insane time.
In these essays, Berry writes about the Second Amendment and whether it forces us to have the kind of society in which we seemed to become accustomed to children killed by gunfire. He continues his critique of industrialized agriculture. And he includes a withering response to a New York Review of Books critic dismissive of “country people,” in which Berry writes: “I have had plenty of time to learn that my happiness does not depend on the judgment of book reviewers.”
My favorite essay in the book takes on the “fundamentalists” of science and technology, showing how they display all the same characteristics of fundamentalists of religion. In it he offers a personal reflection on faith and hope. “‘Faith,’ at root, is related to ‘bide’ and ‘abide.’ It has certainly the sense of belief, but also of difficult belief, of waiting of patience, of endurance, of hanging on and holding together.”
In a sentence that could sum up an entire lifetime of work, Berry writes: “But one cannot learn the holiness of everything that lives by dissecting a frog. The holiness of frogs, and of the rest of us creatures, can be revealed only to imagination and by the works of imagination.”
As with everything else Berry, I loved reading this book. It gave me a Christmas wish that maybe, somewhere in a desk or a basement in Port Royal, there might be more essays for later on, because we need this kind of loving curmudgeon, the kind who doesn’t say “You kids get off my lawn,” but “Fellas, come on over here and let’s mow this field together.”
Luke Burgis, The One and the Ninety-Nine: Forging Identity in the Age of Social Contagion (St. Martin’s)
The title of this book is taken, of course, from Jesus’ statement to the scribes and Pharisees, grumbling about Jesus eating with tax collectors and prostitutes: “What man of you, having a hundred sheep, if he has lost one of them, does not leave the ninety-nine in the open country, and go after the one that is lost, until he finds it?” (Luke 15:4, ESV throughout). The central thesis of this book is that the shepherd in this parable “does not choose between belonging and differentiation.” He wants the flock. And he wants the individual lamb.
We live, though, in a time when we are told that we must choose between individualism and collectivism, between the personal and the communal, between loneliness and herd mentality. This book rejects that false binary. Along the way, Burgis diagnoses what’s really going on with our smartphone/social media ecosystem: “In a dopamine-driven culture, you get dopamine-driven selves.”
Burgis notes that moths evolved to fly toward light because that was the sky—the brightest thing around them. This was necessary to know where and when to fly. “But when humans invented artificial light,” Burgis writes, “moths confused it with natural light because they had not evolved fast enough to know the difference.” What if, he asks, we are the moths now? And what if that’s leading us to the worst aspects of loneliness when we think we’re pursuing individualism, and the worst aspects of mob rule when we think we’re pursuing community? “Our society will remain enervated and paralyzed if we believe that the only choice is between the one and the ninety-nine—between isolation and totalizing conformity.”
This has application to politics, technology, pop culture, and of course religion. Burgis argues—and it rings very true to me—that most religious schisms don’t happen because heretics develop different beliefs from the orthodoxy of the group and start to fight, but the other way around. People start to resent others and then develop new beliefs and new tribal instincts to differentiate themselves.
Burgis does not leave this at critique, though. He presents a vision of apprenticeship and rites of passage that can give us loyalty without groupthink, identity without isolation. And he notes that the way we start is not with individual geniuses, nor with mass movements, but with small circles of people who know and trust each other. The Inklings of Oxford—C. S. Lewis, J. R. R. Tolkien, Charles Williams, Owen Barfield—are a representative case. So are Jesus’ 12 apostles. “Associations of a modest size are large enough to test ideas against resistance, yet intimate enough for personal accountability,” he writes.
Brendan Case, The Justification of the Sinless: In Supralapsarian Christology and the Goodness of Incarnation (Baker)
I remember sitting behind a pulpit about to preach at a church, when a guy stepped up to perform “special music.” The lyrics of this music depicted God looking around at the sin and wreckage of his fallen creation, asking “Who can I send to make this right?” and Jesus stepping forward to say, “Daddy, it’s me.” I think I literally cringed. I realized, though, that a lot of what people think the biblical story is about is just that. The assumption is that a perfect world would be the sons of Adam and the daughters of Eve in Eden, obedient to their loving but distant God, and that the Fall required an emergency Plan B—the Incarnation and Atonement of Jesus Christ.
For over 30 years, the biggest and most central conviction of my life is that’s not how the story goes. God’s first purpose, around which everything else flows, is to sum up all things in Christ (Eph. 1:10). Everything else—the creation of the cosmos, the origin of humanity, the calling of Israel, our own reconciliation to God through the blood of his cross—flows from that first priority. John’s message that “the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory” is, from before time and space began, the purpose of God (John 1:14); indeed, that’s why God says “Let there be light” in the first place (Gen. 1:3).
I realized early on—reading Irenaeus and Maximus the Confessor and others—that my intuitions were very old. I don’t agree with my 20-year-old self (or my 40-year-old self) on everything, but on this I am more convinced than ever. That’s called supralapsarian Christology: that the purpose of the Incarnation is logically prior to everything, including the Fall.
Though this book focuses more on historical theology than biblical theology, it sets forth this view in a compelling way. Case interacts with what the discovery of extraterrestrial life or a multiverse of parallel universes would do to our view of the uniqueness of the Incarnation in ways that are not designed to convince you as much as to make you rethink what really matters.
Don’t let the title fool you. Case is not arguing that the people God justifies are sinless, but rather that God created a world in which Christ Jesus would be the “firstborn of all creation” (Col. 1:15). He—meaning not just the preincarnate Son but Jesus of Nazareth, the offspring of Abraham and heir of David—is the reason for everything and the heir of everything. The Incarnation is not a contingency plan.
