TITLE: OpenAI Codex, Astra Flag Exoplanet Candidates; SETI Astronomer Matches Two Signals, None Confirmed
OUTLET: Tech Times

OpenAI's Nov. 24 post describes two non-professional astronomers who used its Codex and Astra AI tools on public NASA telescope archives and came back with exoplanet candidates. None of the leads is a confirmed planet, and OpenAI says confirming the two Kepler signals could take months or years. Kevin Bond, an OpenAI engineer, ran a Codex-built search on about 100,000 Kepler stars and flagged more than 200 signals; an astronomer at the SETI Institute independently found two of them. Ethan Byrd, founder of a Y Combinator startup, used Astra on about 19 million stars and produced 49 first-pass candidates.
A candidate is a repeating dip in a star's brightness that fits a planet crossing in front of it, and it fits equally well with two stars eclipsing each other. OpenAI's post notes that two stars passing in front of one another can produce a similar dip. The practical test for any similar announcement is whether the dip has survived checks that can separate those look-alikes, not whether a second program found the same dip.
The source is also not neutral. OpenAI published the account, and Bond is an OpenAI engineer, so "amateur" here means a non-professional astronomer, not someone outside the company. The post arrives more than six weeks after Gizmodo reported a similar case: a 28-year-old product manager in Spain who used Claude Code and Codex on TESS data and has follow-up telescope observations scheduled through Nov. 26.
Codex Pipeline Flagged Over 200 Kepler Signals; Ten Matched Known Planets
A transit search looks for a small, periodic dimming of a star. An Earth-size planet crossing a Sun-like star dims it by about 80 parts per million, so the search is a statistics problem before it is an astronomy problem. Standard tools for the job include the widely used box least squares algorithm and the newer transit least squares method, which its authors optimized for detecting small planets. OpenAI does not say which algorithms Bond used. It says Bond had Codex compare published methods, combine them into a search program and test whether code changes improved results.
TechnoEdge describes the program as working in two stages: one AI agent scans light curves and pulls out promising signals, and a second agent examines the flagged signals in detail. Kepler data record only brightness over time, and brightness alone cannot give an object's mass, which is the measurement that helps separate a planet from a brown dwarf or small star. Radial velocity measurements give minimum mass, using the Doppler shift a planet's pull puts in starlight.
Ten of the 200-plus signals belonged to planets astronomers had already confirmed, and TechnoEdge reports Bond cites that as evidence the method works. That is a recovery check: it shows the search finds real planets, not how many of the other signals are look-alikes. OpenAI's post does not report a false-positive rate for the search.
Two of the remaining signals stood out. KIC 5689121, a cool star about half the Sun's size in Lyra, dims every 13.8 days, and a planet responsible would be about 2.4 times Earth's diameter. KIC 6691030, a faint star in the northern sky, dims every 4.8 days in a pattern consistent with a planet smaller than Neptune. The "KIC" prefix is the Kepler Input Catalog, a target database of about 13.2 million entries used for the mission.
SETI Astronomer's Recovery Rules Out One-Program Quirk, Not Binary Stars
Bond contacted an astronomer at the SETI Institute, who ran an independent search and found two of the same signals in the Kepler data. OpenAI's post does not name that astronomer. It names Vishal Gajjar of the SETI Institute, whose title is associate director for international SETI programs, only as a commentator. He calls the initial checks encouraging and says citizen scientists equipped with AI can help astronomers explore Kepler data. Interesting Engineering describes Gajjar as running a separate search, a detail OpenAI's post does not support.
An independent recovery rules out a bug or quirk in one program. It does not rule out a physical look-alike, because both searches read the same Kepler light curve and a physical look-alike would produce the same dip in each. In blended and grazing eclipsing binaries, a third star dilutes the eclipse or one star barely clips the other, and the eclipses are indistinguishable when the two stars have similar masses. A 2012 study estimated the false-positive rate for Kepler transits could reach 40% in single-planet systems, while systems with several candidates have a very low rate. OpenAI's post does not say whether either KIC star has other candidate planets.
Confirmation often runs through radial velocity, which needs high signal-to-noise spectra and is used mainly on relatively nearby stars. OpenAI describes KIC 6691030 as faint and says confirming either signal could take months or years. The post does not explain that timeline, but the radial-velocity constraint on faint stars is consistent with it.
Astra Search of 19 Million Stars Produced 49 Candidates; One Is With TESS Researchers
Byrd tried several AI models before choosing Astra, which researched published methods, wrote the search software and ran first-pass checks on promising signals. Interesting Engineering reports Byrd designed two searches, one for exoplanet candidates in general and one aimed at habitable zones, and TechnoEdge says he ran Astra on public data such as TESS. OpenAI says the search covered about 19 million stars, and the first pass produced 49 candidates. Byrd sent one strong candidate to TESS researchers, and two more await submission. OpenAI says his search is still running.
OpenAI's post refers only to Astra, with no version number, so the model Byrd used is not pinned down. Byrd's company, Primitive, is a Y Combinator Spring 2026 startup building communication infrastructure for AI agents.
TESS has a formal route for outside candidates. The TESS team assigns TESS Objects of Interest, while independent researchers assign Community TOIs, and in October the mission page listed 8,148 candidates against 1,008 confirmed planets. OpenAI's post does not say which channel Byrd used. The Planet Hunters volunteer project is the precedent: by September 2022, 81 of 151 community submissions had been promoted to TESS Objects of Interest.
What Is New Compared With Google's 2017 Kepler-90i Neural Network?
In 2017, Google AI's Chris Shallue and astronomer Andrew Vanderburg trained a neural network on 15,000 previously vetted Kepler signals, then searched 670 star systems already known to host multiple planets. It found Kepler-90i and Kepler-80g, and OpenAI's post cites the Kepler-90 result as background.
The differences are specific. The 2017 system was a purpose-built classifier trained on vetted labels and aimed at multi-planet systems, where false positives are rarer. Bond's and Byrd's searches were built with general-purpose AI agents that researched methods and wrote the code, under direction from non-professional astronomers, and ran across about 100,000 and 19 million stars rather than a list of known multi-planet systems. NASA's open-source ExoMiner++ software sits between the two: it is trained on Kepler and TESS data, flagged about 7,000 TESS targets as candidates on an initial run, and is built to separate planet transits from look-alikes such as eclipsing binaries.
What OpenAI presents as new is the workflow, not the detection method. TechnoEdge contrasts the searches with earlier purpose-built systems: general-purpose AI designed and implemented the methods, and it argues that lowers the barrier to generating candidates. Confirmation is a separate cost.
Telescope Time, Not Candidate Supply, Limits Confirmation
Kevin Hardegree-Ullman, a research scientist at the NASA Exoplanet Science Institute, told Gizmodo the biggest challenge in confirming planets is ruling out false-positive scenarios, made harder by the number of candidates and limited telescope time. He also said AI tools will not necessarily help with confirmation.
The backlog is already large without these searches. ExoMiner++ flagged about 7,000 TESS targets on its first run, and the T16 project announced 10,091 candidates from TESS data. Astronomers have confirmed more than 6,400 exoplanets, per OpenAI.
The October case shows the same concern from the hobbyist side. Pavel Rabtsevich, who used Claude Code and Codex on more than 126,000 TESS stars and flagged a star 116 light-years away that dims every 3.18 days, told Gizmodo he hopes enthusiasm comes with careful checks so professionals are not left sorting poorly vetted signals. Every added candidate competes for the same radial-velocity and photometry time.
Three Results Will Show Whether These Candidates Hold Up
First, the TESS team's analysis of the candidate Byrd submitted will show whether a professional pipeline supports a planetary origin. Second, follow-up photometry or radial-velocity work on KIC 5689121 and KIC 6691030 will test the eclipsing-binary explanation directly. Third, publication of Bond's and Byrd's methods and candidate lists in a form others can test will show whether the searches can be reproduced outside OpenAI's own account. Rabtsevich's follow-up observations through Nov. 26 will provide a second data point on how an AI-assisted amateur lead fares.
For readers, the decision framework is short. A second program finding the same dip checks the software, a radial-velocity or follow-up measurement checks the planet, and until one of those arrives, "candidate" is the correct word for all of it. OpenAI's account is a company post, not a peer-reviewed paper, and the 49 candidates and the two Kepler signals remain unconfirmed.
Frequently Asked Questions
Can amateur astronomers find exoplanets with AI?
They can flag candidates, and volunteers have done so for years through projects such as Planet Hunters, which launched in 2010 and has had candidates promoted to TESS Objects of Interest. What the Codex and Astra searches add is automation of the search design itself. Confirming a planet still takes follow-up observations and peer-reviewed publication.
What is the difference between an exoplanet candidate and a confirmed planet?
NASA defines a candidate as a signal that is likely a planet but needs follow-up observations from additional telescopes to confirm. A confirmed planet has passed that follow-up and typically a statistical or measurement-based argument, such as radial velocity.
Does the SETI Institute match confirm KIC 5689121 or KIC 6691030?
No. According to OpenAI's account of the SETI match, a second search found two of the same signals in the Kepler data. That rules out a quirk of one program, not an eclipsing binary or other physical cause.
What would turn a Kepler dip into a confirmed planet?
Typically a radial-velocity measurement of the star's wobble, which gives a minimum mass and helps separate planets from brown dwarfs and small stars, plus other follow-up and a peer-reviewed paper. OpenAI says confirming the two Kepler signals could take months or years.
