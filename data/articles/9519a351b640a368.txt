TITLE: Google lets a like stand in for 5 seconds of viewing in Shorts conversions
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google has changed what it takes for a YouTube Shorts ad to earn an engaged-view conversion. A viewer who taps the like, comment or share button on a Shorts ad and then converts within the conversion window now produces that credit in Google Ads, according to the company's help page "About YouTube ads and view metrics." Until recently the same credit required five seconds of viewing or a click on the ad's call to action. PPC News Feed reported the change on November 21, 2026, and credited Hana Kobzová with spotting it. The help page carries no publication date, no update date and no effective date for the new wording.
In Short
Google now counts a like, comment or share on a YouTube Shorts ad the same way it counts five seconds of viewing when it decides whether a later conversion gets engaged-view credit. The same three taps also count toward TrueView views, the unit Google bills when advertisers buy Shorts inventory on a cost-per-view basis. Shorts campaigns can now show more engaged-view conversions and views without people watching any longer.
What the help page now says
The change is not confined to one sentence. The three social buttons on the right-hand side of the Shorts player now appear in three separate definitions on the page: engagements, TrueView views and engaged-view conversions. Each keeps its own time threshold, but all three now treat a tap on like, comment or share as an alternative to watching.
The conversion definition reads, in full: "YouTube Shorts engaged view conversion (EVC) counted, when a viewer watches 5 seconds of the ad, clicks the call-to-action on the video ad, or the user clicks the like, comment, or share button on the video ad, and then converts within the Engaged-view conversion window."
That rule sits directly beneath the general definition, which still describes a time-based test for skippable in-stream ads. According to Google, "Engaged-view conversions are counted when a viewer doesn't click on your video ad but watches at least 10 seconds of the skippable ad (skippable after 5 seconds) and then converts within the Engaged-view conversion window."
The engagements table uses near-identical language for Shorts. Its criteria column states: "Viewers watch 5 seconds of the ad, click the call-to-action on the video ad, or they click the like, comment, or share button on the video ad." The column asking whether views can increment answers "Yes, if the view criteria is met," and the column on clicks says: "Click counts as a click, view, and engagement."
The TrueView view definition for Shorts, finally, lists three ways in. A view is counted when "A viewer watches 10 seconds or until the end of the video, whichever comes first," when "A viewer clicks the call-to-action on the video ad," or when "A viewer clicks the "Like," "Comments," or "Share" button on the video ad."
Shorts ad metrics as the help page defines them
What changed
PPC News Feed framed the update around conversions. "An engaged view conversion (EVC) is now triggered not just by watching 5 seconds or clicking the call-to-action, but also when viewers like, comment on, or share the ad," according to the publication, which added that "this change is expected to increase the number of EVCs advertisers see from their Shorts ads."
PPC Land's own record of Google's documentation supports that reading and shows that the like, comment and share wording is recent. In March, PPC Land's guide to what programmatic buyers get wrong about YouTube ads listed a Shorts engagement at five seconds or a click on the call to action. It also noted that a click on a Shorts ad meant a click on the call-to-action button and that pausing did not count. In August, an overview of how YouTube defines views, impressions and ad metrics after the August 24 change put the Shorts engaged-view conversion at five seconds, with a default three-day window, and a Shorts engagement at five seconds or a call-to-action tap. None of those descriptions included likes, comments or shares.
The TrueView view definition moved in the same period. PPC Land's explainer on the term, published October 18, described a Shorts view as 10 seconds, the end of a shorter ad, or a click on the call to action. By November 15, Google's "About video ad formats" page already listed likes, comments or shares as ways a Shorts ad view is counted. The view metrics page now carries the same language across all three definitions.
Shorts definitions over time
How Google describes the counting sequence
According to the help page, a single Shorts ad playback can register up to six distinct events, each with its own trigger.
- Impression. "This metric is counted when the video starts playing in the Shorts feed, in between playbacks of organic Shorts videos."
- YouTube public view. "Shorts ads will count a YouTube public view the moment the video playback starts." On TV screens this figure includes estimated co-viewing, so the page notes that public views can exceed impressions.
- Engagement. Counted at five seconds of viewing, a call-to-action click, or a tap on like, comment or share.
- TrueView view. Counted at 10 seconds or the end of the video, a call-to-action click, or a tap on like, comment or share. A return visit does not add another: "If a viewer sees your ad, swipes away, and then swipes back to the exact same ad in the feed, it does not count as an additional impression or view."
- Engaged-view conversion. Counted when the viewer has met the engagement conditions and then converts within the engaged-view conversion window. Google states that EVC reporting sits under "Segment > Select Conversions > Ad Event Type." The page adds: "If a viewer is counted as a View and an Engaged-view (within watching the same video) and then converts, they'll increment both the "Conversion" column and the "Engaged-view conversion" column."
- Shorts Ads Action Metrics. The individual likes, shares and comments appear in their own reporting columns.
The page frames Shorts as a separate case. "The Shorts watch experience is inherently different from in-stream because all content (organic and ads) is immediately swipeable," according to Google. "As such, we expect performance metrics on Shorts to vary from current in-stream expectations."
Where the money sits
TrueView views are not a vanity count. "TrueView view is how you are billed if you are buying YouTube ads using cost-per-view bidding with Video View Campaign," the page states. Under the current wording, a viewer who taps like two seconds into a Shorts ad has produced a TrueView view, the same billable unit as a viewer who watched for 10 seconds. A tap on the comment button counts the same way.
What, then, counts as a comment? The language is about the button rather than the outcome. The page says a viewer "clicks the like, comment, or share button," which describes tapping the control. Nothing on the page says whether a comment has to be posted or a share completed for the tap to qualify.
The same view metric drives a published benchmark. Google defines the TrueView view rate as "views / impressions" and offers format-specific columns, including "VTR (shorts)." The page cites "About 10-15%" as average for the in-stream format and warns that "the individual formats have different criteria for what is considered an Impression or a view." With interaction taps now counting as Shorts views, the Shorts view rate rests on a broader definition of a view than it did in October.
Some protection against inflated figures is described elsewhere on the page. Google says invalid traffic detection "can take up to 30 days to fully complete," that advertisers are not charged for invalid views, and that billing invoices are "a source of truth for billable impressions."
Three-minute assets and quartile reporting
PPC News Feed also pointed to a passage on long assets. YouTube began accepting Shorts of up to three minutes for vertical videos uploaded from October 15, 2024, and ad assets can run longer than that.
According to Google, "For Shorts ads, there are unique considerations for videos longer than 3 minutes. Up to 3 minutes of the video will play in the Shorts environment, after which the video loops from the start. However, "Video played to" metrics are calculated based on the full length of the asset, not just the 3 minutes that serve on Shorts." The page adds that quartiles "will be counted based on quartile completion of the full video asset" and that "Video played to 100% will only count after the entire video has been watched to the end."
Read together, those sentences mean a four-minute asset served in Shorts stops at the three-minute mark and starts over, so a viewer who sees the whole Shorts serving has reached 75% of the asset (PPC Land calculation). On that reading, the 100% quartile cannot be reached inside the Shorts feed for assets longer than three minutes, since the final portion never plays there. The page does not state that conclusion directly.
The quartiles are, by Google's own account, approximate. "The quartiles aren't as accurate as the mechanism that increments the view count," the page says, describing them as "intended to be directionally helpful."
Google has been building toward social signals
The like button has been moving into Google's Shorts measurement for months. On June 29, Google used its Ads and Commerce Blog to add Shorts Ad Actions reporting columns to Video View campaigns and to claim that Shorts ads with more than 10 seconds of watch time plus a like produced 15% more brand consideration and 20% more favorability than ads without that combination. Google did not disclose the sample size, industry mix or measurement window behind those figures.
The help page's section on those columns is short. "You can now track Shorts Ads Actions, specifically the number of Likes, Shares, and Comments, directly within Google Ads," it says. "These social interactions are completed via the right-hand panel in the YouTube Shorts player and are now broken out into individual reporting columns."
That right-hand panel has itself changed this year. In July, YouTube removed the dislike button from Shorts and added 2x speed and mute controls.
Why this matters for advertisers
Engaged-view conversions began as a time test. When Google announced the metric in September 2020, it set the bar at 10 seconds of a skippable ad and justified it with its own finding that more than 60 percent of skips on direct response in-stream ads happened before the 10-second mark. In February 2021, Google Ads made engaged-view conversions the standard attribution for all video campaigns. For Shorts, the five-second threshold is now one of several routes, and the new routes involve no minimum viewing time at all.
The definition also travels beyond Google Ads. Since August 11, Google Analytics has let advertisers set engaged-view conversion windows to any value from 1 to 30 days, instead of a fixed three days. On November 20, PPC Land reported that the budgeting models in Google Analytics consider YouTube engaged-view conversions alongside click-through conversions and cost data. Whatever counts as a Shorts engaged view therefore also shapes the inputs to those planning tools.
Meta has travelled a similar road from the other direction. In October 2025, advertisers found that Meta counted likes, shares and saves as clicks for click-through attribution. On March 3, 2026, Meta limited click-through attribution to link clicks, moved shares, saves and other social actions into a renamed engage-through category and cut its video threshold from 10 seconds to five. In November, Meta added a six-second option for ThruPlay. Both companies now credit likes and shares through an engagement-based conversion category, with Meta's video threshold at five seconds and Google's Shorts threshold at five seconds or a single tap.
