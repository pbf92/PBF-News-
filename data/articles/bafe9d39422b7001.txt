TITLE: Christian discrimination group demands EU protects Christians as hate-crime incidents soar
OUTLET: Premier Christian News

A Christian hate-crime research group has hit out at attempts to restrict expression of the Christian faith and growing violence against believers as hate-crime attacks increase at a rapid rate.
A report from OIDAC Europe, a group which monitors intolerance and discrimination against European Christians, is demanding there is a dedicated European Union coordinator for anti-Christian hatred.
Nearly 2,500 hate crimes and incidents against Christians in the continent have been identified by the group for 2025, what they call a 44% increase in OIDAC Europe’s Annual Report 2026. Just under 400 of those incidents were attacks on individuals.
In France, where there was the largest government-recorded tally of incidents, there was a reported 70% increase in offences against individuals while attacks on clergy soared fourfold.
Meanwhile in England and Wales, there was a 24% rise in reported cases of hate-crimes against Christians to finish second in their figures.
Executive Director of OIDAC Europe, Anja Tang was clear that attacks against Christians are becoming more prevalent and that Christians were facing threats to expressing their faith.
"What concerns me personally most is less that area of violence where I think we do have to pay close attention, especially protect churches from arson attacks or under other vandalism attacks," Tang told Premier Christian Radio.
"Christians are saying, how they feel, they're facing discrimination in their jobs, or they feel like kind of almost not able to speak out about their faith in a public sphere.
"We talk about religious freedom and we should keep in mind that it has a public dimension. The right to manifest one's religion or belief in the public is also part of that human right.
The UK, unfortunately doesn't have a really good track record the last years of making legislation that, in our view, unduly restrict those rights.
"But if you look at the consequences and how Christians, as a consequence of this these laws, face prosecution even for silent prayer in a public street.
"We have to highlight that this really restricts religious freedom to a level where this is this seems to be really very much problematic when we see that in human rights legislation. the restrictions a government can give have to be very limited and very well argued."
The report highlights cases of the Swiss canton of Zurich requiring Christian hospices to permit assisted suicide on their premises and Spanish bishops and organisations facing investigations over sermons.
Germany and Austria were the third and fourth most reported countries where anti-Christian hate was identified.
The advocacy organisation is demanding that a European Union representative is appointed to tackle the issue and help protect expression of the Christian faith.
Tang continued: "We are advocating for an EU coordinator on combating anti-Christian intolerance and hatred. This is very concrete.
"We have coordinators for similar things for anti-Muslim and anti-Semitic hatred, and we think it's really time to also have someone who looks into those issues affecting Christians.
"In my opinion, one of the key things is that we start again to allow controversial debates to happen.
"I think Christians could be role models here, really talking with other people across opinions, even with someone that might oppose your personal views, teaching the younger generation again that confrontation is not dangerous, but it's enriching."
