TITLE: AI agents are joining the cybersecurity workforce but are they qualified?
OUTLET: SecurityBrief UK

AI is changing the way cybersecurity work gets done. AI agents can now help discover vulnerabilities, analyse code, investigate threats and carry out complex offensive and defensive security tasks. Some are even demonstrating the ability to navigate cloud environments, adapt when requests fail and identify exposed credentials.
But AI being able to do something is not enough. Organisations need evidence they can trust AI agents to perform the cybersecurity roles they are given, consistently, accurately and within the boundaries of their authority.
An AI model might demonstrate impressive capabilities in a benchmark or controlled environment, but that doesn't mean an agent built around it will perform reliably in a real security operation. Organisations need evidence of how the agent performs: whether it interprets the task correctly, uses the available evidence appropriately, stays within scope and produces reliable outcomes when conditions change.
AI is changing how security teams work
Tasks that once required a lot of manual effort, such as analysing vulnerabilities, reviewing code or investigating attack paths, can be accelerated using AI. This increases productivity and gives security professionals more capacity to focus on complex problems, validate findings, and make decisions.
Our AI-Augmented vs Human-Only Cybersecurity Performance Benchmark, which assessed 1,078 teams across 36 cybersecurity challenges, demonstrated this potential. AI-augmented teams achieved 1.4 times more output across all teams within the competition timeframe, rising to 4.1 times among elite performers.
However, the results also revealed that across all of the teams that successfully solved challenges, the AI-augmented teams were actually 12.5% slower on median solve times than human-only teams. Key here is that performance changed a lot with experience. In the top 25% of teams, AI-augmented teams were 2.4% faster, and this rose to 20.5% for the top 10% and 51.6% for the top 5%.
This suggests that the benefits of AI depend heavily on the capabilities of the people directing it, their ability to recognise when an agent is taking the wrong approach and their understanding of when human intervention is needed.
A benchmark is not proof of competence
AI benchmarks tell us what a model is capable of under fixed conditions. They do not tell us whether an agent can reliably perform a specific cybersecurity role.
An agent might perform brilliantly in one environment and fail in another. It might complete a task but take an inappropriate route to reach the result, disregard the intended scope or make assumptions that would be unacceptable in a real security operation. Its performance might also change following an update to the underlying model, its tools or its integrations.
This means organisations need to assess the agent to learn what it can achieve in realistic cybersecurity environments. This includes how it behaves in unfamiliar challenges and whether it operates within the boundaries set.
Crucially, assessment must go further than checking whether an agent completes a task. It should examine how it performs, the decisions it makes along the way, whether it follows the rules of engagement and how consistently it delivers the expected results.
Before AI agents are given cybersecurity work, organisations should define what competent performance looks like for that role and establish evidence that it can meet that standard before giving it greater autonomy.
Continuous assessment, not a one-off test
A successful appraisal is not a permanent guarantee of competence. Changes to the underlying model, tools, integrations or operating environment can alter performance. This means organisations need to re-appraise agents as those conditions change.
Organisations need to put agents under pressure, observe their behaviour and measure more than the outcome. Understanding where an agent fails, whether it stays within its authorised scope and how it responds to unexpected situations is essential.
Crucially, credible assessment of AI agents needs more than AI expertise. It needs genuine cybersecurity depth; realistic technical environments and an understanding of the work security professionals are actually expected to perform. The objective is not to rely on vendor demonstrations or isolated benchmark results, but to have repeatable, evidence-based assessments of agent competence.
The human workforce also needs to evolve
None of this reduces the importance of human expertise. In fact, with more capable AI agents, the skills needed to manage them are more important.
Cybersecurity professionals need to understand how to direct agents, evaluate their outputs, recognise when something is wrong and intervene when necessary. They also need to understand emerging threats associated with AI itself, including prompt injection, model exploitation and agent hijacking.
Our 2026 Cybersecurity Workforce Intelligence Report, based on data from more than 702,000 cybersecurity professionals across 251 countries and territories, identified AI penetration testing as one of the leading global training priorities. This reflects two capability requirements for the human workforce: security professionals need to know how to use and supervise AI effectively in security operations, while also understanding how AI-enabled systems themselves can be attacked and defended.
