TITLE: Residential solar panels can help reduce health risks from extreme heat
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

With intensifying climate change, extreme temperatures are becoming more common across the globe. Such extreme temperatures represent an urgent public health challenge that warrants both effective environmental and energy policy. Previous studies have linked both extreme heat and extreme cold to higher mortality risks, which are projected to grow as climate change increases the frequency and severity of temperature extremes. Addressing these threats requires effective climate adaptation measures, including ways to help people maintain safe indoor temperatures.
Households commonly rely on cooling devices, such as air conditioning, to protect against temperature extremes. However, the effectiveness of this practice relies largely on energy affordability. When energy prices rise, households may opt to cut back on cooling, increasing their risk of exposure to unhealthy indoor temperatures. Increasing global energy prices, due to inflation or geopolitical issues, can further worsen this burden. Consequently, there is an urgent need for developing solutions that can ensure people’s access to effective climate control while preventing energy-related poverty.
In a recent study, Assistant Professor Yi-Chun Ko from the Asian Growth Research Institute and Professor Shinsuke Uchida from the Faculty of Policy Studies at Doshisha University, Japan, explored how residential solar photovoltaics (PV) can mitigate the mortality risks associated with extremely hot temperatures by reducing dependence on the power grid. “Air conditioning protects people during extreme heat, but only if households can afford to run it,” explains Prof. Uchida. “While the environmental benefits of distributed renewable energy adoption are well established, we provide the first causal evidence that household solar PV can also work as a public health intervention, reducing heat-related mortality through improved energy affordability.” Their study was made available online on October 12, 2026, and published in Volume 410 of Social Science & Medicine in December 2026.
To investigate the impact of household solar PV systems, the researchers leveraged a unique natural experiment created by the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake, which resulted in the shutdown of nuclear power plants and, consequently, electricity price increases of up to 38%. During the same period, rooftop solar infrastructure was also promoted through a nationwide subsidy program in Japan.
The researchers combined monthly prefecture-level mortality data from 44 prefectures (excluding Iwate, Miyagi, and Fukushima, which were directly hit by the earthquake and tsunami) between 2009 and 2014 with high-resolution weather data and official records of residential solar PV installations. Households that install solar PV systems might differ from those that do not in terms of factors like housing quality, energy efficiency investments, environmental preferences, or broader climate adaptation strategies, all of which could independently affect mortality. To isolate the effect of solar PV systems, the researchers used an instrumental variable (IV) strategy within a two-stage least-squares framework. The instrument combines long-term regional differences in solar radiation, which make solar panels more productive in sunnier areas, with lagged regional electricity prices, which increase the savings from self-generated power, to identify the causal effect of solar PV adoption on temperature-related mortality.
The results showed that residential solar PV adoption significantly reduces mortality associated with extreme heat, with the greatest impact observed among older individuals aged 65 and above—the demographic most vulnerable to extreme temperatures. Using the annual average growth rate of solar PV installations during the study period, the researchers estimated that the excess deaths caused by each extremely hot day were reduced by approximately 8.2% per year for the population as a whole, and by 8.5% per year among adults aged 65 and older. The team also found that the protective effects were stronger in prefectures that are warmer, experienced higher post-2011 electricity price spikes, and faced stricter energy conservation targets. In contrast, no comparable effects were observed at cold temperatures, which is consistent with the fact that solar panels generate most power during sunny, hot summers.
These patterns point to a clear mechanism: solar panels make it more affordable for households to keep running air conditioners when cooling is most needed. Consistent with this, prefectures with more solar installations purchased less electricity from the grid on hot days. A placebo analysis further supported this interpretation. Specifically, the protective effects were concentrated in cardiovascular mortality, which is highly sensitive to heat stress, while no statistically significant effects were observed for accident-related deaths.
These findings indicate a previously underexplored strategy through which renewable energy could enhance climate resilience. “Rooftop solar can serve as an effective adaptation strategy against heat-related health risks, on top of its well-known benefits of reducing emissions and enhancing energy security,” remarks Prof. Ko.
However, the benefits may not be distributed equally. The researchers found that solar PV adoption tends to be concentrated in higher-income areas, while the renewable energy surcharge that funds the feed-in tariff for solar power raises electricity prices for all households. Additionally, households without air conditioning or those unable to install panels—such as renters and apartment dwellers—may not benefit directly. The researchers stress the need for targeted policies to reduce inequality and ensure that public health protections reach vulnerable populations.
“Our findings show that homes that generate their own electricity can become part of the infrastructure that protects public health,” notes Prof. Uchida. “With effective policies, residential solar adoption could make societies both greener and more resilient, while narrowing rather than widening health inequalities.”
The researchers note that the findings come from a period of unusually sharp electricity price increases in Japan, when self-generated electricity was especially valuable; similar situations can arise during other energy price shocks, such as those following the Russia–Ukraine conflict. Overall, this study presents distributed household renewable energy adoption in a new light, highlighting its potential not only to enhance energy independence but also to improve public health.
About Professor Shinsuke Uchida from Doshisha University, Japan
Dr. Shinsuke Uchida is currently a Professor at the Faculty of Policy Studies at Doshisha University. He received his Master’s degree from University College London, UK, in 2002 and his Ph.D. from the University of Maryland, USA, in 2014. His research interests include applied econometrics, climate change, natural disasters, and environmental issues in health, agriculture, and economic development. He is a recipient of the 2022 Nagoya City University International Research Collaboration Award and the 2018 University of Verona Internationalisation Programme CooperInt award.
About Assistant Professor Yi-Chun Ko from Asian Growth Research Institute, Japan
Dr. Yi-Chun Ko is currently an Assistant Professor at the Asian Growth Research Institute. She received her Master’s degree and Ph.D. from Tohoku University in 2018 and 2022, respectively. She also serves as a visiting researcher at Tohoku University and a part-time lecturer at Nagoya City University. Her research focuses on environmental economics and applied econometrics, particularly the impacts of climate change and adaptation mechanisms in agriculture, energy, and health. She received the NERPS Best Presentation Award and the Best Speed Talk Award from the Society for Environmental Economics and Policy Studies in 2022.
