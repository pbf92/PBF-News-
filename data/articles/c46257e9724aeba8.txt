TITLE: Advertised product name
OUTLET: PPC Land

Advertised product name is a dimension in Amazon Ads unified reporting that labels each row of performance data with the title of the product an ad promoted. Amazon identifies products by the Amazon Standard Identification Number (ASIN), a 10-character code such as B0XXXXXXXX that is precise but unreadable to most people. The name field exists to do that matching inside the report, so impressions, clicks, cost and sales can be read against a product title rather than a string of characters.
It belongs to a group of nine advertised product attributes that describe the item featured in an ad: ID, name, parent ID, brand, category, subcategory, product group, marketplace and SKU (stock keeping unit). A parallel group of converted product attributes describes the item a shopper actually bought.
Where the field appears
Amazon lists the dimension in its Support Center reference for unified reporting, the single reporting system that opened in beta on November 11, 2025 and reached general availability on June 8, 2026. The reference, updated on November 11, 2026, sets out 156 attributes in 11 categories. Of those, 137 carry a marker for DVA+, the AI-led campaign type that merges buying across Amazon's demand-side platform (DSP), Sponsored Display and Sponsored TV, while 85 apply to Sponsored Products, 81 to Sponsored Display and 73 to Sponsored Brands.
The product category holds 18 attributes. PPC Land's field-by-field reading of the reference found that advertised product attributes apply to DVA+, Sponsored Display and Sponsored Products, but not to Sponsored Brands or Sponsored TV, and that advertised product name "carries the Sponsored Products marker alone." The category totals are consistent with that reading: DVA+ and Sponsored Display each show 17 product attributes, one fewer than Sponsored Products' 18, while Sponsored Brands shows nine, matching the converted product set alone.
The reference also records former names, so that teams moving reports from legacy tools can map old columns to new ones. Here the field is odd. Advertised product name maps to the former sponsored ads column "Advertised SKU," while the separate advertised product SKU field carries no former name. PPC Land listed this among entries on the page that "do not line up." A SKU is a code a seller assigns to its own inventory, not a title, so the mapping as published appears to point a text field at an identifier.
How the value fits a Sponsored Products row
The field is easiest to understand through the ad format it is attached to. In Sponsored Products, Amazon's cost-per-click format for individual listings in search results and on product pages, each ad is a single product. Sellers add products to an ad group by SKU; vendors, who sell wholesale to Amazon, add them by ASIN. That one-to-one structure makes product-level reporting straightforward.
Before unified reporting, the relevant legacy file was the advertised product report, which covers every ASIN that received at least one impression, and its counterpart, the purchased product report. Amazon's Support Center describes the second as detailing "the non-advertised SKUs a customer purchased after clicking your ad." Its columns include Advertised ASIN ("The ASIN associated to an advertised product"), Advertised SKU ("The SKU being advertised") and Purchased ASIN ("Non-advertised ASIN purchased by the customer attributable to your campaign"), with a 60-day lookback. Neither report, as documented, carried a product title column. In the version 3 Reporting API, the spAdvertisedProduct report type exposes advertisedAsin as a column, according to the open-source python-amazon-ad-api documentation, with no title column shown.
Titles came from elsewhere. Asked in October 2023 how to get product names into reports, an Amazon staff member answering on the company's ads-advanced-tools-docs GitHub forum replied: "Yes, you should be able to get product name from ASIN using the product metadata API." The answer added a restriction: "it will only work for ASINs tied to your Amazon advertising account." Agencies and software vendors therefore joined titles to ASIN rows after download. The unified field moves that join inside Amazon's report.
A hypothetical row shows the result. A coffee brand runs a Sponsored Products campaign. The advertised product ID reads B0EXAMPLE1, the advertised product name reads the title as shown on the product detail page, such as "Example Roasters Whole Bean Coffee, Medium Roast, 1 kg," and the brand, category and subcategory fields read Example Roasters, Grocery and Coffee. If a shopper clicked that ad and bought a different roast from the same brand, the converted product fields would name the second item, and the product relevance field would mark the sale as halo rather than direct.
What Amazon has not published is how the name is sourced in time. The reference does not say whether a row shows the title as it stood when the ad was served or the title in the catalogue when the report runs. The difference is material: Amazon limits most titles to 200 characters, and its title rules tightened on January 21, 2025, prompting many sellers to rewrite listings. A report spanning that period could show either version.
The DSP lineage
Amazon's DSP had a product name column long before unified reporting. The DSP product report, as listed by the data connector company Funnel, includes "Product name" alongside ASIN, "Parent ASIN," "Brand name," "Product category," "Product subcategory," "Product group" and "ASIN conversion type." Amazon's August 2022 DSP self-service reporting guide gives "Promoted" and "Brand halo" as values of ASIN conversion type. Brand halo "expands attribution to all ASINs that have the same brand name(s) as the ASINs tracked by the order," according to the guide.
In the unified scheme, those DSP names largely survive on the converted side. PPC Land found that converted product attributes "mostly inherit DVA+ names such as 'Item name,' 'Parent ASIN' and 'Brand name,'" and apply to all five ad products. ASIN conversion type became product relevance. The DSP's view was always about what was bought or viewed after an ad, since display and video orders track lists of products rather than featuring one item per ad. That history may explain why advertised product name carries no DVA+ marker.
Origin and evolution
The building blocks are older than the field. ASINs date to Amazon's early catalogue. Sponsored Products launched in 2012 as Amazon's first self-service search ad format, and ASIN-level reporting followed. The DSP, renamed from Amazon Advertising Platform on September 5, 2018, organised its product report around promoted and brand halo ASINs. In June 2023, Amazon extended the Sponsored Brands Attributed Purchases report to book publishers, giving them an ASIN-level breakdown for campaigns created after February 11, 2023. By October 2023, Amazon staff still described Sponsored Brands as having no ASIN-grain reporting "since there can be multiple ASINs in a single ad."
Unified reporting, announced at unBoxed on November 11, 2025, promised "product-level and audience-level reporting options," according to Amazon's launch notice. The dimensions reference of November 11, 2026 was the first public document to name the advertised product name field. Amazon renamed its DSP to DVA on September 29, 2026, which is why the reference uses DVA+ rather than DSP in its product markers.
Why it matters
Merchandising, pricing and inventory decisions are made by product, and the people making them rarely memorise ASINs. A readable label lets an analyst sort a report by spend and spot, say, out-of-season items among the top ten advertised products without a lookup step.
The field also sharpens the advertised-versus-converted question. Amazon's attribution credits sales of products other than the one advertised, and the gap can be large in categories with many variants. Placing advertised product name beside converted product name in one row shows whether an ad for a flagship item is mostly selling accessories or cheaper alternatives.
Limitations and disputes
Coverage is the first limit. The field applies only to Sponsored Products, according to PPC Land's reading of the reference, leaving DVA+ and Sponsored Display users with the advertised product ID but no matching title field, while Sponsored Brands carries no advertised product attributes at all. Its ads feature several products at once.
The former-name mapping to "Advertised SKU" is unexplained, and Amazon has not said whether it is an error. Titles are also an unstable key. Sellers change them, the same title can apply to several variants, and titles differ across marketplaces and languages. Any analysis that groups by name rather than by advertised product ID risks merging or splitting products incorrectly. Finally, Amazon has not documented how the field behaves when the advertiser does not own the catalogue record, the restriction its staff described for the product metadata API in 2023.
How it differs from related fields
- Advertised product ID is the ASIN of the featured product. It is the stable key; the name is a label for people.
- Advertised product SKU is the seller's own inventory code. It applies to DVA+, Sponsored Display and Sponsored Products, and has no former name in the reference.
- Converted product name is the title of the item bought after an ad interaction, inherited from the DSP's "Item name," and available for all five ad products.
- Ad name is the label an advertiser gives an ad or creative, not the product it features.
Recent developments
PPC Land's November 12 analysis set out the product category's 18 attributes and the "Advertised SKU" mismatch a day after Amazon published the reference. Also on November 12, Amazon moved the shutdown of legacy reporting from December 31, 2026 to January 31, 2027, with creation and editing of legacy reports stopping on December 17, 2026. From January 1, 2027, display impressions in DVA will count only once on screen, and Amazon expects counts to fall by about 63% on average, which will change impression-based figures beside product rows on the DVA side.
