TITLE: In B2B, the safe brand beats the better product, LinkedIn argues
OUTLET: PPC Land

Companies buying from other companies do not always pick the best product on offer. They pick the one they can defend if it goes wrong. That is the argument of a question-and-answer post LinkedIn's B2B Institute published on November 23, 2026, featuring Dr. Marcus Collins, Clinical Assistant Professor of Marketing at the University of Michigan's Ross School of Business.
The post, titled "Why Better Products Can Lose and More Buyable B2B Brands Win," was written by Mimi Turner, Global Head of Thought Leadership Strategy at the LinkedIn B2B Institute, and sits in the Research and insights section of the LinkedIn Marketing Blog. It rests on a survey of 750 senior B2B buyers and on follow-up interviews. It also works as a trailer. Collins has written up the full findings for MIT Sloan Management Review, in an article titled "How B2B Marketers Misunderstand Their Customers" and dated September 2026, and twice the post tells readers that a key number is in that article rather than on the page.
In Short
LinkedIn published an interview with marketing professor Marcus Collins arguing that business buyers often choose the vendor they can justify to colleagues, not the one with the strongest product. That matters to anyone selling to companies, because the people who help approve a purchase, such as finance chiefs and procurement teams, rarely click on ads or download sales material. The post gives one survey figure - 34% for being able to defend the decision - and sends readers to a separate journal article for the rest of the numbers.
What LinkedIn published
The post opens with a two-sentence summary: "Marketers are selling capability. But B2B buyers want defensibility." Turner then sets out the research behind it. According to the post, LinkedIn asked "750 senior B2B buyers from around the world to identify the most important emotional jobs to be done they needed to feel confident picking a vendor from a shortlist." In product research, a "job to be done" is the underlying goal a customer is trying to achieve, as opposed to the product feature they end up buying. Here the jobs are emotional ones: feeling safe, feeling supported, feeling that colleagues agree.
Turner wrote that she and her colleague Jann Schwarz collaborated with Collins, whom she describes as "the storied author, academic and brand strategist," as part of a wider LinkedIn programme on "how to build more buyable B2B brands." Collins is the author of "For the Culture: The Power Behind What We Buy, What We Do, and Who We Want to Be," published by PublicAffairs in 2023. The interview itself is short, six questions, and Turner says she "pushed him to talk more about the parts that surprised us both."
Turner's job title differs from earlier coverage. When PPC Land reported on LinkedIn and Bain's "Principles of Buyability" in June, it described her as Head of Marketplace Innovation at LinkedIn.
The page ends with a sales pitch. Below the related articles sits a box reading "Do business where business is done," with the line "Reach the right audience with LinkedIn ads" and a "Create an ad" button. The research comes from a company that sells the advertising its conclusions point towards, and that context matters when reading what follows.
The one figure the post shows: 34%
The post's only published statistic sits in a chart credited to Bain & Company and LinkedIn. It is headed "Four out of the top five emotional 'jobs to be done' are relational and organizational." The chart ranks five items. Only the first carries a value.
The chart's footnote defines the measure: "% who felt 'Extremely confident' that this dimension influenced their decision-making." So the 34% is the share of the 750 buyers who gave the top rating to defensibility as an influence on their choice. Defensibility led the list. Even so, about two-thirds of respondents did not give it that top rating - that is PPC Land's reading of the footnote, not a claim the post makes. Only one of the five items is about the product itself - confidence that it would do the job - and it came second.
Turner's reading of the chart is that "four out of the top five reasons were to do with relationships, and those relationships were mostly within their own organization." The post does not give fieldwork dates, the countries covered, the industries surveyed, the full list of jobs tested or the values of the other four bars.
Mr. Spock goes to the office
Collins's central claim is that B2B marketing is built on a wrong picture of the buyer. Asked what assumption marketers get wrong, he described a split personality. "That in their personal lives, buyers are recognizably human: swayed by emotion, by the people around them, by all the things B2C marketing has understood for decades. Then they walk into the office and become something else. Fully rational, feature-driven, immune to everything that moved them an hour earlier."
He put it in fictional terms. "It's a Jekyll and Hyde story, except Hyde is Mr. Spock. A creature that is driven by entirely rational, logical and selfless reasoning. Almost every B2B marketing program I've seen is built on it."
Why has a decade of new tools not changed that? According to Collins, "the changes were all executional. The channels went digital, the data made targeting sharper, the measurement got more precise. All genuinely better, but none of it required anyone to revisit the premise underneath." His conclusion is one of the sharper lines in the interview: "You can get dramatically more efficient at reaching the wrong person with the wrong argument."
Why the safe choice wins
The heart of the argument is about blame. "Because nobody in that room is only deciding what to buy. They're also deciding what they can defend," Collins said. "If you buy from an established, reputable company and it underperforms, that's bad luck. Nobody blames you. If you buy from a young startup and it underperforms, your judgment is the thing under review."
That imbalance, he said, "sits in the room whether or not anyone says it out loud, and it means the more defensible option frequently wins over the stronger value proposition." The follow-up interviews added a practical point: "Our follow-up interviews suggested it's also simply easier to get the defensible option through procurement." The post does not say how many interviews were held or who took part.
The idea is not new to LinkedIn's research. In June, the "Principles of Buyability" paper gave the fear a name, FOMU, or fear of messing up, and blamed it for stalling 40% of B2B deals. Earlier that month, a breakdown of figures from a LinkedIn summit said that 40% of B2B deals die from indecision rather than from losing to a rival. What the November post adds is Collins's framing, and his claim that the effect reaches past the shortlist into procurement.
The buyers marketing never reaches
The finding Collins said surprised him most concerns people who never show up in marketing data. Most B2B campaigns aim at the known prospect, the technical expert whose clicks, downloads and form fills register in a conversion funnel. But the wider buying group also includes senior staff who are rarely targeted.
"But the decision is also being shaped by a COO, a CFO, someone in procurement," Collins said, referring to chief operating officers, chief financial officers and purchasing teams. "People who typically don't have a nuanced grasp of the value propositions being compared. They don't download white papers. They don't attend webinars. They don't appear on lead sheets or pipeline trackers. They are, in marketing terms, invisible, and they are not neutral."
For marketers who measure success through leads, that is an awkward point. Campaigns judged on form fills and sales pipeline will, by design, say little about people who never fill in a form.
Two numbers the post holds back
The post twice describes a finding without giving the figure. The first concerns brand familiarity: "We also measured how often the eventual winner was already known to everyone with a voice in the decision. The number is in Marcus's article, and it makes a strong case for brand budgets." The second concerns the hidden buyers: "We put a number on how much these hidden buyers' perception of your brand shapes the outcome. It's in the article, and it's big." A third point, what buyability actually depends on, is also left to the journal piece, which Collins called "the part of the research I'd most want people to read for themselves."
The post links to the MIT Sloan Management Review article through the journal's online store rather than to a free page.
LinkedIn has, however, published figures on both questions before. Its June 11 "Principles of Buyability" paper, produced with Bain & Company, said 81% of purchases were made from vendors that "almost everyone" in the buyer group already knew, and only 4% from vendors known only to the team recommending the purchase. That paper also said vendors are 20 times more likely to be chosen when the entire buyer group knows and trusts the brand at the start, compared with when only the technical champion does, and that finance, legal and procurement staff hold roughly 50% of decision-making influence. The November post does not say whether the journal article uses the same figures or newer ones.
The last of the earlier figures came from LinkedIn's three-phase B2B launch framework, which cited Bain data showing that 86% of B2B buyers begin with a shortlist of names they already trust and 81% end up buying from it. Bain itself published a related brief on June 19, 2026, "Raising the Odds on a Deal: How Likelihood to Buy Rewires B2B Growth," which argues that most deals that look lost were never winnable because the vendor never made the buyer's first shortlist.
What "buyability" means
LinkedIn has built a research programme around the word buyability, and Collins offered a short definition of it. "It's the confidence to actually make the purchase. It's not awareness and it's not intent. It's the gap between 'this vendor clears the bar' and 'I'll put my name on this.'"
The distinction matters because most B2B measurement tracks the first two: brand awareness and purchase intent, the likelihood that someone will buy. Collins argues that a vendor can score well on both and still lose when a decision-maker will not attach their name to it. "Existing vocabulary didn't cover that gap, which is probably why so few marketing programs are built to close it," he said.
LinkedIn's wider version of the idea, set out in the June 19 buyability paper Turner wrote with Jann Martin Schwarz, was produced with NewtonX and Collins and drew on three years of joint research with Bain. That paper said 94% of B2B buying groups now consult large language models before contacting sales, and that a recommendation from a peer is more than three times as influential as a better product or price. It ranked being able to defend the decision above confidence in the product, the same order shown in the November chart.
Collins's one change
Asked what a chief marketing officer should change after reading the research, Collins gave a direct answer. "Stop marketing exclusively to the person who answers your emails. Work out who else is in that room, what they value, and what would make choosing you the easy thing to defend." He went further: "That's a bigger determinant of who wins the deal than whether your product is cheaper, faster or more efficient."
That is a strong claim, and the post does not show the number behind it. It points to the journal article.
