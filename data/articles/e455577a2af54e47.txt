TITLE: Model sues RomperJack after 2021 photos became AI-generated Instagram ads
OUTLET: PPC Land

A Los Angeles model and actor filed a federal lawsuit on November 18, 2026 against Cooper Street, LLC, the Texas company that trades as the clothing brand RomperJack, alleging that it took still photographs from a 2021 shoot he was never paid cash for and turned them into artificial intelligence-generated images and video for its social media advertising.
Jeffrey S. Stanfield's 17-page complaint, filed in the United States District Court for the Central District of California as case 2:26-cv-13465, pleads five claims, led by false endorsement under federal trademark law. Its central factual point comes from the defendant's own side. According to the complaint, RomperJack's owner, Emmanuel Sullivan, "acknowledged in correspondence that the artificial-intelligence enhancements were created after" he acquired the business in January 2025. The challenged content, the filing argues, "was not an inherited 2021 photograph passively maintained in an archive. It was new advertising created and published by the current operation."
Everything that follows is allegation. The complaint is the plaintiff's account, no response from Cooper Street is on the docket, and none of the claims has been tested in court.
In Short
A model says a clothing brand took photos from a 2021 shoot he did in exchange for clothes, used AI to turn them into new pictures and a video of him moving and reacting, and ran them as Instagram ads after the brand changed hands. The case matters to brands, agencies and anyone buying a company with an archive of old photography, because it tests whether loose permission for a still photo can stretch to a synthetic performance the person never gave. He wants damages, the company's profits and a court order barring any AI depiction of him without his written consent, and the company has not yet answered.
The complaint
The case is captioned Jeffrey S. Stanfield v. Cooper Street, LLC d/b/a RomperJack; and Does 1 through 10. The complaint is dated November 17, 2026 and was entered on the docket on November 18, together with a civil cover sheet, a request for summons and a notice of interested parties. The docket classifies the case under nature of suit code 840, trademark, with the cause listed as 15 U.S.C. § 1125. Stanfield paid a $405 filing fee and demands a jury trial. He is represented by Joseph N. Casas of Talent Rights Law, the trading name of The Casas Law Firm, PC, in Beverly Hills.
Four exhibits were filed as separate attachments: screenshots from the 2021 session, two Instagram advertisements and a still image from a video advertisement. The complaint describes them but does not reproduce them.
The five claims combine a federal false endorsement theory with four California law theories, including the statutory and common law versions of the right of publicity.
Federal jurisdiction rests on the Lanham Act claim, with the state claims attached under supplemental jurisdiction. Stanfield asks for assignment to the court's Western Division because he lives in West Hollywood.
Who is suing, and what his face is worth
Stanfield, according to the complaint, "is a professional model and actor who earns income by licensing his image, likeness, identity, and performance to commercial brands." When he discovered the RomperJack advertisements, the filing says, he was appearing in three national advertising campaigns, two commercials and one print campaign. None of those brands is named.
The complaint puts numbers on the market it says was taken from him. "His ordinary modeling day rate is approximately $2,500," it states. "Depending on the scope, duration, media, territory, and exclusivity of a negotiated license, his prior commercial usage fees have ranged from approximately $2,500 to $10,000."
Why does a model with active campaigns care about one apparel brand's Instagram posts? The filing's answer is control. "Unauthorized advertising can create conflicts with existing or prospective campaigns, cause viewers and industry professionals to believe he approved a brand relationship that he did not approve, and impair the market value of his identity," it says.
The 2021 shoot
The origin of the photographs is informal by any measure. In or about January 2021, according to the complaint, Stanfield took part in an outdoor still photography session near the Santa Monica Pier involving RomperJack apparel. A friend arranged it through Samir Janjua, described as a Los Angeles-area commercial photographer.
The complaint lists what did not happen. "Mr. Stanfield had no direct communications with RomperJack, signed no model release, made no oral agreement granting usage rights, and received no cash compensation. He received clothing and copies of photographs from the session." It adds that he "did not sign a model release, license, waiver, work-for-hire agreement, or other writing granting RomperJack a right to use his image or likeness."
Clothing and prints, the filing argues, settle nothing about later uses: "Neither item conveyed a perpetual advertising license or consent to create synthetic or derivative performances." That language goes to the heart of a long-running fight over usage rights, where perpetual terms are the sharpest point of dispute and AI rights the newest. Here there is no contract to interpret at all.
The complaint also pleads a fallback. "Even if Defendants could prove some narrow implied permission to use one or more original still photographs in connection with the 2021 session, which Plaintiff denies, that permission would not include later republication by a new owner in new campaigns, the creation of synthetic derivative content, or the fabrication of an audiovisual performance Mr. Stanfield never gave."
A new owner, and new ads
Cooper Street, LLC is a Texas limited liability company formed on November 6, 2024, according to the complaint, which says the identities of its members are unknown to the plaintiff. Upon information and belief, it operates the romperjack.com online store and the brand's social media accounts. RomperJack, through Sullivan, represented that the business was acquired from Justin Clark in January 2025. The complaint gives no purchase price and does not say whether the deal transferred assets or ownership interests.
The acquisition is where the complaint draws its line. After the purchase, it says, the new owner "had control over whether inherited photographs would be used, removed, relicensed, altered, or transformed into new advertisements. Defendants could have requested a written release from Mr. Stanfield before using his identity. They did not." The conclusion follows: "Defendants therefore cannot attribute the challenged synthetic advertisements solely to a predecessor's historical conduct."
Sullivan acknowledged, according to the filing, that a RomperJack Instagram post depicting Stanfield appeared on June 13, 2025.
The advertisements
The complaint describes three pieces of content. The first, Exhibit B, is an Instagram advertisement that "used an artificial-intelligence-generated depiction of Mr. Stanfield and another model against a fabricated beach setting." Its copy read: "OWN THE STYLE. SHOP THE ROMPER NOW."
The second, Exhibit C, "used an artificial-intelligence-generated, full-body depiction of Mr. Stanfield alone," with the lines "Tired of the same old summer outfit?" and "Meet your one-piece upgrade." This version, the complaint says, "altered details of his body, pose, facial expression, lighting, and surrounding environment while retaining his readily identifiable face, physique, clothing, and persona."
The third is a video. According to the complaint, it "placed Mr. Stanfield into simulated motion and created new facial expressions, gestures, and audiovisual conduct that did not occur during the 2021 still-photography session." It "depicted Mr. Stanfield in fabricated movements and reactions to another speaker." Only a single still from it, Exhibit D, is before the court.
That video is the most unusual allegation in the case. Disputes over brands reusing a model's old photos are common; a claim that a brand generated a moving, reacting performance from stills is not. "The source photographs and the challenged advertisements are not the same works," the complaint says. "Defendants used generative tools to create new scenes and performances from Mr. Stanfield's identity and then presented the synthetic output as commercial advertising." The filing does not name the tool.
On distribution, the complaint is explicit about what it does not know. Upon information and belief, it says, the content ran "through Instagram, Facebook, and other digital channels, including paid or boosted advertising," driving traffic to the online store. "The full duration, targeting, impressions, advertising spend, engagement, and resulting revenue are within Defendants' possession and control."
How the complaint traces the path from shoot to ad
According to the complaint, the challenged content moved through six stages:
- The shoot. Still photographs taken in or about January 2021 near the Santa Monica Pier, arranged through photographer Samir Janjua, with Stanfield compensated in clothing and copies of the photographs.
- The change of ownership. Cooper Street, formed in November 2024, takes over RomperJack in January 2025, gaining control of the website, social media and advertising accounts, customer data and brand assets.
- Selection. The new operation "selected photographs depicting Mr. Stanfield and caused new advertising materials to be created from them."
- Generation. AI-generated still images with fabricated beach settings and altered bodies, poses and expressions, plus a video with simulated movement, created after the acquisition according to Sullivan's correspondence.
- Publication. Posts on RomperJack's commercial accounts carrying the account name, the apparel and sales copy, with, upon information and belief, paid or boosted placement across Instagram, Facebook and other channels.
- Removal. After a June 23, 2026 demand letter, the defendants said they removed the challenged materials; the complaint says they kept the source photographs, the generated assets and control of the accounts.
The consent dispute
The complaint previews the defense it expects. According to the filing, RomperJack responded to the demand letter by saying it "had relied on materials inherited from the prior owner." The defendants also asserted, the complaint says, "that a photographer reported a verbal arrangement." Sullivan's correspondence is not attached, so these positions are known only as the plaintiff characterizes them.
Stanfield denies any such agreement and attacks its scope. The asserted arrangement, the complaint says, "does not identify any words spoken by Mr. Stanfield, any duration, territory, medium, product, compensation term, or grant of synthetic-media rights." And even a real one would fall short: "A purported verbal understanding for clothing and photographs in exchange for some use of original still images would not authorize the post-acquisition creation of artificial-intelligence-generated images, fabricated video performances, or new commercial advertisements years later."
The complaint also confronts an argument a defendant might raise about AI alteration. "The common-law tort protects identity, not merely an unaltered photograph," it says. "Defendants cannot avoid liability by using artificial intelligence to transform the source photograph while retaining and exploiting the identity that gives the advertisement its value." Under the statutory claim, it adds: "The artificial-intelligence generation did not remove his identity. It appropriated and transformed it while preserving the features necessary for viewers to recognize him."
