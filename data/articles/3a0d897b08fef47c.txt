TITLE: Ireland's DPC delayed or cancelled 9 AI launches in five years of reviews
OUTLET: PPC Land

Ireland's Data Protection Commission published a 75-page report on September 25, 2026 setting out how it examined roughly 180 artificial intelligence products and services from companies including Meta, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, TikTok and X between 2021 and 2025, most of them before they reached European users. Nine launches were delayed or cancelled because of the regulator's interventions. Transparency problems appeared in 72% of the recommendations it issued, and the report closes with a position that reaches beyond its five-year window: training AI on private messages, the DPC says, faces a legal threshold that will be "very difficult to achieve."
In Short
Ireland's privacy regulator has published five years of figures on how it reviewed about 180 AI products from Meta, Google, OpenAI, X and others, mostly before launch. It matters because the DPC is the lead EU regulator for many of the large platforms that sell advertising in Europe, so its views on AI training, opt-outs and transparency travel with those platforms across the bloc. Nine launches were delayed or cancelled, 59% of AI products launched in 2024 and 2025 received recommendations, and the DPC now says a company is unlikely to be able to justify training AI on private messages under legitimate interests.
What the report is
The document is titled "Responsible Artificial Intelligence Innovation: Insights from the Data Protection Commission's Supervision of AI (2021-2025)" and carries a September 2026 date on its cover. It is not a decision, a fine or a guideline. It records the work of one team, the Technology Multinational Supervision Unit (TMSU), which sits inside the DPC's Supervision Function and whose remit, according to the report, "is to engage with the large multinational tech companies based in Ireland." Its engagement statistics all come from that unit's internal records.
That distinction matters. Supervision, according to the DPC, "operates completely separately from other regulatory functions in the DPC, such as those involved in complaints or inquiries." It does not look backwards at alleged infringements. It engages "on a voluntary basis, primarily before a product or service launches," and the report describes the result as a "quasi-regulatory sandbox."
The companies named include several of the largest sellers of digital advertising. According to the DPC, the Supervision Function engaged on "the creation, training, and deployment of AI by companies, including Airbnb, Apple, Deepseek, Google, LinkedIn, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Pinterest, TikTok, X (formerly Twitter), and others." The technologies covered include large language models, age assurance, facial recognition, recommender systems, personalisation and agents.
Most case studies in the report do not name the company involved. Meta and X, under its former name Twitter, are the exceptions, and Google appears in the list of open inquiries.
The numbers
The headline figure is growth. According to the report, "from 2021 to 2025, the DPC Supervision Function has seen a tenfold increase in AI-related engagements with large technology controllers." A footnote records that preliminary figures given to the Oireachtas Joint Committee on Artificial Intelligence on February 24, 2026 had described "over a six-fold increase." The final number uses the full calendar year of 2025.
Generative AI drove most of it. The DPC counted 25 generative AI engagements in 2023, 36 in 2024 and 82 in 2025, for 143 in total, against 37 non-generative engagements over the whole period. Generative AI therefore accounted for 79% of all AI engagements, even though, according to the report, generative engagements only began in 2023.
The report breaks the engagements down by technology type, with some engagements counted under more than one type.
Advertising appears as its own category in seven engagements, and personalisation in 17. The report does not say which products those were.
One detail in the denominators deserves a note. The "1 in 4" figure for 2025 refers to engagements "with large technology controllers," while the totals plotted in the report's second chart reflect, according to its note, "all engagements with controllers and other stakeholders as reported in the DPC Annual Report." The ratio and the chart do not share a base, and the report does not print the yearly AI counts behind its first chart.
The report also sets this activity against wider adoption data. Citing Eurostat, it says AI use among EU enterprises rose from 13.5% to 20% in 2025, and that "52.7% of EU firms which considered using AI technologies and chose not to, cited data protection and privacy concerns as a reason why."
How a Supervision engagement works
According to the DPC, a Supervision engagement follows six stages:
- "An open and honest conversation regarding a controller's intended processing or the capabilities of launches, products, services, etc. that will process personal data."
- Questions from the DPC and answers from the controller.
- Engagement with peer EU regulators "to identify issues of concern." For high-profile matters, the unit holds what the report calls "flash meetings" early on.
- Recommendations, where necessary, "that indicate areas for improvement or point out likely issues for compliance."
- Follow-up by the controller on those recommendations.
- "Ongoing monitoring by the DPC post-launch."
For AI, the review may cover a Data Protection Impact Assessment, a Legitimate Interests Assessment and other risk documents, and may extend to monitoring how mitigations work in practice, "such as the operation of an objection form for compliance with GDPR Article 21." The process is voluntary and, according to the report, distinct from "the more formal prior consultation under Article 36 of the GDPR."
There is a limit written into the model. "The DPC will never approve or state that a process, product, or service is compliant with the GDPR through Supervision," the report says. The recommendations are "non-binding in nature," although where "redline issues" are found, the unit "can indicate that the DPC may consider other regulatory powers if the controller decides not to implement the recommendations."
Timing is the other variable. Most engagements, the DPC says, happened "shortly before the anticipated launch," and it considers "a minimum 8-week lead time is necessary to enable the DPC to engage substantively." Controllers that engaged "8 weeks or earlier, with good documentation have been predominantly able to launch without delay due to DPC engagement."
The report also states a position on impact assessments. The DPC "considers that the training of a new AI model will likely require a Data Protection Impact Assessment," because training a new model with personal data "will necessarily involve a new technology and likely large quantities of data" - two criteria drawn from the Article 29 Working Party's 2017 guidelines, WP248 rev.01.
What the recommendations said
The report quotes several recommendations verbatim, without naming the recipients. Among them:
- "The DPC recommends that clear retention periods or criteria to determine such periods, be developed and provided to users with regard to the transcription of audio data."
- "The DPC recommends that additional transparency notices should be published prior to the creation of the datasets that will be used for training and testing of the models."
- "If the controller intends to continue to process any personal data of users who object to the processing, the controller must demonstrate compelling legitimate grounds prior to launching in the EU/EEA."
- "The DPC recommends a critical re-evaluation and adjustment of the consent flow, to ensure that it meets the requirements of 'freely given' consent under Article 7(4) of the GDPR."
- "The DPC recommends that the controller develop and apply measures to remove, pseudonymise, anonymise, or otherwise protect full names of private individuals from the dataset prior to any AI training."
- "The DPC recommends that the controller take immediate steps to avoid collecting or processing personal data from pages, groups and elsewhere on the platform that would have a likely prevalence of special category data, or data capable of revealing special category data."
The analysis passed to controllers can be blunt. According to the report, it may include "Stating the DPC's opinion that a controller's legitimate interests balancing test has not been met" or "Informing a controller that the compatibility assessment of processing purposes has not been met."
The five issues cited most often in AI recommendations were transparency, technical and organisational measures, Article 25 (data protection by design and default), data minimisation and legal basis. On PPC Land's reading of the report's unlabelled bar chart, transparency was cited roughly four times as often as the next issue. The full ranking continues with objection, children-focused issues, fairness, retention, data subject rights, Article 9, purpose and compatibility, Article 35 and DPIAs, non-user data subjects, notice, Article 22 and the Article 15 right of access.
When engagement turns into enforcement
The DPC is careful to present Supervision as cooperative. Yet the report also catalogues where cooperation ran out.
The sharpest example involves X. According to the report, the DPC learned in July 2024 "that key mitigation measures were not in place with regards to Twitter training its Grok AI model using user personal data." The DPC asked Twitter to stop. "Twitter refused," the report says. On August 8, 2024, the regulator applied to the Irish High Court under section 134 of the Data Protection Act 2018, a power that lets the Commission seek an order "suspending, restricting or prohibiting the processing of personal data" where there is "an urgent need to act." Following a hearing, Twitter "undertook to cease processing user data that had been collected during the period of time when the mitigations were not in place, and to delete the datasets that included such data." Twitter's European entity was rebranded X Internet Unlimited Company in April 2025, according to the report.

Post-launch monitoring produced its own example. In January 2025, according to the report, the Supervision Function found that the opt-out from AI training for "a particular chatbot was not available to non-subscribers using mobile devices, although access to the chatbot was available to all users." The cause was a bug. "Approximately 7.7 million mobile users in the EEA could potentially have been impacted," the report says. A fix was in place by February 2025, and the controller "committed to not training AI on any data collected during the period when the opt out was unavailable." The chatbot is not named.
Since 2023, the DPC has also required periodic reports from controllers building and deploying large language models. These rely on live data to evaluate the performance of the models, the performance of safeguards, "reactions of actual individuals or the wider public," and other real-world privacy issues. Each report has required the controller to re-evaluate its DPIA and LIA against what actually happened. In one case cited, the controller identified additional data points to exclude from training, fixed output errors reported by users and added work "to identify and block hallucinations."
