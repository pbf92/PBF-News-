TITLE: Review Links Sjögren’s Disease Autoimmunity to Heightened Osteoporosis and Fracture Risks
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive narrative review evaluating the clinical and biological intersection of autoimmunity and skeletal health indicates that Sjögren’s disease serves as an independent risk factor for systemic bone loss and fragility fractures. Synthesizing data across pathophysiological investigations, observational cohorts, and clinical guidelines published between 2000 and 2025, researchers detailed how the chronic autoimmune condition compromises bone integrity. Osteoporosis, pathologically characterized by decreased bone mineral density and the progressive deterioration of skeletal microarchitecture, frequently targets postmenopausal and middle-aged women. In individuals diagnosed with primary Sjögren’s disease, this bone deterioration is substantially compounded by systemic immune dysregulation, chronic metabolic disturbances, and secondary complications arising from long-term clinical therapies, creating a substantial risk of severe skeletal injury that conventional diagnostic pathways regularly underestimate.
The review outlines the formal search framework employed by the authors to gather evidence on bone metabolism in Sjögren’s disease across international biomedical databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. By querying controlled vocabulary and keyword combinations addressing autoimmune exocrinopathy alongside bone mineral density, osteoprotegerin signaling, cytokine cascades, and diagnostic tools, the investigators screened relevant human research published in the English language. Foundational studies published prior to 2000 were incorporated strictly where necessary to clarify core biochemical mechanisms. From the identified literature, 38 primary references met predefined inclusion standards, covering cohort studies, case-control analyses, clinical trials, and clinical practice guidelines, while excluding animal experiments, unverified in vitro models, and small case series of fewer than ten patients that did not involve rare skeletal pathologies.
Epidemiological syntheses within the review demonstrate that prevalence rates for combined osteopenia and osteoporosis among patients with Sjögren’s disease range between 33.1 percent and 51.6 percent across international cohorts. Because Sjögren’s disease exhibits a strong demographic predilection for women, maintaining an established female-to-male clinical ratio spanning approximately 9:1 to 10:1, the intersection of autoimmune processes with postmenopausal estrogen deprivation generates a heightened risk profile. Mendelian randomization analyses cited in the paper confirm a direct genetic causal link, showing that Sjögren’s disease inherently acts as an independent risk factor for osteoporosis with an odds ratio of 1.191, rather than functioning merely as an indirect surrogate for other clinical variables. In a documented cohort of 128 patients in China, only 15.6 percent retained normal bone mineral density, while 32.8 percent demonstrated osteopenia and 51.6 percent had established osteoporosis, with bone impairment reaching 39.5 percent in postmenopausal individuals compared to 26.1 percent in premenopausal subjects.
The authors emphasize that significant methodological differences account for the wide variation in reported osteoporosis rates between different geographic populations and clinical registries. Densitometric definitions vary across published studies, as the standard World Health Organization T-score threshold of minus 2.5 or lower applies strictly to postmenopausal women and men aged 50 or older, whereas younger cohorts require Z-score comparisons against age-matched references. Furthermore, skeletal sites evaluated across lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip measurements yield divergent classifications, particularly when degenerative spinal alterations in older adults artificially inflate lumbar bone density values. Discrepancies also stem from the reliance on international reference datasets versus regional Asian population databases, as well as shifts in clinical diagnostic criteria over time, transitioning from the 2002 American-European Consensus Group standards to the 2016 ACR/EULAR criteria across heterogeneous patient populations.
At the cellular level, the pathogenesis of skeletal fragility in Sjögren’s disease is driven by osteoimmunology, wherein heightened inflammatory cascades uncouple normal bone remodeling by simultaneously promoting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and blunting osteoblast-driven bone formation. The primary regulatory axis involves receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B ligand, or RANKL, which is abundantly secreted by activated memory B cells, T cells, osteocytes, and stromal tissues. RANKL binds to its cognate RANK receptor on osteoclast precursor cells, stimulating intracellular signaling via TRAF6 and downstream transcription factors NFATc1, c-Fos, and NF-kappa B. In healthy tissue, osteoprotegerin acts as an antagonist decoy receptor that neutralizes RANKL, but autoimmune inflammation skews the RANKL-to-osteoprotegerin ratio upward. This pro-resorptive state is amplified by elevated systemic pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and interleukin-17, alongside the concurrent suppression of mesenchymal stem cell differentiation into osteoblasts through the upregulation of Wnt signaling inhibitors such as dickkopf-related protein 1.
Extraglandular renal manifestations represent another primary physiological driver of accelerated bone demineralization in primary Sjögren’s disease, occurring in approximately 5 to 10 percent of patients predominantly as tubulointerstitial nephritis. The most frequent renal complication is distal renal tubular acidosis, which stems from autoimmune-mediated dysfunction of the proton-secreting alpha-intercalated cells within the renal collecting ducts. Because affected kidneys cannot lower urinary pH below 5.3 despite systemic metabolic acidosis, the human body compensates by mobilizing alkaline calcium carbonate and phosphate salts from bone tissue to buffer circulating hydrogen ions. This proton exchange directly strips calcium from the skeletal matrix, producing persistent hypercalciuria, hypophosphatemia, and hypokalemia. The resulting high-turnover metabolic bone disturbance ranges from severe osteoporosis to osteomalacia, occasionally presenting in young or pediatric patients with severe pathological fractures that rapidly resolve only after targeted systemic alkali therapy and nutritional repletion.
Compounding this skeletal vulnerability, serum concentrations of 25-hydroxyvitamin D are substantially reduced in patients with Sjögren’s disease relative to healthy controls, impairing crucial immunomodulatory pathways. Vitamin D deficiency arises from a convergence of factors: behavioral avoidance of sunlight due to cutaneous photosensitivity, defective renal conversion of precursor molecules into active 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D caused by tubulointerstitial nephritis, and accelerated metabolite catabolism driven by chronic systemic inflammation. Through the vitamin D receptor expressed on lymphocytes and macrophages, active vitamin D normally suppresses Th1 and Th17 cell responses, dampens interleukin production, and moderates autoantibody generation by B cells. A large real-world TriNetX cohort study encompassing 19,177 subjects identified that vitamin D deficiency in Sjögren’s disease correlates with increased risks of all-cause mortality, major adverse cardiovascular events, and major adverse kidney events, highlighting that systemic chronic inflammation drives pervasive multi-organ vulnerability.
Pharmacological treatments utilized in clinical rheumatology present distinct, opposing influences on skeletal mineral maintenance. Systemic glucocorticoid administration, frequently prescribed for extraglandular complications including inflammatory arthritis and pulmonary involvement, represents a prominent iatrogenic driver of bone loss. Glucocorticoids stimulate osteoclast activity while suppressing osteoblast survival and disrupting intestinal and renal mineral handling, with daily dosages exceeding 7.5 milligrams producing rapid spikes in fracture susceptibility even at bone densities higher than conventional osteoporotic thresholds. Conversely, hydroxychloroquine, widely used for dryness, fatigue, and systemic discomfort, displays potential bone-sparing properties. In a cohort of 54 primary Sjögren’s patients, 69 percent of those receiving hydroxychloroquine exhibited higher bone mineral density at the lumbar spine and femoral neck, an observation corroborated in systemic lupus erythematosus cohorts and thought to be mediated by the alleviation of background systemic inflammation.
Diagnostic assessment of fracture risk in Sjögren’s disease faces major limitations when reliant solely on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and the Fracture Risk Assessment Tool, known as FRAX. Clinical evidence reveals that FRAX consistently underestimates the actual probability of fragility fractures in primary Sjögren’s cohorts, failing to capture microstructural damage occurring in patients who do not cross the standard densitometric threshold of minus 2.5. To bridge this diagnostic gap, the review highlights the Trabecular Bone Score, a software analysis utilizing grey-level texture extraction from standard lumbar DXA scans to measure bone microarchitecture independently of density. Furthermore, research utilizing high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography has identified localized bone erosions in 56.7 percent of evaluated patients, particularly within the metacarpal heads of older postmenopausal women, challenging the traditional paradigm that Sjögren’s musculoskeletal disease is strictly non-erosive.
Comprehensive management of bone health in Sjögren’s disease necessitates an integrated, multidisciplinary regimen tailored to overcome the daily barriers imposed by sicca symptoms and persistent fatigue. Non-pharmacological approaches incorporate anti-inflammatory dietary strategies, structured intake of calcium reaching 1000 to 1200 milligrams daily in adults, and targeted vitamin D supplementation of 600 to 800 international units daily, with adaptations like sauces and moisture-rich meals to aid ingestion impaired by severe xerostomia. Exercise programs following structured physiological frameworks, including the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia pacing protocol, provide physical conditioning to counteract skeletal unloading without exacerbating chronic autonomic dysfunction and autoimmune fatigue. The authors conclude that proactive screening combining early urinary electrolyte monitoring, bone density scans supplemented by Trabecular Bone Score assessments, and coordinated metabolic interventions offers the most reliable clinical strategy to avert debilitating skeletal fractures.
