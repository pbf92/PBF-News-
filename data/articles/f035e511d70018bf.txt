TITLE: Voting sites faced 180 times more hack attempts than DDoS, Cloudflare finds
OUTLET: PPC Land

Government election websites in the United States drew website vulnerability attempts 180 times more often than denial-of-service attacks during the 2026 midterm cycle, according to an analysis Cloudflare published today. The company, which says it helped protect more than 1,400 election-related websites through three free programs, found that attackers spent the year probing state and local voting sites for a way into internal systems and data rather than trying to knock them offline. On November 3, Election Day, it recorded no significant distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack across any of those sites, while ordinary traffic to state and local election websites rose to about 602 million requests.
The post, written by Patrick Day, Jocelyn Woolbright and Carlos Azevedo, puts figures on the shift. Vulnerability exploit attempts climbed from 200,000 a day in January 2026 to 2 million a day in October, peaking in late October, several weeks before the vote. Over the 12 months before the election, Cloudflare says it mitigated 392 million such attacks on government election websites alone. And on October 22, one day after a critical PHP flaw became public, a state government election website in the Western United States set off Cloudflare's PHP defense more than 9,000 times.
In Short
Cloudflare says the US government election websites it protected in the 2026 midterms faced attempts to break into their systems 180 times more often than attempts to knock them offline, and that Election Day passed without a single significant DDoS attack. That matters to anyone who runs election, campaign or news websites, because the pressure built for months before the vote, reaching 2 million exploit attempts a day in October, while newsrooms and civil society groups still absorbed the bulk of the flood attacks. What changes is the picture of election-season risk: on the day itself, the biggest load came from voters, with requests to state and local election sites more than four times higher than two days earlier.
Voters, not attackers, filled the servers
"Ordinary user traffic to state and local government election websites was higher on Election Day (November 3) than any other day in 2026," according to Cloudflare. Requests to those sites reached approximately 602 million, up from 130 million on November 1, a Sunday. By PPC Land's calculation, that is 4.6 times the Sunday level, an increase of 363%, which matches the figure Cloudflare puts at the top of its post. Traffic across the rest of Cloudflare's global network rose by only 12% over the same period, according to the company.
There is a wrinkle in the wording. The post's opening sentence says "visitors" to government election websites "surged 363%," while the section that gives the underlying numbers counts requests. A request is not a person: a single visit to a polling-place lookup can generate many requests for pages, scripts and images, and the post gives no count of unique visitors.
Why these sites draw attention is set out plainly in the post. "State and local government election websites provide authoritative information to voters on how to register, where to vote, and who won," according to Cloudflare. "Because of their critical role, these platforms are often targeted by those seeking to suppress voter turnout, disrupt the reporting of results, or steal sensitive information."
Cloudflare's vantage point is large. The company's investor slides, covered when it reported third-quarter results on November 5, say 20% of the internet and 37% of the top 10,000 websites run on Cloudflare, and the company described its Radar data in October as drawn from roughly a fifth of all web traffic. Its 2025 Year in Review put global internet traffic growth at 19% for that year.
The figures in one place
From floods to break-ins
The central finding is a change in what attackers wanted. "Unlike what we saw in other recent elections, attackers focused on gaining access to internal systems and data, rather than knocking websites offline," according to Cloudflare. Government election websites faced vulnerability attempts at roughly ten times the rate of Cloudflare's broader customer base, the company says.
What does such an attack look like? The post gives two examples. "Website vulnerability attacks exploit outdated or vulnerable website code to gain access to internal systems and data," according to Cloudflare. "For example, cross-site scripting attacks inject malicious code into web pages in order to capture administrator credentials or deface site content. SQL injection manipulates database queries allowing attackers to potentially extract voter records or alter data."
The volume rose through the year. From 200,000 attempts a day in January, the count reached 2 million a day in October, a tenfold increase by PPC Land's calculation, and peaked in late October, several weeks before polling day. The 12-month total of 392 million works out to an average of about 1.07 million a day (PPC Land calculation), which sits between the two monthly rates the post gives.
The post uses several labels for this category: "vulnerability scans," "vulnerability attempts," "vulnerability exploit attempts" and "website vulnerability attacks." It does not separate automated scanning, which looks for weaknesses, from attempts to use a weakness once found. Nor does it say whether any attempt succeeded.
Automated probing of this kind is not confined to election offices. In August, GreyNoise tracked scanners on 824 IP addresses forging the names of AI crawlers while hunting for credential files, and on November 16 an FBI alert tied more than 1,000 infected WordPress sites to a malware operation linked to over $28 million in ransomware payments. The first shows how routine automated probing of websites has become; the second, what a compromised site can be turned into.
One flood attack worth counting
Election Day itself was quiet on the DDoS front. "On Election Day itself, Cloudflare did not record a single significant distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack across any of its 1,400 election-related websites," according to the post. The only significant DDoS attack Cloudflare reports came the day before, on November 2, against a candidate for the US House of Representatives in California, and totaled 520,000 requests. The post names neither the candidate nor the district, and it does not say whether the site went down.
Cloudflare sets that number beside Moldova. The Moldovan Election Commission experienced more than 898 million malicious requests in a single 12-hour period on its Election Day in 2025, with attacks peaking at 324,333 requests per second, according to the company. By PPC Land's calculation, the Moldovan 12-hour total was roughly 1,700 times the size of the California attack.
Earlier Cloudflare figures mark the upper end, measured in bandwidth rather than requests. In October 2024 the company disclosed a DDoS attack that peaked at 3.8 Tbps, then the largest publicly disclosed, and its March 2026 threat report described a 31.4 Tbps baseline for DDoS traffic, which it said was fueled partly by the Aisuru botnet, with hyper-volumetric incidents up 700% in 2025. The election post gives request counts only and does not define what makes an attack "significant," so the threshold behind the Election Day zero is not stated.
Phishing that passed the first checks
Campaigns protected through Cloudflare for Campaigns received malicious email at about the same rate as everyone else. In the 90 days before the election, 3.9% of emails sent to participating organizations were identified as malicious, against a 4.8% baseline across other Cloudflare customers, according to the post.
The difference lay in quality. "Nearly 42% of those malicious emails managed to bypass basic email authentication checks, such as verifying sender identity or preventing in-transit tampering," according to Cloudflare, which says its advanced phishing detection tools still identified them. Applied to the 3.9% rate, that means roughly 1.6% of all mail reaching participating campaigns was malicious and had passed those basic checks (PPC Land calculation).
The company's broader data offers a related figure. Cloudflare's 2026 threat report, published in March, found that nearly 46% of analyzed emails failed DMARC, one of the main email authentication standards. The two numbers measure different things: one is the share of all analyzed email that failed a check, the other the share of malicious email that passed basic checks.
Newsrooms absorbed the floods
The picture flips for the press and civil society. "Although website vulnerabilities were the most common attack overall, for journalism and election-related civil society organizations, DDoS was the most common," according to Cloudflare. Over the 12 months before the election, the company mitigated over 10.8 billion DDoS requests targeting these groups.
A single attack accounts for a large part of that. On September 10, 2026, a US-based news organization was hit with 3.04 billion requests, the largest single attack in the period, according to the post. If that attack is counted within the 10.8 billion, which the post implies but does not state, it represents about 28% of the year's total (PPC Land calculation). The news organization is not named.
Cloudflare ties the pattern to earlier research. A Project Galileo report released earlier this year found that among all Project Galileo entities in 120 countries, DDoS was by far the most common attack, accounting for 81% of all attacks, according to the post.
php2shell reaches a voting website
The one specific exploit named in the post is php2shell. "On October 21, security researchers disclosed serious vulnerabilities (known as php2shell) in PHP, an open source back-end programming language used by 70% of the world's websites," according to Cloudflare. "The flaws reportedly could allow attackers to execute malicious code on vulnerable servers, expose customer data, or disrupt services." The company says it deployed Web Application Firewall (WAF) protections for all customers before public disclosure.
The disclosure itself was covered on the day. Cloudflare's rules went live at 3:02 am UTC on October 21, about nine hours before public disclosure by Cloudflare's count, and targeted two flaws tracked in the CVE system as CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619. PHP released fixes at 13:30 UTC that day in versions 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35, with CERT-EU rating both flaws 9.2 on the CVSS scale. OpenAI said the same day that Calif had found one of the flaws using GPT-5.6 Sol, with Wiz finding both independently.
On October 22, Cloudflare's PHP defense was triggered more than 9,000 times on a state government election website in the Western US. "This attack was only detected on one election-related site and appears to have been an isolated incident rather than part of a broader trend," according to the company. The post does not name the state, say whether the site ran an affected PHP version, or say whether the 9,000 triggers came from one source.
How Cloudflare's election programs are organized
According to Cloudflare, the protection behind the data runs through three programs, each for a different type of organization, and each offered "at no cost":
- Athenian Project - "for state and local election websites."
- Cloudflare for Campaigns - "for political campaigns."
- Project Galileo - "for civil society, democracy, and journalism organizations."
Together, the post says, they covered more than 1,400 election-related websites during the 2026 midterms, "including those belonging to state and local governments, candidates, political parties, voting-rights advocates, journalists, and civil society groups."
