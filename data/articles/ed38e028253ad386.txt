TITLE: U.S. menopause hormone therapy fills more than doubled since 2023, led by estrogen patches and vaginal treatments
OUTLET: Medical Daily

Pharmacies are now filling about 4.9 million menopause hormone therapy prescriptions a month for U.S. women ages 40 to 74, up from about 2 million in 2023, according to a Johns Hopkins study published in JAMA as a research letter on Nov. 19. The rise began in 2024 and continued through 2025 and into this year.
Researchers estimated that the dispensing rate rose from 28 to 67 prescriptions per 1,000 women in that age group, Science News reported. Estrogen patches and vaginal estrogen drove the growth, along with progesterone products, while oral forms stayed stable.
The data measure how much hormone therapy women are picking up. They don't show whether more women are better off, and they don't measure benefits or harms.
Patches and Vaginal Estrogen Lead the Rise
The study, by Johns Hopkins researchers Dana Sarnak, Dr. Wen Shen and Dr. G. Caleb Alexander, used national pharmacy dispensing data to track fills by age group, formulation and route.
Transdermal estrogen goes through the skin as a patch, gel, or spray. Vaginal estrogen comes as a cream, tablet or ring, mostly for dryness, pain with sex, and urinary symptoms. Progesterone is prescribed with estrogen for women who still have a uterus, to protect its lining.
The shift away from pills is notable. The Women's Health Initiative, whose 2002 results led many women to stop hormone therapy, studied oral pills, and its participants were on average in their 60s. Many clinicians now favor skin-based estrogen for some women because it may carry a lower blood clot risk than pills.
Sarnak told Science News that greater attention to women's health, updated medical guidance supporting individualized treatment, and social media may all be contributing. She also said clinician colleagues report more patients seeking menopause care.
Policy Changes Before and During the Surge
The timing complicates any simple explanation. Fills began rising in 2024, more than a year before federal health officials moved on Nov. 10, 2025, to remove boxed warnings about heart disease, breast cancer and probable dementia from hormone therapy labels, according to the HHS announcement on hormone therapy labels.
FDA Commissioner Dr. Marty Makary said at the time that women and their physicians should make decisions based on data, not fear. The boxed warning about endometrial cancer for systemic estrogen-only products stayed.
A separate look at Johns Hopkins patients found the same pattern. Prescribing of vaginal estrogen, progestogen products and transdermal estrogen was rising before the label change, with the steepest increase among women ages 45 to 59, Sarnak and Shen told Contemporary OB/GYN.
The label changes have rolled out product by product. Six hormone products were relabeled in February, and three estradiol patches followed Oct. 30, bringing the total to nine, MedicalDaily previously reported. Because the JAMA data run into 2026, they capture part of that rollout, but not enough time to measure its full effect.
Demand has outrun supply in places. The FDA has attributed this year's shortage of estrogen patches to rising demand, and MedicalDaily previously reported that supply problems spread to estradiol injections and vaginal creams.
MedicalDaily Evidence Check: This was a cross-sectional analysis of national pharmacy dispensing data, published as a research letter in JAMA. It shows that fills rose sharply. It does not show who started or stopped therapy, whether prescriptions matched guidelines, or whether women's symptoms or health improved. It was not designed to explain why use rose.
Women Most Affected by the Shift
The trend matters most for women in their 40s and 50s with hot flashes, night sweats, sleep problems or vaginal symptoms. For women younger than 60 or within 10 years of menopause who have no contraindications, the benefits of hormone therapy generally outweigh the risks for treating bothersome hot flashes and preventing bone loss, according to the Menopause Society's hormone therapy position statement.
It is not right for everyone. Women with a history of breast cancer, blood clots, stroke, heart disease or liver disease need careful evaluation, and starting after 60 or more than 10 years past menopause carries more risk. Hormone therapy is approved to treat symptoms and to help prevent bone loss, not to prevent heart disease or dementia.
Rising demand has a household cost. Women who switch forms because of shortages may face a new copay, a prior authorization request, or a gap in treatment. Symptoms can return quickly after a missed refill, which is why planning refills matters more now than it did a few years ago.
Telehealth companies have also moved into menopause care. That widens access, but women should know which pharmacy fills their prescription and whether a product is FDA-approved or compounded.
Questions for a Clinician or Pharmacist
Women considering hormone therapy can ask which form fits their health history, how long treatment should last, and what follow-up is needed. Those with only vaginal symptoms can ask whether low-dose vaginal estrogen, which acts mainly on local tissue, is enough.
Women already on therapy should not stop suddenly without guidance. If a pharmacy cannot fill a patch, a pharmacist can check other strengths, brands or forms, and the prescriber can adjust the prescription.
Compounded "bioidentical" hormones are not FDA-approved and are not tested the same way as approved products. Generic estradiol pills and patches often cost less than brand-name versions.
Sudden chest pain, shortness of breath, leg swelling or pain, a severe headache, vision changes, or unusual vaginal bleeding during hormone therapy need prompt medical care. MedicalDaily will report on new research into who benefits most.
Key Questions Answered
What did the JAMA study find? Monthly hormone therapy fills for women 40 to 74 rose from about 2 million in 2023 to 4.9 million.
Which forms grew most? Skin-based and vaginal estrogen, along with progesterone. Oral forms stayed stable.
Did the FDA label change cause the rise? The study cannot say. Fills began rising in 2024, before the November 2025 label change.
Does the study show hormone therapy is safer now? No. It measured pharmacy fills, not benefits or harms.
Who is hormone therapy usually recommended for? Women with bothersome menopause symptoms who are under 60 or within 10 years of menopause and have no major risk factors.
What if my pharmacy is out of my patch? Ask the pharmacist about other strengths, brands or forms, and do not stop suddenly without your prescriber.
