Anthropic's Claude Code Builds Four Miami Driving Games on Budgets From $1 to $1,000
Anthropic's Claude Code can build a playable open-world driving game for less than a dollar, and can build a far more elaborate one for roughly $1,000. That is the central finding of a four-tier experiment in which the same one-page brief for a Miami-set game was handed to the coding agent with spending caps of $1, $10, $100 and $1,000.
The test varied more than the wallet. Each tier ran on a different Claude model, and the two most expensive builds also deployed teams of agents. All four games were browser-based 3D driving titles built from an identical specification calling for traffic, missions, beaches, a radio and a day-and-night cycle.
Claude Code operated in headless mode, building each game in the background without an open interface. Its loop opened the game in a hidden browser, checked for problems and passed feedback to a fresh session, with a hard cap stopping the build once the budget was exhausted.
Note: The $10 tier figure reflects a rerun after an initial attempt was discarded. The $100 build's completion time was not disclosed in the comparison.
At the bottom of the range, the cheapest model produced a game called Neon Tide Miami Drive for 98 cents across 29 rounds, at roughly three cents per round. Players get seven missions, including package runs and drift challenges. Traffic stops at red lights, pedestrians cross streets, and a bridge leads to an island with a lighthouse. The three radio stations generate music in code rather than streaming audio files. Buildings and the car are largely boxes with windows, but the game has functioning driving logic and physics.
The $10 tier, built on Claude Sonnet 5.5 and titled Palmetto Drift, expanded both activities and hazards. It offers 21 missions, police chases with a wanted level, rival racers and three cars. Rain makes roads slippery, and a pier adds a Ferris wheel to the city. Five radio stations play fictional news bulletins or advertisements between songs, typically after every couple of tracks.
That version required a rerun. In the first attempt, the model concluded the game was finished around round 30, then spent roughly 90 rounds opening and playing it without making changes. That consumed $4.72, about half the budget, before the run was discarded. For the second attempt, each round was required to focus on a single task and deliver a change a player could notice. The budget ran out during the final edit, just after the main game file had been cleared, but a checkpoint saved at each round allowed the game to be rolled back.
At the $100 tier, Claude Opus 5.5 working with three agents added menus, vehicle customization and more to discover, producing a game with more than 19,000 lines of code. At the top tier, Claude Fable 5.2 and about eight agents built a city roughly 6 kilometers wide containing more than 5,000 buildings, a run that took almost 12 hours.
The experiment lands as Anthropic pushes to make verification, rather than after-the-fact review, the center of AI-assisted software development. Claude Code's creator, Boris Cherny, has argued that giving Claude a way to check its own output is probably the single most important factor in getting good results, estimating that it improves outcomes by two to three times. That figure is his own estimate rather than a measured result across all software tasks.
The company's internal practice offers a sense of how far the model-driven workflow has already gone. About 90% of code in the Claude Code team is written and reviewed by Claude, according to Anthropic's engineering blog, and daily merged changes in its central monorepo rose from roughly 2,500 in May to more than 30,000 in September, a twelvefold increase in four months. Over June through September, merged pull requests in the Claude Code codebase more than doubled while the share of changes that had to be rolled back fell by more than 40%.
Anthropic attributes much of that to a layered review pipeline that runs from pre-commit self-verification through automated AI review, security checks, CI, risk-tiered human approval, a merge queue and rapid rollback. Changes are graded by risk rather than by the author's judgment, with authentication, billing, CI/CD workflow files and core installation scripts always requiring human sign-off regardless of how low a classifier scores them. Agent approvals never count toward the human review requirement, and whoever directs an agent to change code is treated as the author and first in line of responsibility.
The company has also had to engineer against a failure mode familiar to anyone who has watched two AI systems argue. In one case, a pull request went through more than 50 rounds of review and revision overnight before the author called a halt. Git blame showed that about 59% of the issues flagged in later rounds were pre-existing code that had been present in the first round. After capping review at three rounds, limiting later passes to newly submitted diffs and banning repeat filings of already-published comments, the share of pull requests passing after a single round rose from 65% to 78%, those needing five or more rounds fell from 8.9% to 1.5%, and the average number of rounds to approval dropped from 2.08 to 1.32.
For developers weighing how much to spend on an AI-built prototype, the budget experiment suggests a clear ladder. A sub-dollar run on the cheapest model is enough to prove a concept is viable, while each additional order of magnitude buys more content, more polish and, at the top end, teams of agents working in parallel on a single large world. The $10 run also carries a practical lesson: an agent left without a specific job each round can burn half its budget playing its own game. The remedy adopted in the successful rerun was to assign one task per round and require a visible change before moving on.
The caveats are worth noting. Anthropic has not published specific false-positive or false-negative rates for its reviewers, nor disclosed how much extra inference cost model-based verification adds. The published material reflects internal practice validated on the company's own codebase, which does not guarantee it transfers directly to others. The approach also assumes changes are small, reversible and detectable within minutes; for systems that cannot be rolled back after launch, such as payment settlement or hardware firmware, human review remains essential.
