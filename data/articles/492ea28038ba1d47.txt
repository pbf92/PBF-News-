TITLE: The two ways rings are carved around a newborn star
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Fukuoka, Japan—How are planets formed in a solar system? While the question is simple, the answer is not so straightforward. Astrophysicists have long peeked into the sky, observing early stars and planets to find the answer. A common pattern observed in early stars is sequential rings of bright and dark areas emanating from the center, like grooves on a vinyl record.
To understand how these rings form, a research team from Kyushu University studied archived data of V1094 Sco, a young star with these sequential patterns. They applied new imaging techniques to get a clearer look and discovered that the grooves form in two different ways depending on their proximity to the star. The inner rings are formed by a new planet orbiting the young star, while the outer ring is formed through dust clumping together under its own gravity, a mechanism known as “permanent gravitational instability.”
The study, published in The Astrophysical Journal, provides new insights into how solar systems like ours form, and demonstrates how much new science can be extracted from old data using new imaging techniques.
Planets are born inside disks of gas and dust surrounding newborn stars. Observing these stars can provide clues into how solar systems like ours form. One such newborn star is V1094 Sco. It is about 500 light-years away in the constellation Lupus and is only about two million years old. By comparison, our solar system is about 4.6 billion years old. Over the past decade, researchers have used the ALMA (Atacama Large Millimeter Array) radio telescope to observe young stars like V1094 Sco and have discovered interesting patterns in the disks surrounding new stars.
“These new stars have bright rings and dark gaps patterned in concentric circles resembling the grooves of a vinyl record,” explains Post-doctoral Fellow Masayuki Yamaguchi from Kyushu University’s Faculty of Science, who led the study. “These patterns aren’t new; what was a mystery was how these grooves were carved. The most popular explanation is that young planets make these gaps as they orbit the star. However, when a disk has many rings, it becomes harder to explain since it’s hard to imagine that multiple planets have already formed.”
Instead of requesting new telescope time, the team used public ALMA data of V1094 Sco and applied a super-resolution imaging technique developed in Japan called PRIISM. This technique reconstructed the image data, enabling the researchers to see the rings with approximately twice the sharpness.
“We observed that the disk extends approximately 380 astronomical units (AU)—about 13 times the distance between the Sun and Neptune—from the center. Within that, we found four narrow rings and gaps between 100–230 AU from the star’s center,” explains Yamaguchi. “When we compared the widths and depths of the gaps with the "one planet per gap" model, we could not explain the entire pattern. Instead, the origin appears to differ from place to place.
The innermost gap can be explained by a single planet about 55 times more massive than Earth. Farther out, the rings were more evenly spaced. Combined with the fact that the temperature and level of turbulence were lower in this region, the team concluded that these rings formed through a different mechanism: dust clumping together under its own gravity in a process called “permanent gravitational instability.”
“Because ALMA is a radio telescope, we have not yet directly detected the planet that generates the gap closest to the star. Using other telescopes such as the Subaru Telescope will help us get a clearer picture of this hidden planet,” concludes Yamaguchi. “As we analyze more disks, our understanding of the fundamental question of where and how planets form will deepen. Each new finding adds a thread to the tapestry of our knowledge of the universe.”

About Kyushu University 
Founded in 1911, Kyushu University is one of Japan's leading research-oriented institutions of higher education, consistently ranking as one of the top ten Japanese universities in the Times Higher Education World University Rankings and the QS World Rankings. Located in Fukuoka, on the island of Kyushu—the most southwestern of Japan’s four main islands—Kyushu U sits in a coastal metropolis frequently ranked among the world’s most livable cities and historically known as Japan’s gateway to Asia. Its multiple campuses are home to around 19,000 students and 8,000 faculty and staff. Through its VISION 2030, Kyushu U will “drive social change with integrative knowledge.” By fusing the spectrum of knowledge, from the humanities and arts to engineering and medical sciences, Kyushu U will strengthen its research in the key areas of decarbonization, medicine and health, and environment and food, to tackle society’s most pressing issues.
