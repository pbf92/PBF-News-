TITLE: Sarcopenia Linked to Worse Clinical Outcomes Across Colorectal Cancer Care
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Skeletal muscle loss and impaired muscular function represent a widespread clinical challenge across the continuum of colorectal cancer care, directly correlating with increased surgical complications, diminished chemotherapy tolerance, and reduced survival rates. In a narrative synthesis published in Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology, researchers led by Hanchi Dong, Hongliang Cao, Shaohua Wang, Jiayue Cui, and Fengchun Zhang evaluated evidence spanning observational clinical cohorts, preclinical mechanistic models, and supportive intervention trials. Drawing from an extensive literature search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library through September 2026, the authors examined how the deterioration of muscle quantity and physical performance shapes patient trajectories from primary resection through adjuvant therapy and metastatic disease. Sarcopenia is defined clinically as a skeletal muscle disorder marked by diminished muscle strength alongside reduced muscle mass or quality, with physical performance decrements indicating heightened severity. While the condition frequently coexists with malnutrition, cancer cachexia, and myosteatosis, the review highlights that these entities describe partially distinct biological and clinical features that require discrete diagnostic evaluation and targeted supportive strategies.
Clinical investigations documented across global cohorts reveal that low skeletal muscle mass is notably prevalent among patients diagnosed with colorectal malignancies, although reported frequencies fluctuate depending on diagnostic thresholds and imaging techniques. Across individual observational studies cited in the review, prevalence figures ranged from 15.0 percent to 38.9 percent. In an early cohort analyzed by Lieffers and colleagues involving 234 individuals with stage II to IV colorectal cancer, CT-defined low muscle mass was identified in 91 patients, representing 38.9 percent of the group, with higher rates observed among those presenting with more advanced stages. Conversely, an assessment by de Souza and colleagues reported a 15.0 percent prevalence in 197 patients, linking low muscle mass to lower body mass index, reduced serum albumin, and malnutrition. A meta-analysis of 44 observational studies encompassing 18,891 patients calculated a pooled prevalence of 37.1 percent, while a subsequent meta-analysis by Luo and colleagues focusing on 28 studies in neoadjuvant settings identified an even higher pooled prevalence of 50 percent.
For instance, diagnostic cutoffs for low skeletal muscle index in females range from below 29.9 square centimeters per square meter to below 41.0 square centimeters per square meter across published series. The authors stress that pooled meta-analytic figures represent mathematical averages across heterogeneous study criteria rather than uniform population benchmarks. Consequently, standardized reporting of imaging modalities, demographic stratification, and consensus diagnostic definitions will be essential to establish reliable clinical baselines across future oncology cohorts.
The clinical consequences of diminished muscular reserves manifest prominently in adverse surgical and survival outcomes. In a retrospective surgical cohort of 297 patients evaluated by Traeger and colleagues, postoperative ileus occurred in 41.8 percent of patients classified as sarcopenic compared with 26.5 percent among non-sarcopenic peers, establishing a statistically significant difference that underlines the perioperative risks associated with muscular depletion. Long-term oncologic trajectories demonstrate comparable vulnerabilities. In a cohort of 72 patients investigated by da Cunha and colleagues, individuals with low muscle mass experienced a median progression-free survival of only 7.2 months compared to 15.2 months in preserved cohorts, yielding a hazard ratio of 1.78 alongside poorer overall survival after controlling for metastatic extent and performance status. Similarly, Hu and colleagues linked low skeletal muscle index with inferior five-year disease-free and overall survival among 114 patients, showing that combining muscle measurements with interleukin-23 expression provided additive prognostic value. A meta-analysis of 20 studies by He and colleagues corroborated these associations, showing significantly decreased overall survival, disease-free survival, and cancer-specific survival among sarcopenic cohorts.
Muscle depletion also significantly impairs systemic therapy completion rates and exacerbates compounding systemic conditions. In an analysis of 196 patients receiving eight cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy for stage II and III disease conducted by Shan and colleagues, low psoas muscle index correlated with reduced treatment tolerance as well as shorter recurrence-free and overall survival. Among patients receiving targeted therapy with fruquintinib, Yao Wang and colleagues observed worse clinical trajectories in those with low baseline muscle index, though the modest sample of 60 patients requires cautious extrapolation. In the phase II NIPICOL trial investigating nivolumab and ipilimumab, baseline elevations of growth differentiation factor 15, a circulating biomarker associated with muscle wasting, correlated with poorer immune-related survival. Sarcopenia frequently overlaps with other high-risk phenotypes, including obesity, systemic inflammation, anemia, and malnutrition. Longitudinal postoperative muscle loss similarly portends unfavorable outcomes, highlighting the value of serial functional monitoring.
To explain this systemic physical deterioration, the review outlines multiple intersecting biological mechanisms, centering first on chronic systemic inflammation and dysregulated intracellular proteolysis. Tumor cells and reactive host immune cells secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-6, and interferon-gamma, which shift muscle protein balance toward net catabolism. Experimental models indicate that binding of tumor necrosis factor-alpha to its receptor activates nuclear factor-kappa B signaling, promoting the transcriptional upregulation of muscle RING finger 1 and muscle atrophy F-box, also known as atrogin-1. MuRF1 operates as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that tags structural myofibrillar proteins, such as myosin heavy chain, troponin I, and actin, for breakdown via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. In the ApcMin/+ mouse model of intestinal tumorigenesis, elevated circulating interleukin-6 closely tracks muscle and adipose wasting, whereas interleukin-6 genetic deletion attenuates muscle loss despite persistent intestinal polyposis. Downstream, interleukin-6 signaling activates the Janus kinase 2 and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 pathway. However, the authors caution that much of this signaling data derives from animal models, leaving longitudinal validation in human skeletal muscle relatively sparse.
Metabolic reprogramming, gastrointestinal disruption, and neuroendocrine stress further accelerate muscular decline. Preclinical studies suggest that tumor-driven nutrient competition reduces glutamine availability, suppressing anabolic mechanistic target of rapamycin signaling and mitochondrial respiratory capacity in cultured myotubes. In cancer cachexia models, accelerated adipose tissue lipolysis delivers excess fatty acids to skeletal muscle, provoking ectopic lipid accumulation, myosteatosis, and insulin resistance, although models like colon-26 adenocarcinoma present heterogeneous lipid phenotypes. Concurrently, the Cori cycle imposes substantial energetic demands as hepatic ATP is consumed to convert tumor-generated lactate back into glucose. Clinically, gastrointestinal obstruction, anorexia, pain, nausea, and surgical resection directly restrict caloric and amino acid intake. Experimental lines also implicate gut barrier breakdown, altered claudin expression, and lipopolysaccharide-mediated Toll-like receptor 4 activation in sustaining systemic catabolism, alongside hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activation that increases circulating cortisol. Furthermore, cytotoxic regimens like oxaliplatin and pelvic radiotherapy exert neurotoxic and local tissue stresses that impair satellite cell proliferation and muscle regeneration, compounding physical decline.
Addressing sarcopenia within routine oncology workflows requires structured screening and comprehensive supportive care rather than isolated pharmacological remedies. Current clinical guidelines, including those from the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism, emphasize screening for nutritional risk, involuntary weight loss, and reduced dietary intake starting from initial cancer diagnosis. Clinical evaluations should integrate functional metrics, utilizing screening tools like SARC-F or SARC-CalF alongside objective tests of muscle strength and mobility, including handgrip dynamometry, chair-stand tests, and gait speed. While abdominal computed tomography scans acquired during standard cancer staging offer opportunistic assessments of skeletal muscle cross-sectional area and radiodensity, imaging alone cannot replace functional testing to confirm consensus-defined sarcopenia. In addition to dietary counseling and oral nutritional supplements, managing intake-limiting symptoms such as nausea, mucositis, pain, and diarrhea remains critical to removing barriers to patient nutrition, though direct clinical trials demonstrating that symptom control alone reverses established sarcopenia remain unavailable.
Structured physical activity, particularly individualized resistance training, forms the primary non-pharmacological strategy for preserving muscular performance, though clinical evidence reveals mixed outcomes regarding treatment tolerance. Cancer survivorship guidelines recommend exercising major muscle groups at least twice weekly using two or more sets of 8 to 15 repetitions at approximately 60 percent or more of one-repetition maximum, adapted to patient recovery, peripheral neuropathy, and bone stability. However, dedicated oncology trials underscore the complexity of achieving systemic therapeutic benefits. The multicenter FORCE trial evaluated home-based resistance training among 181 patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy for stage II and III colon cancer. The trial found that home-based resistance exercise did not significantly improve chemotherapy relative dose intensity or reduce overall patient-reported symptoms compared with standard care, despite observed secondary reductions in fatigue and nausea. A separate randomized study by Brown and colleagues in 181 patients undergoing postoperative chemotherapy similarly revealed that home resistance exercise failed to prevent functional decline in a cohort with high baseline performance.
Targeted nutritional adjuncts and investigational therapies have yielded exploratory signals but currently lack definitive confirmation in large-scale colorectal cancer populations. In an exploratory randomized trial of 47 patients receiving chemotherapy conducted by Mazzuca and colleagues, supplementation with 13.5 grams daily of highly purified whey protein increased the proportion of lean body mass from 68.5 percent to 71.2 percent in reassessed participants, whereas control values declined, though functional gains were not demonstrated. Multi-ingredient prehabilitation combining blood-flow-restriction training, whey protein, creatine, and L-citrulline demonstrated modest lean mass gains in small abdominal surgery cohorts, but specific nutritional effects could not be isolated from exercise. Evidence for omega-3 fatty acids shows anti-inflammatory potential without consistent gains in muscle mass, while vitamin D supplementation remains limited to correcting verified biochemical deficiencies.
