TITLE: Psychosocial Interventions Reduce Stress and Burnout in Nurses, Review Finds
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Psychosocial interventions significantly improve nurses’ professional quality of life by lowering perceived stress, burnout, and compassion fatigue while boosting compassion satisfaction, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Continued occupational exposure to patient suffering and death leaves nursing personnel vulnerable to severe emotional exhaustion and secondary traumatic stress, workplace hazards that harm both personal wellness and clinical care quality.
To examine the inconclusive evidence surrounding these workplace interventions, researchers evaluated randomized controlled trials assessing programs such as mindfulness training, cognitive-behavioral therapy, structured self-care initiatives, and resilience training. The investigative team followed PRISMA reporting guidelines and prospectively registered the systematic review protocol in the PROSPERO database under identification number CRD42024553920.
The authors searched five major electronic databases—PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, CINAHL, and Web of Science—for relevant research published through October 2024. Across these databases, seven randomized controlled trials meeting the pre-established inclusion criteria were identified, encompassing a combined sample of 863 participating nurses. Data were systematically extracted regarding study designs, participant sample sizes, specific intervention categories, implementation frequencies, and total programmatic durations.
Outcomes across the included trials were evaluated through validated psychological assessment tools, specifically the Professional Quality of Life scale, the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale, and the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scales. Statistical syntheses were conducted using Review Manager version 5.4.1, applying random-effects models to address methodological heterogeneity across trials. Analyses across specific outcome categories demonstrated low statistical heterogeneity, with I-squared values ranging from 0 percent to 10 percent.
The pooled results indicated that psychosocial interventions achieved moderate reductions in stress when assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale, yielding a standardized mean difference of -0.47 with a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.73 to -0.22. Greater stress reductions were recorded on the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales, with a standardized mean difference of -0.75 and a confidence interval spanning -1.09 to -0.40. Burnout rates also declined across trials, demonstrating an overall standardized mean difference of -0.59 with a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.79 to -0.38, though burnout remained comparatively higher in cognitive-behavioral therapy and long-term resilience-based interventions.
The interventions produced a significant decrease in compassion fatigue, showing a standardized mean difference of -0.62 with a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.83 to -0.41, with the strongest relief observed following mindfulness-based practices and self-care programs. Concurrently, compassion satisfaction among nurses increased substantially, registering a standardized mean difference of 0.68 and a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.47 to 0.88. The authors noted that the diversity of intervention and outcome methodologies reflected the variety of tested interventions rather than statistical inconsistency.
Despite these measured psychological gains, the authors characterized the overall quality of evidence as low to moderate. This cautious assessment stems from the modest number of randomized trials available for synthesis, the methodological limitations of the primary investigations, and the relatively short follow-up periods examined. Furthermore, because only seven trials met the inclusion criteria, the investigators could not formally evaluate publication bias across the literature and instead reported detailed subgroup results.
In their concluding evaluation, the researchers highlighted that healthcare institutions should prioritize structured, nursing-specific psychological support programs grounded in empirical evidence. Providing targeted psychosocial interventions to alleviate workplace stress and secondary trauma represents an important institutional pathway for improving nursing workforce well-being, job satisfaction, and the broader quality of patient care.
