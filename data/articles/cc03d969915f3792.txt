TITLE: Meta-Analysis Finds Ten Percent Perioperative Mortality After Pericardiectomy in Africa
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating surgical pericardiectomy for constrictive pericarditis across African centres has established a pooled perioperative mortality rate of 10.0 percent. The quantitative investigation synthesised data from eleven observational cohorts encompassing 604 surgical patients and 59 documented early deaths, yielding a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 6.5 percent to 14.0 percent. Constrictive pericarditis represents a disabling condition characterized by a fibrotic, noncompliant pericardium that impedes ventricular diastolic filling, diminishes stroke volume, and induces progressive systemic venous congestion. While surgical excision of the diseased pericardium serves as the primary definitive intervention, outcome data across the African continent had previously remained fragmented across small, single-centre retrospective case series. By systematically aggregating these diverse cohorts, the investigators sought to establish the first continent-wide benchmark of surgical risk, evaluate the reported aetiological landscape, and identify consistent clinical correlates of early postoperative death in resource-limited surgical settings.
The methodological framework for the review complied strictly with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and maintained a prospectively registered protocol on PROSPERO under registration code CRD420261451874. The research team executed exhaustive searches across major bibliographic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, African Journals Online, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science Core Collection from their inception through May 2026, supplemented by citation tracking and Google Scholar indexing without linguistic restrictions. Across an initial yield of 2,148 identified bibliographic records, two reviewers independently managed deduplication and conducted title and abstract screening using Rayyan software, achieving substantial inter-rater agreement with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.795. Full-text appraisal of 21 potentially eligible studies demonstrated near-perfect inter-rater consensus at a kappa of 0.81, ultimately identifying 16 studies meeting formal inclusion criteria for qualitative synthesis. Retrospective cohort designs and case series enrolling ten or more patients of any age undergoing pericardiectomy for confirmed constriction were eligible, while studies focusing primarily on non-constrictive tamponade or drainage were formally excluded.
Quantitative data extraction captured extensive clinical, procedural, and institutional variables into standardised spreadsheets, including diagnostic modalities, surgical access routes, extent of resection, cardiopulmonary bypass employment, in-hospital or 30-day mortality events, and documented causes of death. Methodological quality across the twelve peer-reviewed primary studies was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Series, which categorized eight studies as exhibiting low risk of bias, two as moderate risk, and two as high risk. The high-risk designations reflected incomplete retrieval of identified patient records or reliance on secondary tabulated summaries rather than primary source records. An additional four grey literature studies comprising university repository theses from Morocco and Kenya were identified and reviewed narratively; however, they were deliberately excluded from quantitative pooling because their primary underlying datasets could not be independently verified.
To account for expected methodological and clinical diversity across surgical eras spanning from 1973 to 2024, the primary statistical synthesis applied a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model coupled with Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformations to stabilise variance across raw single-arm proportions. The resulting pooled perioperative mortality point estimate of 10.0 percent exhibited substantial statistical heterogeneity, evidenced by an I-squared value of 51.5 percent and a statistically significant Cochran’s Q statistic of 20.61 across ten degrees of freedom. Individual study mortality estimates varied widely across individual surgical series, ranging from zero percent in a contemporary Ghanaian series reported by Tettey and colleagues to 27.3 percent in an earlier Ethiopian cohort reported by Johnson. The overall crude pooled mortality across all eleven quantitative series stood closely aligned at 9.8 percent, representing 59 fatal events among 604 operated individuals.
Geographic subgroup analyses revealed noticeable numerical gradients across African regions, although overlapping confidence intervals and modest subgroup sizes precluded definitive statistical interaction testing. Among the three East African cohorts conducted in Ethiopia, pooled perioperative mortality was highest at 12.9 percent across 106 surgical patients, with twelve recorded deaths. In contrast, the six West African surgical cohorts spanning Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Senegal, and Nigeria demonstrated an intermediate pooled mortality of 9.8 percent across 268 patients and 26 deaths. The two Southern African surgical series from South Africa reported the lowest regional pooled mortality at 8.5 percent across 230 patients with 21 deaths. Funnel plot asymmetry was statistically evident via Egger’s linear regression test, which yielded a significant intercept p-value of 0.004, signalling the presence of small-study effects, publication discrepancies, or genuine clinical variation linked to cohort sample size.
Extensive sensitivity and model robustness analyses substantiated the core surgical mortality findings while clarifying primary drivers of between-study variability. Alternative random-effects estimators, including restricted maximum likelihood and the Paule-Mandel iterative method, generated near-identical pooled mortality estimates between 9.97 percent and 9.99 percent, while a binomial-normal generalised linear mixed model with a logit link fitted via Gauss-Hermite quadrature yielded 9.2 percent. Calculating a 95 percent prediction interval produced a wide expected distribution between 2.1 percent and 22.8 percent for future surgical cohorts. Crucially, temporal subgroup stratification based on recruitment era midpoint demonstrated that four cohorts recruiting prior to the year 2000 exhibited a pooled mortality of 14.1 percent with marked residual heterogeneity of 79.6 percent, whereas seven cohorts recruiting from 2000 onward displayed a lower pooled mortality of 9.4 percent with zero percent residual heterogeneity. Leave-one-out sensitivity testing confirmed that omitting individual series shifted the point estimate within a tight corridor between 9.05 percent and 11.15 percent without disproportionate distortion by any single institution.
Investigating the underlying aetiological spectrum of constrictive pericarditis highlighted dramatic divergence tied directly to institutional diagnostic practices. When synthesizing eleven studies reporting presumed or clinically diagnosed causes based on broad empirical criteria, the pooled proportion attributed to tuberculous or infectious origins was 72.5 percent, accompanied by extreme statistical heterogeneity with an I-squared of 96.4 percent and individual series estimates stretching from 27.3 percent to 99.2 percent. Conversely, when the meta-analysis restricted its scope to the nine cohorts reporting definitive, histologically or microbiologically confirmed tuberculosis, the pooled proportion fell to 32.5 percent while heterogeneity decreased substantially to 60.9 percent. This pronounced reduction demonstrated that diagnostic certainty represented a primary driver of reported aetiological variation across centres, confirming that approximately one in three operated African patients presented with microbiologically verified mycobacterial disease, while the remainder reflected clinically empirical treatments alongside non-tuberculous pathologies.
Evaluation of procedural approaches revealed wide institutional diversity across surgical eras, encompassing both median sternotomy and left anterolateral thoracotomy incisions, alongside varying degrees of pericardial resection categorized as total, subtotal, or partial decortication. Cardiopulmonary bypass utilization remained limited and non-uniform, ranging between zero percent and 16.5 percent among reporting institutions. However, inconsistent documentation and strong collinearity between surgical technique, recruitment era, and specific operative facilities prevented formal meta-regression.
Preoperative New York Heart Association functional class IV status was identified as the most consistent and prominent correlate of early perioperative death, achieving statistical significance across seven of the nine studies that formally evaluated risk predictors. Nevertheless, because primary surgical publications predominantly reported univariable significance tests without multivariable effect sizes, only a single included investigation by Mutyaba and colleagues provided an extractable multivariable risk estimate. In that South African cohort, preoperative NYHA class IV was independently associated with an early mortality hazard ratio of 3.42 relative to combined functional classes I through III, alongside preoperative serum sodium which demonstrated a protective hazard ratio of 0.88 per unit increase. Other variables noted as significant univariable predictors in isolated individual series included depressed cardiac index, coexisting mitral regurgitation, elevated baseline creatinine, persistent postoperative diastolic constriction, and bilateral pericardial involvement.
Employing the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation framework, the review authors formally designated the overall certainty of evidence for pooled perioperative mortality as very low. This rating reflected mandatory baseline downgrades inherent to observational case series, compounded by serious risks of retrospective selection bias and substantial inconsistency across historical eras and geographical settings. Because complementary testing via Peters’ regression for single-arm proportions yielded a non-significant p-value of 0.395, and contour-enhanced funnel plots localized smaller cohorts within statistical significance contours, publication bias was deemed suspected rather than conclusively established. The investigators emphasized that documented associations between advanced functional class and surgical mortality must serve as hypothesis-generating indicators rather than proven causal mechanisms, urging the implementation of prospectively maintained, standardized multicountry surgical registries across African cardiothoracic centres to establish whether earlier clinical detection and referral can successfully mitigate perioperative loss.
