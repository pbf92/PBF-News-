TITLE: AI agent makers that skip user controls face action, UK's ICO warns
OUTLET: PPC Land

The UK's Information Commissioner's Office on November 24 warned companies building AI agents for consumers that it will step in against products released without proper privacy safeguards. "Where we see organisations rush to market, without considering privacy risks and without building meaningful user controls, we will act," William Malcolm, the regulator's Executive Director for Regulatory Risk and Innovation, wrote in a blog post published that day.
The post does three things. It sets out what the ICO expects from organisations that build, deploy or plug in AI agents aimed at the public. It names the legal questions the regulator still considers open. And it ends with seven steps that the ICO and the Competition and Markets Authority recommend to anyone thinking of using an AI agent or chatbot. There is no new rule in it, and no fine. There is a timetable: formal guidance in spring 2027, shaped by a public call for evidence that closed on November 20.
In Short
The UK's data protection regulator said on November 24 it will take action against companies that release AI agents for consumers without thinking through privacy risks or giving people real control over what the software does. This matters to anyone building or connecting AI tools that act on someone's behalf, such as booking a restaurant or sending invitations from a phone's contact list, because those tools can reach personal information without a person approving each step. Formal guidance is due in spring 2027, and in the meantime the ICO and the UK competition regulator have published seven checks for people using agents, one of which asks whether their data can be used for advertising.
What makes an agent different
The ICO treats agentic AI - software that carries out tasks on its own rather than only answering questions - as a break with the chatbots most people already know. According to the blog, the arrival of these products for consumers "marks a significant change in how personal information is used by AI systems and services."
The difference is who acts. Many existing chatbots "primarily respond to user prompts," the post says. An agent goes further: it "can take autonomous actions on someone's behalf, which may include gaining access to their personal information or that of a third party."
The regulator's example is an everyday one: an agent "could seek to access someone's contacts to send invitations for an event," the blog reads. That single task touches the names, phone numbers and email addresses of people who never signed up to the service at all. They are the third parties in the ICO's sentence.
The ICO does not dismiss the technology. Agents "could bring real benefits to people's personal lives when they are automating low-risk tasks, such as making a reservation at a restaurant," according to the post. It attaches a condition, though: "those benefits will only be sustainable if people can trust how the technology uses their information."
Trust, in the regulator's framing, has two parts. People "need to understand how their information is protected and have meaningful control over how it is used." They also need to trust that the organisations designing and deploying the systems "are doing everything in their power to keep them and their personal information safe and secure."
Privacy built in from the start
The central expectation fits in one sentence. "We expect organisations developing, deploying or integrating consumer-facing agentic AI to embed a privacy by design and default approach into their products from the outset," the ICO writes.
In plain terms, privacy by design means building protections into a product while it is being made, not bolting them on after launch. Privacy by default means the most protective settings are the ones switched on when someone first uses the product. The sentence also covers three kinds of company, not one: those that build agents, those that offer them to customers, and those that wire an agent into an app or service they already run.
What does that mean in practice? According to the blog, organisations "must be able to demonstrate how they comply with UK data protection law on an ongoing basis, including by identifying and taking appropriate steps to mitigate risks of harm to users and the wider public." Two words do most of the work. "Demonstrate" puts the burden on the company to show its compliance, not merely claim it. "Ongoing" means a check at launch is not the end of the job.
The ICO ties this position to an international text. It says it "was pleased to co-sponsor the Global Privacy Assembly's resolution on Privacy-protective agentic AI, which was adopted this week." The Global Privacy Assembly is a yearly gathering of data protection and privacy authorities from around the world. The blog does not reproduce the resolution or say which other authorities backed it.
The questions the ICO says are still open
The regulator concedes that the law does not map neatly onto every part of how agents work. "We recognise that consumer-facing agentic AI raises some novel questions about how the law applies to some aspects of processing," the post says. It names two.
The first is security. "The dynamic nature of data access permissions may pose data security risks," according to the ICO. An agent's access can change from task to task: a calendar for one job, a payment method for another, a contact list for a third. Every new grant is another door that has to be locked again afterwards.
The second is transparency. "Meaningful transparency may be a challenge if people are not informed about agentic AI-specific risks around permissions or actions they authorise," the blog reads. A privacy notice written for a website may say little about what happens once software is allowed to act alone.
Researchers have raised the same problem. In October, 65 researchers, most of them affiliated with Google, argued that consent prompts and fixed permissions cannot keep up with AI agents, listing 51 open problems.
The ICO is working through these issues "through a call for evidence on agentic AI and upcoming guidance." A call for evidence is a public request for information from companies and experts before a regulator writes its rules. This one "closed last week," Malcolm writes, and "will inform guidance due to be published in spring 2027, giving organisations greater regulatory certainty." When the ICO opened the call on October 8, responses were due by the end of November 20. The call contained 30 separate requests for evidence across six themes, covering topics such as keeping one user's session apart from another's, logs that cannot be quietly altered, and how an agent identifies itself.
Four regulators, one message
The ICO is one of four members of the Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum, alongside the Competition and Markets Authority, the Financial Conduct Authority and Ofcom. "Alongside our partner regulators at the Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum (DRCF), we are taking action to understand and manage the risks of agentic AI," the blog says.
The forum's thinking is not new. Its March 31 foresight paper warned that agentic AI was already in use and needed watching, and noted that agents need broad access to data. That puts pressure on data minimisation, the principle that organisations should collect and use only the personal data a task requires.
According to the ICO, the joint work includes "engaging with firms launching these new technologies in the UK market and providing feedback where we see services not meeting our expectations - or the public's." The warning quoted at the top of this article follows directly.
The forum has also gone to government. According to the blog, "The DRCF has written to ministers urging organisations to engage early with the relevant regulators and demonstrate that they meet their legal obligations before products launch so that innovation and the protection of the public advance together." The letter is dated November 16, 2026, according to a summary on the Regulation Tomorrow blog.
The order matters. The regulators are asking to hear from companies before launch, not after the first complaint arrives.
Companion chatbots and personalisation
Agents are not the only target. The ICO says it is studying how consumer AI services tailor themselves to each person, across general-purpose assistants, role-play bots and companion chatbots, which are built to act as a friend or partner. In the blog's words, the regulator is "researching public views and engaging with technology firms on the broader personalisation of consumer-facing AI services."
That work, the ICO says, "will allow us to focus on securing improvements in data protection practices that have the greatest impact on the public's use of agentic AI." It adds that it "will also continue to monitor and assess agentic AI as consumer products come to the UK market, maintaining close supervision of these technologies and the organisations developing them as they evolve."
Seven checks from the ICO and the CMA
The last part of the post turns from companies to the public. "Data protection and consumer rights closely overlap where AI is concerned," the ICO writes, so it has "joined forces with the Competition and Markets Authority to recommend the following steps for anyone thinking about using an AI agent or chatbot."
There are seven, in two groups. Three deal with what an agent can do. Four sit under the heading "When it comes to your data". The table records the regulators' headings word for word and summarises the text beneath each.
Seen from the company side, the list doubles as a description of the features the regulators expect to find. Check two assumes an agent offers a limited mode and asks before acting. Check six assumes agent memory, the feature that lets an assistant keep details from earlier conversations, can be switched off, edited or wiped. A product without those controls would leave people with nothing to check.
Why this matters for marketers
Advertising is named outright. The fourth check tells people to find out whether their information can be used to "personalise content or advertising". That points straight at the business models behind many consumer AI products. When researchers audited 30 AI products in October, only 14 of the 29 where the question applied let users stop their data being shared for advertising.
Retailers are in scope too. The ICO's expectations cover organisations "integrating" agents, not only the companies that build them, and that includes stores opening their checkouts to shopping agents. Walmart, for example, said in October that its new Agent Profiles would make each purchase need approval by default, with an optional spending limit such as $50 or $100. That is close to the kind of control the ICO and CMA tell people to look for.
Then there is trust. The regulators' warning that "agents can make mistakes" lands on a public that is already wary. A YouGov poll for payments company ACI Worldwide found 60% of UK adults would drop an AI shopping agent after one mistake.
None of this means the ICO is against advertising as such. In May the regulator advised the government to relax consent rules for low-risk ads such as contextual targeting and aggregated measurement. Its stance on agents is a different matter: the questions are about software that acts, not software that only shows something. The ICO also published a report on AI in online advertising in mid-November, which, according to a LexisNexis summary, found that large language models and agentic AI do not create new legal requirements for the sector, though organisations may need to adapt how they comply.
The UK is not moving alone. In Brussels, the European Commission's Digital Fairness Act proposal would require AI shopping agents to disclose paid suggestions, with fines of up to 6% of worldwide turnover.
