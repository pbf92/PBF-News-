TITLE: Pixalate estimates fake TVs cost advertisers $135 million in six months
OUTLET: PPC Land

On November 23, 2026, ad fraud measurement company Pixalate published research on a streaming TV scheme that, by its estimate, cost advertisers about $135 million between May and October. In Pixalate's data, a typical home internet address caught up in the scheme reported about six new television sets a day. None of them came back the next morning. According to the Virginia-based company, those sets do not appear to exist at all. Pixalate calls the scheme Mayfly. Like the insect, whose adult life can last a single day, each fake TV lives for one day.
In Short
Pixalate says a fraud scheme it calls Mayfly sold streaming TV ad space from TVs that do not appear to exist, borrowing the names of 3,277 apps such as Hulu, SYFY and The Roku Channel. Advertisers buying streaming TV ads through automated auctions bore the cost, which Pixalate estimates at $135 million from May to October 2026, peaking at about $1.9 million a day in October. The fake TVs were invented fresh each day and thrown away at midnight UTC, and most of the traffic that carried seller information came through companies the apps had not listed as authorised to sell their ads.
What Pixalate says it found
The report, titled "'Mayfly' Uncovered: 'Burner TVs' Behind $135M Ad Fraud Scheme Faking Premium CTV Apps Including Hulu, SYFY, The Roku Channel & More", appeared on Pixalate's blog under its Investigations and Ad Spend sections. It is credited to Pixalate as author, with seven named contributors including chief technology officer Amin Bandeali and Tyler Loechner, vice president of platform growth.
Connected TV, the industry's name for streaming apps watched on smart TVs, streaming sticks and set-top boxes, is a market where ads are increasingly bought through automated auctions rather than negotiated by hand. Pixalate's opening sentence sets out the claim. According to the company, Mayfly is "a sophisticated Connected TV (CTV) ad fraud scheme that sells ad impressions from TVs that do not appear to exist."
The company describes the scheme as "predominantly a US-focused ad fraud scheme". Its headline figures, as of November 2026, are set out below.
An "impression" is one ad shown once. The money figure rests on a single assumed price: $25 for every thousand ads, a measure the industry calls CPM. The arithmetic holds. At that rate, 5.46 billion impressions come to about $136.5 million, which Pixalate rounds to "~$135 million", and 76 million impressions a day comes to $1.9 million a day.
A scheme that grew fourfold in five months
Pixalate's monthly chart shows how the scheme scaled. Average daily volume on the days it sampled sat between 12 million and 21 million impressions from May to August, rose to 33.7 million in September and jumped to 76.0 million in October.
Multiplying each monthly average by the days in the month reproduces Pixalate's 5.46 billion total almost exactly, which suggests that is how the six-month figure was built. On that basis, by PPC Land's calculation, October alone accounts for about 43% of the six-month volume, and so of the estimated cost. Daily volume in October was roughly 4.2 times May's level and 2.3 times September's. Pixalate describes the chart as a "retrospective analysis of sampled days on platforms Pixalate measures". It does not say how many days were sampled or what share of the streaming TV market its platforms represent.
How Mayfly works, according to Pixalate
Pixalate lays the scheme out in six steps. The company hedges almost every step with "appears", and the description below keeps that hedge.
- Fake the device. Every request for an ad has to say what kind of TV it comes from. "Mayfly appears to have no TV, so it picks one from an apparent fixed list," according to Pixalate. On Apple TV, the same 35 device types appeared in May and in November 2026, and none runs tvOS 18, the Apple TV software released in September 2024, or anything newer.
- Generate the device ID. "A real TV keeps the same device ID unless the user resets it from the device," the report states. Mayfly instead appears to calculate its "device ID" from three inputs: the internet (IP) address, the device's user-agent string (a short text label a device sends to describe itself) and the date. One ID observed on November 3, 2026 was faf3ee89-e328-5c1c-9887-2a2f6672ec0b. On November 7, 2026, the method changed across several seller accounts at once, and the app's identifier became a fourth input.
- Attach a real home IP address. Each request carries an address that appears to belong to a real household on an ordinary broadband provider. "To a buyer, it appears to come from a household rather than a data center," according to Pixalate.
- Claim a premium app. Each request names the app the ad will supposedly run in, drawn from a list that includes Hulu, ESPN, CNN, Peacock, Tubi and Pluto TV. Pixalate counted 3,277 app names in Mayfly traffic.
- Sell it through resellers. The request enters the market through seller accounts at smaller ad exchanges and is resold, "sometimes several times, until an advertiser buys it."
- Discard the "burner TV" and start over at midnight. Because the date is part of the ID, every ID expires at midnight UTC. The next day, according to Pixalate, most of the same home addresses (60% or more) are back, each carrying a fresh batch of new device IDs.
The ID format Pixalate names is a "UUID version 5". That is a published standard for building identifiers by running a set of inputs through a fixed scrambling formula: the same inputs always produce the same output. Feed in the same address, the same claimed TV and the same date, and the ID stays steady all day. Change the date, and a brand-new ID appears. Pixalate's illustration, which it labels as using illustrative values, shows a single household address claiming to be an AppleTV5,3 on tvOS 16.6, producing one ID on November 3 and a completely different one on November 4. "Same home IP, same claimed TV. Only the date changes, and so does the 'device ID,'" its caption reads.
One consequence follows from the design. Because no ID survives from one day to the next, every day of Mayfly traffic shows up in campaign reports as fresh, never-before-seen devices. Pixalate's later advice to examine campaigns with "perfect reach" rests on that point.
The apps whose names were borrowed
Pixalate lists the 20 apps most often named in Mayfly traffic and publishes shares for the top 10. Its chart carries an explicit caveat: "Apps are those named in ad requests; they are not implicated." On Pixalate's account, the apps' owners had their names borrowed; they are not accused of taking part.
Ranks 11 to 20, in order, without published shares: ESPN (Fire TV), discovery+ (Roku), Sling TV (Samsung TV), HGTV GO (Android TV/Fire TV), ViX (Samsung TV), Plex (Android TV/Fire TV), Xumo Play (Android TV/Fire TV), Philo (Android TV/Fire TV), Fubo (Android TV/Fire TV) and ESPN (Apple TV).
By PPC Land's calculation, the top 10 account for 32.8% of identified Mayfly impressions, so the remaining two-thirds were spread across more than 3,200 other app names. By claimed platform, Fire TV and Android TV together made up 35%, Roku 27%, Apple TV 26%, Samsung TV 12% and LG TV and others under 1%.
Why Pixalate says the TVs are fake
The report compares Mayfly traffic with what it calls genuine streaming TV traffic, defined as "traffic without Mayfly's signatures" and measured on sampled days from May to November 2026. Platforms vary by row.
The midnight test
The sharpest evidence comes from a single two-day window. On one platform, on September 26 and 27, 2026, Pixalate looked at households active in the 30 minutes before and after two moments: noon UTC and midnight UTC. Among Mayfly device IDs, 97.8% survived noon and 0.7% survived midnight. Among genuine Fire TV device IDs, 88.2% survived noon and 70.8% survived midnight.
The timing matters. "Midnight UTC was 8 p.m. Eastern on these dates, prime viewing time. Most real TVs kept their ID through it," Pixalate's chart states. A household watching television at 8 p.m. in New York would have no reason for its TV to change identity on the hour. Mayfly's IDs did, almost without exception.
Software that never updates
Real devices update. On November 3, 2026, 56% of genuine Apple TV traffic in Pixalate's measurement ran tvOS 18 or newer; none of Mayfly's did. On Roku, 95% of genuine traffic ran Roku OS 15 or newer, against 0.4% of Mayfly traffic. "Genuine TVs update their software. The versions Mayfly reports stay fixed," the company's chart reads.
Pixalate sets out how the pieces fit together. "Pixalate identifies Mayfly by home IP addresses whose device IDs turn over completely from one day to the next," the report states. The other signals, it argues, show the TVs are apparently fake. It also addresses an obvious objection, that the daily IDs might simply be session identifiers: "These also do not appear to be ordinary session IDs, which would not explain the outdated software, and the ID method changed on the same day across multiple seller accounts."
That last point is the closest the report comes to describing coordination. A formula that changes on the same date, November 7, across several seller accounts suggests the accounts draw on a common source, although Pixalate does not say so directly. Nor does it name those accounts or the exchanges that host them.
Where the traffic entered the market
The supply chain section is where the report speaks most directly to buyers. Two public files are meant to show who is allowed to sell an app's ads. In app-ads.txt, an app publisher lists the ad companies authorised to sell its ad space. In sellers.json, an ad exchange lists the accounts that sell through it and says whether each one is the publisher itself or a middleman.
According to Pixalate, among Mayfly impressions that carried supply chain information:
- 59% were first sold by an account registered as an intermediary, and only 9% by a publisher.
- Another 23% were first sold by accounts missing from their own exchange's sellers.json file.
- 77% were sold by a seller not listed in the app's app-ads.txt file.
The same app could look clean or dirty depending on the route its traffic took. For one large streaming app that Pixalate does not name, 0.03% of impressions sold through an exchange the app lists were Mayfly traffic. Among impressions that arrived from a reseller with no supply chain information, 79% were. "The same app can show very different rates depending on the path," the report notes.
The percentages above also leave a gap. Intermediary, missing and publisher accounts add up to 91%. Pixalate does not say what the remaining 9% were.
How the $135 million was estimated
The method has three layers, and each carries its own uncertainty. First, Pixalate measured sampled days, not every day. Second, it extrapolated those days to six months. Third, it priced every impression at $25 per thousand.
That price is not drawn from what buyers actually paid for Mayfly impressions. The report links it to a 2026 benchmark page on streaming TV ad costs published by North American Media Experts. That page puts connected TV prices between $15 and $85 per thousand ads depending on the type of inventory, gives a blended US average of about $26 citing eMarketer, and lists free ad-supported channels at $15 to $25. It gives no single $25 figure. Traffic resold several times through smaller exchanges may have cleared at very different prices. The real loss could be higher or lower; the report gives no range.
On validation, Pixalate says it tested the detection "through user acceptance testing and by comparing it against platforms with known-clean CTV traffic, where flag rates stayed at baseline and genuine device IDs persisted from day to day." It does not publish false positive rates.
