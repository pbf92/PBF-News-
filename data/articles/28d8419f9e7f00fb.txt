TITLE: Updated Review Finds Progestogens Provide Little to No Reduction in Miscarriages
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

An updated systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating randomized controlled trials found that progestogens provide little to no difference in the risk of miscarriage when directly compared with placebo in women at an increased risk of pregnancy loss. Drawing on high-certainty direct clinical evidence, the researchers established that progestogen administration corresponded to approximately ten fewer miscarriages per one thousand women, with an uncertainty interval spanning from thirty-two fewer to twelve more miscarriages. The pooled analysis demonstrated a relative risk of 0.96 for miscarriage, accompanied by a ninety-five percent confidence interval ranging from 0.87 to 1.05 and a low heterogeneity statistic of ten percent. Concurrently, the evidence regarding live births indicated at most a modest improvement, showing a relative risk of 1.03 with a ninety-five percent confidence interval between 1.00 and 1.06 and zero percent heterogeneity.
The newly published investigation was conducted to update a previously published systematic review that evaluated the capacity of progestogens to prevent miscarriage. Prior evidence syntheses had already provided relatively precise indications that any overall impact of progestogen therapy on live birth rates was likely to be small. Consequently, the research team sought to update the direct randomized evidence contrasting progestogens against placebo to establish clearer certainty across key obstetrical outcomes. In doing so, the investigators re-evaluated all seven clinical trials that had been included in the prior review against updated, rigorous eligibility criteria. The authors specifically focused on enforcing the strict requirement for a direct placebo comparator so that both previously established studies and newly identified clinical investigations were assessed under an identical population, intervention, comparator, and outcome framework.
The primary objectives of the systematic review were explicitly defined to evaluate the specific effects of progestogen treatment versus placebo on live birth, miscarriage, preterm birth, and ongoing pregnancy at twelve weeks of gestation among women facing an elevated risk of losing a pregnancy.
To gather the relevant literature, the research team searched major medical databases including PubMed, Cochrane, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov for prospective studies published from 2020 onward, executing the final electronic search on December 10, 2025. Across the entire search history, the included randomized controlled trials spanned publication dates from 1963 through 2024. Eligible trials were required to enroll women who were actively attempting to conceive or who were in early pregnancy up to fourteen weeks of gestation, presenting with either threatened miscarriage or a verified history of recurrent pregnancy loss. Furthermore, the protocol strictly mandated that progestogens had to be administered specifically for the prevention of miscarriage; all trials involving progestogens for assisted reproduction, general infertility management, or alternative medical indications were systematically excluded.
Ultimately, the systematic review included six randomized controlled trials, comprising four trials retained from the earlier systematic review and two newly identified trials published up to 2024. In absolute numbers, the observed live birth increase favoring progestogens represented approximately twenty-two additional live births per one thousand women, within a ninety-five percent confidence interval extending from zero fewer to forty-three more live births. Beyond live births and miscarriages, moderate-certainty evidence demonstrated that ongoing pregnancy at twelve weeks of gestation was slightly more frequent among patients allocated to progestogens, yielding a pooled relative risk of 1.04 with a ninety-five percent confidence interval of 1.01 to 1.07 and zero percent heterogeneity. In contrast, the evaluation of preterm birth generated a pooled relative risk of 1.09 with a ninety-five percent confidence interval ranging from 0.93 to 1.27, an estimate deemed compatible with little or no difference as well as a possible increase in preterm risk under moderate-certainty evidence.
Methodological quality across all included investigations was systematically evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. The reviewers examined five specific methodological domains for each trial: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement techniques, and the selection of reported results. Judgments were formulated at both the domain level and as overall risk assessments, with the resulting risk profiles visualized through summary and traffic-light plots generated using the robvis tool before being integrated into the clinical interpretation of the data. The researchers also carried out prespecified subgroup analyses to investigate whether therapeutic outcomes differed by route of administration or baseline miscarriage risk profile; however, these subgroup evaluations revealed no statistically detectable differences, though the authors emphasized that the analyses were inherently constrained by the small number of contributing trials.
In their concluding assessment, the review authors determined that progestogens compared with placebo result in, at most, small differences in pregnancy outcomes among women categorized at increased risk of miscarriage. The highest certainty evidence demonstrated little to no reduction in miscarriage frequency alongside at most a small potential rise in live birth occurrences. Furthermore, the synthesis found no clear evidence of any protective benefit against preterm birth, with point estimates even leaving open the potential for increased risk. Taking together the full body of randomized clinical evidence accumulated between 1963 and 2024, the investigators concluded that available data do not indicate a large clinical benefit of progestogen therapy for miscarriage prevention in this clinical population.
