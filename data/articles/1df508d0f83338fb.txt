TITLE: Catalan regulator folds AI privacy and rights checks into one 64-page form
OUTLET: PPC Land

Two separate risk reviews that European law can demand of the same artificial intelligence project now sit in one document in Catalonia. The Catalan Data Protection Authority (APDCAT) presented a combined model on October 29, 2026: a 10-page set of guidelines dated October 2026 and a 64-page template that takes an organisation from a first yes-or-no screening to final risk scores for privacy, data security and a list of other basic rights.
The two reviews have different legal roots and, until now, usually lived in different files. One is the data protection impact assessment, or DPIA, a written check of privacy risks that organisations must carry out before handling personal data in ways likely to cause high risk to people. The other is the fundamental rights impact assessment, or FRIA, a check of how an AI system could harm rights such as equal treatment, which certain users of high-risk AI must complete before putting the system to work.
In Short
Catalonia's data protection authority has published one template that combines the privacy check required by EU data law with the rights check required for some high-risk AI systems. It matters to public bodies, lenders, insurers and any organisation building or buying AI that uses personal data, because a single project can trigger both reviews. The template asks shared questions once and reuses the answers, although it is a non-binding model and some of its own scoring tables do not add up.
Two checks, one starting point
The privacy check comes from Article 35 of the General Data Protection Regulation, the EU's main privacy law, which in 2026 marked ten years since its adoption. The rights check comes from Article 27 of the AI Act, the EU's artificial intelligence law adopted in 2024. According to the guidelines, the two laws are "complementary," and the reason is a sentence in the AI Act itself.
Article 27(4), as quoted in the guidelines in its 2026 form, reads: "If any of the obligations laid down in this Article are already met through the data protection impact assessment conducted pursuant to Article 35 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 or Article 27 of Directive (EU) 2016/680, the deployer may, when conducting the fundamental rights impact assessment referred to in paragraph 1 of this Article, include cross-references to the relevant sections of that data protection impact assessment or include the relevant parts thereof in the fundamental rights impact assessment."
That wording is new. The 2024 text said the rights assessment would complement the privacy assessment, without spelling out how. The change arrived with the Digital Omnibus package of EU simplification laws, and the guidelines say so directly: "in the 2026 version, following the omnibus reform approved by the European Union, it provides that cross-references or relevant parts of the DPIA may be included in the FRIA."
The authority's answer is to treat the opening parts of both reviews as one job. "The methodology is structured around two parts that are common to both assessments," the guidelines state. "In an integrated model, these need only be examined once, with the results then being used for both the DPIA and the FRIA." The stated aim is "to avoid duplicating the assessment of the same elements twice (for example, with regard to the analysis of the context of use) and to ensure a coherent flow of information between the two assessments."
The two shared parts are a description of the project and a test of whether either review is legally required. After that, the paths split: a privacy track with its own scoring, and a rights track with another.

Who wrote it, and what weight it carries
The guidelines come from a working group inside DPD en Xarxa, a learning community for data protection officers that the authority runs. Alessandro Mantelero of the Polytechnic University of Turin and Joana Marí of the authority coordinated it. The credits list 22 participants: five from the authority, plus people from three Catalan government departments, the Barcelona and Sant Feliu de Llobregat city councils, two public-sector consortia, three Catalan universities, a health and social care technology foundation, a telecommunications research centre and CaixaBank, the only private company named.
The document is not a binding ruling. Its legal notice is plain: "The opinions expressed in this document are the responsibility of their authors and do not necessarily reflect the official opinion of the APDCAT." It also does not replace the authority's existing privacy template. "The resulting model does not intend to replace the APDCAT DPIA template or existing templates developed by various institutions, but rather to present an agile model that can be easily integrated with the FRIA template adopted by the APDCAT," according to the guidelines. Both documents are licensed under Creative Commons BY-NC-ND 4.0, which allows sharing with credit but not commercial use or modified versions.
In an introduction, director Meritxell Borràs i Solé describes two gaps the authority set out to fill: how to carry out a rights assessment at all, and how to fit it together with a privacy assessment. The first was addressed by the Catalan FRIA model, which the authority presented to the Parliament of Catalonia on January 28, 2025, and by a free application built on it, presented on October 7, 2026. The introduction states that the model "was one of the first to be adopted by the European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights." The guidelines cite no document for that claim. The authority's October 29 announcement names Brazil, the Basque Country, Croatia and Malta as places that have recommended or adopted the earlier model.
According to APDCAT's announcement, more than 100 data protection officers attended the third meeting of the community, where Joana Marí Cardona, the authority's data protection officer and head of strategic projects, explained the new model and described it as a tool that will need to be adapted and expanded. The community has nearly 400 members and was founded in July 2023, according to the authority. English editions of the guidelines and the template are listed on the authority's templates page and on the DPD en Xarxa site alongside Catalan and Spanish versions. Neither page dates the English files.
How the template works, step by step
According to the template, an assessment runs through six stages. The template describes itself as "a model that seeks to identify and control the risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals, arising from the processing of personal data and the use of AI."
- Screening. Six yes-or-no questions open the form: whether personal data is used, whether data is reused for another purpose, whether AI is involved, whether the project uses practices banned under Article 5 of the AI Act, whether AI use could affect fundamental rights, and whether "less intrusive methods (e.g. synthetic data)" could reach the same result. The organisation also marks the AI system's phase from seven options, running from research and proof of concept to training, validation and deployment.
- Project description (section 1). This shared section records the data processed, its source and how long it is kept, then each processing step with its data, output, recipient and location. A separate part describes the AI system with checkboxes: six system types, from general-purpose models to agentic systems; ten functional categories; five degrees of autonomy, from human-in-the-loop assistance to "Autonomous execution without human intervention"; six architectures, including retrieval-augmented generation; eleven privacy-enhancing techniques, among them pseudonymisation, differential privacy, synthetic data and machine unlearning; eight data types; seven data origins, including web scraping and data bought from third parties; and five purposes. Each organisation involved is then listed with its role under both laws: provider or deployer under the AI Act, controller or processor under the GDPR.
- Need test (section 2). The template first lists two situations in which a privacy assessment is not required. A "technical sheet" follows, based, according to a footnote, on "Version 1.0 of the EDPB's Template for Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), adopted on 10 March 2026." It records the reasons for the assessment against the three cases in GDPR Article 35(3) and nine further high-risk criteria, and asks for the data protection officer's opinion. For the rights check, the template asks three questions on the type of system and three on the type of organisation. "If there are positive answers (yes) to the questions relating to both the objective and subjective scope, a FRIA will need to be carried out." Seven change triggers, including purpose, context of use, frequency or intensity of use and human supervision, decide whether an earlier rights assessment must be redone.
- Privacy assessment (section 3). This is the longest part. It tests necessity and proportionality, with AI-specific prompts such as a request to "Explain why 'doing nothing' (or maintaining the current situation) is not an option." It then checks the purpose, the six legal bases for processing, children's data, sensitive categories of data, criminal records data, consent, transfers outside the EU and the lawfulness of training data. Nine further subsections test how the organisation handles people's rights, from information and access to erasure, objection and the right not to be subject to automated decision-making.
- Data security scoring (section 3.3). The template scores the impact of a loss of confidentiality, integrity and availability of data, counts yes answers to a list of risk questions to estimate probability, applies security controls, and then repeats the exercise to reach a residual risk.
- Rights assessment (section 4). "This section examines the impacts on fundamental rights and freedoms other than the right to data protection, which have already been the subject of the DPIA." A planning questionnaire in four parts (A to D) asks about the system, the legal context, existing controls and the involvement of affected groups, with eight questions in part D alone on stakeholders, supply chains, public communication and staff training. Each affected right is then scored through a chain of tables, and a footnote to the final table states that "a reference must be included to the final result of the DPIA in terms of residual probability and severity."
Two ways of measuring risk
The guidelines argue that both reviews measure risk the same way at heart, by weighing how likely harm is and how serious it would be. They reproduce tables from the French regulator CNIL's privacy assessment template, February 2018 edition, and from the authority's own rights model to make the point. The difference lies in what gets scored.
"While the DPIA analyses the various risks to a single right, reflecting the procedural nature of data protection in terms of potential breaches (for example, unauthorised access to data or unwanted changes), the FRIA considers each affected right in its entirety, without identifying subsets of risks for each type of potential harm," the guidelines state. For that reason, according to the document, "to align the DPIA and the FRIA, it is not necessary to revise the matrices used in the DPIA to assess the level of risk to data protection."
