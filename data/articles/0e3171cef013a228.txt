TITLE: Spain fines Axesor €160,000 for reselling self-employed workers' data
OUTLET: PPC Land

Spain's data protection authority has fined Axesor Conocer para Decidir S.A.U., a business information company, €160,000 for buying and reselling data about self-employed people that started life in the country's tax records. The Agencia Española de Protección de Datos (AEPD) found that Axesor had no legal ground to use the data for marketing and had never told the people concerned that it held their details. It also ordered the company to stop using the data and delete it.
The 44-page resolution, in sanctioning procedure PS/00464/2025 (file EXP202511665), is signed by AEPD president Lorenzo Cotino Hueso and carries no date. The last event it records is October 9, 2025, when Axesor paid a reduced €128,000 to close the case. The legal news service MLex reported that payment on September 21, 2026. Axesor told the regulator it did not accept responsibility and paid only "for practical reasons, given the very high probability of being fined". Quotations from the resolution below are PPC Land translations from the Spanish.
In Short
Spain's privacy regulator fined a company called Axesor for selling lists of self-employed people built from data that the tax office passes to the chambers of commerce, which the law reserves for public uses such as a business register. If you buy or build business contact lists in Spain, the decision says a register being public does not make its contents free to sell, and sole traders' home addresses often sit in those lists. Axesor paid €128,000 and has three months, once the decision is final, to stop using the data and delete it.
How a tax record became a sales list
The case concerns Spain's "autónomos", the self-employed workers and sole traders who register their business with the tax agency, the Agencia Estatal de Administración Tributaria (AEAT), using forms 036 and 037. Those forms ask for a name, tax identification number, phone, email and address. For many sole traders, the business address is their home.
According to the resolution, that information then moved through four hands before it reached Axesor's customers.
Camerdata was set up in 1985 by the chambers of commerce. It told the AEPD on November 13, 2023 that "the total number of self-employed people currently in the database is 1,665,049". That figure describes Camerdata's file, not Axesor's. The resolution does not say how many of those records Axesor held.
The 2009 contract spells out why Infotel wanted the list. According to the text quoted in the resolution, the company was interested in "obtaining a list of the business activities of self-employed entrepreneurs in order to enrich part of its file", which formed part of a data warehouse that the company "or its clients use in their commercial prospecting activities to carry out promotional and advertising marketing actions". That is data enrichment in plain terms: topping up an existing database with fields bought from someone else. Camerdata committed to send an updated file at least once a year, with each new version replacing the last. Where the phone field was empty at source, it would be filled from telephone directories.
Axesor confirmed the sales to the regulator in writing. "We confirm that there has been commercialisation of data of sole traders provided by CAMERDATA to third-party companies and entities. Those entities belong to both the public and private sector," it stated. It said it kept five years of records under its retention policy and gave these contract counts:
Axesor's standard instructions to clients, also quoted in the resolution, required that any campaign using the data be "Business to Business", meaning it could only pitch products or services aimed at the person's business, not their private or family life.
What the lists contained, and where it came from
A key part of the AEPD's reasoning rests on the difference between three versions of the same data. The chamber publishes a free public register on its website, which it calls the "Censo Nacional de Empresas". It also holds a fuller internal census built from tax agency data, a copy of which it sold to Camerdata. Camerdata then added fields from other suppliers.
The tax identification number is the clearest gap. The AEPD checked the chamber's public register in August 2025 and found that it does not show the number. Camerdata nonetheless listed the public register as the source of that field. According to the resolution, the internal census that the chamber sold to Camerdata "contains more personal data than those contained in the public business census, because it contains the tax identification number of the self-employed". Camerdata's file in turn holds "additional information" beyond that census. "Therefore, the processing of data carried out by AXESOR is not limited to data from the public business census," the AEPD concluded.
The Axesor contract annexes list name, trading name, full address down to street number and district, the business activity tax code, and the phone for the business address. The 2012 addendum widened the supply to include the tax number (CIF), the company name and the street address down to staircase, floor and door, for both companies and sole traders.
What was on Axesor's website
The complaint that started the case was filed on December 27, 2022 by an organisation whose name is redacted in the resolution. The digital rights group Xnet has said it filed a complaint with the AEPD in December 2022 over the use of self-employed workers' data. The complaint included screenshots of Google results for a self-employed person's name, which surfaced pages on expansion.com and autonomos.axesor.es. The AEPD recorded what the Axesor site showed at three points.
Why the AEPD found no legal basis
Under the GDPR, any use of personal data needs one of six legal grounds, listed in Article 6(1). Axesor argued for the sixth, legitimate interest, and for a Spanish rule that presumes it in some cases. The AEPD rejected both.
Sole traders are covered by the GDPR
Axesor's first argument looked back to the old Spanish rules. A 2007 regulation stated that data about sole traders, "when they refer to them in their capacity as traders, industrialists or shipowners", fell outside data protection law. The AEPD said that exclusion did not survive the GDPR, which has applied in Spain since 2018 and lists no such exception, and that Spain's 2018 data protection law repealed incompatible older rules. "The fact that the AEPD has not questioned this processing in the past does not imply that it has confirmed its validity," the resolution states.
It cited the Court of Justice of the European Union's ruling of April 3, 2025 in case C-710/23, which held that the name, signature and contact details of a person representing a company are personal data, and that "the fact that this information is part of the context of a professional activity cannot deprive it of its classification as personal data".
The tax law closes the door
The core of the decision is Article 8 of Law 4/2014, which tells the tax authorities to give the chambers of commerce the data they need for a public business census. The law, as quoted in the resolution, says "this information will be used for the preparation of the public business census, for the fulfilment of the public-administrative functions that this Law attributes to the Chambers, as well as for the preparation of the electoral census". Only chamber staff designated by the chamber's governing body may see the tax data, and they carry the same duty of secrecy as tax officials.
The AEPD read that as a hard limit on purpose limitation grounds. "This legal limit prevents further use of the data by third parties based on legitimate interest for purposes other than those expressly provided for in the law, such as its commercialisation through business databases," the resolution states. It added that the confidentiality duty "does not disappear simply because some data form part of the public census".
The presumption only covers contact details
Article 19.2 of Spain's data protection law, the LOPDGDD, presumes a legitimate interest when a company processes a sole trader's contact details. Axesor relied on it. According to the AEPD, the presumption only covers contact data, only when it refers to the person "solely in that capacity" as a business owner, and only when the aim is not to deal with them as a private individual. It can also be rebutted. "Axesor has processed data that go beyond contact data," the authority found, and a specific law limits the use of that data.
Reading the rule as broadly as Axesor did would, according to the AEPD, cancel the accountability principle for an entire group of people. "The rules make clear that legitimate interest is not a blank cheque for any type of processing of personal data," the resolution states. It also cited the European Data Protection Board's Guidelines 1/2024 on legitimate interest for the point that a specific legal restriction on certain data must prevail over any presumed legitimate interest.
Home addresses are not just business data
Axesor argued that the addresses it processed were the places where sole traders run their businesses. The AEPD answered that the tax forms allow a self-employed person to register a home as the business address, and that there is "a high probability" that the two coincide in many cases. "The fact that an individual entrepreneur carries out their activity from their personal home does not turn that home into exclusively professional data," the resolution states. Axesor had no measure to tell the two apart, which the AEPD described as a lack of diligence.
No balancing test on file
Even without the tax law, legitimate interest would have required a documented, strict balancing exercise with safeguards: real transparency, an easy way to object, a data protection impact assessment where needed, minimisation and periodic review. "There is no record in this case that AXESOR carried out the necessary weighing," the resolution states. Because the people concerned were never told, they could not use their right to object under Article 21 of the GDPR, which in the AEPD's view "would invalidate" any balancing Axesor might have done.
The duty to tell people
The second fine concerns Article 14 of the GDPR. When a company gets personal data from someone other than the person concerned, it must tell that person who it is, why it holds the data, on what legal ground, where the data came from, who receives it and how to object. The deadline is a reasonable period of no more than a month, or earlier if the data is passed on to someone else.
Axesor never did. It argued that writing to every self-employed person would be impossible or would take "disproportionate effort", the exemption in Article 14(5)(b). The AEPD said the exemption is not automatic for large datasets and requires a concrete assessment of why notice is impossible and what other measures could be taken. "The GDPR does not contemplate economic viability as a decisive criterion to exempt from the duty to inform," the resolution states. "Transparency is not satisfied simply by the theoretical accessibility of the information."
Contract clauses that pass the job to clients did not help either. Camerdata told the AEPD it signs contracts with between 500 and 900 clients a year, and its standard terms say any client that stores the data becomes its controller and must send individual notices, with a template sentence naming Camerdata as the source. In Axesor's case, the AEPD said "the mere inclusion of clauses in contracts with its clients does not guarantee that the information is effectively transmitted", and does not replace Axesor's own duty.
