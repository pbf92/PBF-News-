TITLE: Scoping Review Links Vehicle Entrapment and Prolonged Extrication to Collision Mortality
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Rescue and extrication characteristics following road traffic collisions are significantly associated with patient morbidity and mortality, according to a scoping review published in the Scandinavian Journal of Trauma, Resuscitation and Emergency Medicine. Led by researchers Rob Fenwick, Willem Stassen, Lauren Rodgers, and Tim Nutbeam, the synthesis evaluated evidence surrounding the Early Rescue phase of the Road Injury Chain of Survival. Road traffic collisions claim approximately 1.3 million lives worldwide every year, and sub-optimal post-collision care contributes to preventable mortality across trauma systems. Although improving post-collision outcomes requires structured intervention frameworks, the procedures carried out during early rescue and patient extrication have historically remained poorly standardised. To address this gap and support foundational work for developing standardised Utstein-style data elements for road injury, the investigative team mapped available literature to understand how rescue and extrication variables correlate with clinical outcomes.
The authors conducted their scoping review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines, known as PRISMA-ScR. The research protocol was formally registered on the Open Science Framework. To capture relevant investigations across the medical and technical literature, the researchers conducted systematic literature searches on June 2, 2025, without applying any date restrictions or language limits. The team queried six major academic electronic databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycInfo, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, and IEEE Xplore. The search strategy specifically sought studies that evaluated rescue or extrication characteristics after motor vehicle collisions and reported measurable outcomes relating to morbidity, mortality, physiological deterioration, entrapment duration, or the delivery of time-critical prehospital interventions.
Screening and data extraction procedures followed structured protocols designed to ensure rigorous evaluation of the literature. Two review authors worked independently to screen the identified titles, abstracts, and full-text manuscripts against predefined inclusion criteria. Across the six queried databases, the initial search queries retrieved a total of 1,836 records. After the removal of duplicate citations, 1,117 distinct records underwent screening. Following independent dual-reviewer assessment, the team selected 34 published studies that met all inclusion criteria for qualitative evidence synthesis. Data from these 34 publications were extracted and charted using a standardised form, and the investigators organised the resulting evidence descriptively by classifying each study into one of seven core categories based on its primary focus.
The 34 included studies spanned multiple facets of collision scene management and emergency response. Field triage criteria formed the largest single category, comprising nine studies that evaluated on-scene decision tools and assessment metrics. Seven investigations focused on the epidemiology of entrapment, detailing patient demographics, entrapment prevalence, and general collision characteristics. Six studies examined specific extrication techniques used to release trapped occupants from damaged vehicles. Prehospital time factors were the primary focus of five studies, assessing how various time intervals influence clinical courses. The remaining literature comprised three evidence synthesis and clinical practice guideline documents, two studies evaluating direct patient experience during extrication events, and two studies categorised as addressing other miscellaneous operational factors.
Within the epidemiological literature, the review documented that physical entrapment occurs in approximately 9% to 16% of all road traffic collision patients evaluated in the analysed cohorts. Across these studies, the presence of entrapment consistently demonstrated an association with increased injury severity scores and elevated patient mortality compared to non-trapped individuals. In the evaluation of field triage criteria, prolonged extrication, typically defined as an extrication duration exceeding 20 minutes, served as a reliable predictor of severe anatomical injury in most identified triage studies. However, the evidence regarding time was not entirely uniform across all study designs; the authors noted that one included prospective study identified no statistically significant association between total extrication time and patient mortality.
Analysis of extrication techniques revealed critical distinctions between movement biomechanics and clinical patient outcomes. Biomechanical data gathered from healthy volunteer participants suggested that self-extrication generates less spinal movement than traditional assisted extrication techniques. Despite these volunteer biomechanical findings, the scoping review highlighted that direct comparisons of clinical patient outcomes between different extrication techniques remain extremely limited in the published literature. Regarding broader prehospital timelines, the synthesised evidence showed that longer scene times are generally associated with increased patient mortality, with on-scene extrication activities contributing substantially to total scene duration. In response to these competing priorities, existing consensus guidance emphasises prioritising time-sensitive emergency care over absolute, rigid spinal motion restriction.
In their conclusions, the review authors emphasized that while rescue and extrication characteristics are clearly linked to post-collision outcomes, the underlying evidence base exhibits substantial heterogeneity. Included publications demonstrated marked variations in their working definitions of entrapment, methods of measuring time intervals, and reporting of clinical outcomes. By identifying key variables and outcomes relevant to the Early Rescue phase of the Road Injury Chain of Survival, this scoping review establishes an evidence foundation for establishing consensus-based, Utstein-style data elements. The authors concluded that creating these standardised data reporting elements will be essential for facilitating future international comparisons, improving data quality, and driving system-level enhancements in post-collision trauma care.
