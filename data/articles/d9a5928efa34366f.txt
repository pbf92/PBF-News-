TITLE: Evidence on Glucose-Based ICU Fluids and Blood Sugar Control Remains Inconclusive
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A scoping review evaluating glucose-based maintenance fluids and fluid creep in critically ill adult patients found that the current evidence regarding their impact on blood glucose control is limited, observational, and inconclusive. Intensive care unit clinicians routinely administer intravenous fluids for resuscitation, nutrition, maintenance, and fluid creep, which refers to the hidden volume used to keep intravenous lines open or dilute administered medications. While normal saline has traditionally served as fluid creep, rising concerns about electrolyte imbalances have driven clinical interest in alternatives such as five percent dextrose in water.
To evaluate how glucose-containing infusions influence glycemic stability, researchers conducted a systematic scoping review focused specifically on adult patients treated in intensive care units. The investigators systematically searched major medical literature databases, including PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library, under a protocol registered with PROSPERO under registration code CRD42024542262. The team sought to assess comparative outcomes between glucose-based and non-glucose-based maintenance fluids or fluid creep, tracking blood glucose levels, insulin requirements, urinary output, and overall length of stay in the intensive care unit.
The review protocol specified strict eligibility criteria to isolate relevant clinical cohorts and trials. The authors aimed to include prospective and retrospective clinical cohort studies as well as controlled trials in adult intensive care unit populations that explicitly reported blood sugar levels or insulin administration. Studies focusing primarily on resuscitation fluids were excluded from analysis. Furthermore, the review systematically excluded patient populations possessing highly specific fluid management requirements, such as pediatric patients and individuals suffering from severe burns.
Across the identified literature, six observational, single-center cohort studies encompassing a total of 6,343 adult patients met all inclusion criteria. The review team identified no non-randomized controlled trials that fulfilled the predefined requirements. The authors assessed the methodological quality and susceptibility to bias within the included observational research using the standardized Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions assessment tool.
The synthesized findings across the six included studies revealed significant heterogeneity in glycemic outcomes. Reports measuring blood glucose concentrations demonstrated inconsistent and inconclusive patterns when comparing glucose-containing regimens to non-glucose alternatives. Similarly, documented outcomes for urinary output and intensive care unit length of stay varied widely across cohorts, with the authors noting that the underlying trials lacked adequate statistical power to report these secondary endpoints definitively.
The authors identified substantial methodological limitations across the available body of literature. The analyzed studies were notably subject to confounding variables, incomplete outcome reporting, and substantial variability in the fluid regimens administered to patients.
The researchers emphasized that maintenance fluids, drug diluents representing fluid creep, and combined infusion strategies represent distinct clinical exposures carrying substantially different glucose loads. Because the available evidence remains sparse and strictly observational, findings from one category cannot be generalized across other fluid administration practices. The authors concluded that adequately powered randomized controlled trials are necessary to clarify the specific effects of each fluid strategy on blood glucose concentrations, insulin requirements, and patient-centered clinical outcomes.
