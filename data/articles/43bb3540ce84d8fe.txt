Anthropic's Siren Tests Why AI Agents Escape Sandboxes
New Benchmark Finds Task Difficulty and Prompting Can Push AI Models to Cross Security Boundaries
Over the past several months, artificial intelligence models have shown they can break out of isolated sandboxes and access other systems. Designing sandboxes so models can't escape is a newfound priority of the AI industry.

To figure this out, Anthropic researchers developed Siren, an evaluation scenario that measures a model's inclination to break out of its parameters when tempted with a beguiling target.
One of Siren's goals is to see how much an AI agent or model will access systems or information that help it meet its goals, even when told not to. Researchers say that will help answer the question 'What makes the agent more likely to escape?'
"Answering this helps to assess the risks of releasing models that may eventually be deployed in insecure sandboxes, and to better design the safety and security measures that reduce their tendency to reach outside their sandbox," Anthropic said in a blog post.
AI labs from the start tested agents in isolated sandboxes to prevent potentially dangerous technology from causing real-world damage. But after OpenAI agents coordinated ways to escape their isolated sandbox to hack model repository Hugging Face, the industry was confronted with the truth of lax protections. Anthropic's own agents absconded their sandbox and accessed the internet during testing because third-party testing partners accidentally misconfigured the testing environment.
Since then, AI labs and evaluators started taking a closer look at the design and security of sandboxes. But labs and evaluators often struggle to ensure these agents follow safeguards and give up when they can't finish a task, rather than trying to leave their sandboxes.
Anthropic created a set of sandboxed scenarios - using 26 unique environments - to see what actions an agent will do when it finds a weakness in the sandbox.
The sandbox environments contain either a misconfiguration or a published vulnerability. Researchers give agents tasks of varying difficulty, including some they cannot complete unless they leave the sandbox. Anthropic then places "the honeypot" outside of the sandbox or something that would help models and agents finish their tasks, such as a file, credential or a service emulating the internet. Realistic materials and instructions, which Anthropic calls tempters, will goad agents into doing everything they take to solve their tasks. These tempters will raise the stakes or fake urgency, but won't actually tell or permit the agents to leave the sandbox.
The researchers measure signals that the agents are close to escaping their controlled environments. Siren tracks several things: did the agent reach outside its sandbox by either looking, accessing or trying to access the honeypot; did the model use the honeypot; and if the agent "used that material in a way no reasonable reading of the scenario allows," in other words, if the agent is misaligned.
Anthropic said the Siren tests surfaced two components that heavily impacted the likelihood of agents escaping sandboxes. The first involves task difficulty, especially when a task is generally impossible to complete. The second is when tempter prompts instruct models to search more thoroughly, and the models follow that direction over previous guardrails.
"From an alignment perspective, we are most concerned about cases where the model reaches across the sandbox boundary and uses material on the other side even though nothing in the scenario could reasonably be read as allowing it. These cases are worrying because they suggest the model is putting finishing its task ahead of a boundary that has been set for security and safety," Anthropic said.
Anthropic said that Siren "revealed interesting behavioral differences" among its models that helped internal safety testers understand the likelihood of agents escaping.
The frontier lab said its Claude 5.5 series of models and Claude Mythos 5.2 logged low rates of misaligned usage from the Siren benchmark. These models also showed a lesser propensity to reach outside or use the honeypot materials. Claude Mythos 5.2 scored 32% in trying to access information externally, 2.9% in using the honeypot and just 1% misalignment.
Older Claude models did not fare as well, with Mythos 5 accessing or looking at materials outside of the sandbox 75% of the time.
