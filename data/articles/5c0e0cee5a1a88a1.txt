TITLE: Tai Chi May Reduce Insomnia Severity and Daytime Sleepiness in Older Adults
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Tai Chi significantly reduces insomnia severity and daytime sleepiness in middle-aged and older adults experiencing sleep disturbances, according to a systematic review, meta-analysis, and trial sequential analysis published in Aging Clinical and Experimental Research. Conducted by Bilal Wazir Khan and colleagues, the investigation evaluated whether the practice could serve as an effective non-pharmacological intervention for older populations. Sleep disturbances frequently affect older individuals and contribute to impaired physical function, cognitive decline, and reduced overall quality of life. Although Tai Chi has emerged as a promising non-pharmacological intervention, previous findings across randomized controlled trials had remained inconsistent, prompting this comprehensive evaluation of cumulative clinical evidence.
To evaluate the intervention systematically, the authors conducted their review and meta-analysis in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines, registering the protocol in the PROSPERO database under registration number CRD420261377755. The research team searched five major electronic databases—PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov—from database inception through February 2026. The investigators designated insomnia severity and sleep quality as their primary clinical outcomes. Random-effects meta-analyses were used to pool treatment effects across trials, while trial sequential analysis was employed to evaluate the sufficiency of cumulative evidence and determine the potential need for further randomized trials.
The systematic search identified seven randomized controlled trials comprising nine distinct comparisons. Methodological quality and risk of bias within the individual trials were appraised using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. Among the seven included trials, three studies were judged to have an overall low risk of bias, while four studies were assessed as having some concerns. Notably, no study was judged to have an overall high risk of bias across the assessed domains, establishing the baseline methodological quality of the synthesized comparative clinical trials.
Compared with combined control conditions, Tai Chi produced statistically significant reductions in insomnia symptoms and daytime sleepiness. Insomnia severity assessed via the Insomnia Severity Index decreased significantly, demonstrating a pooled mean difference of minus 1.70 (95% confidence interval minus 3.15 to minus 0.25; P equals 0.02), supported by low-certainty evidence. In addition, daytime sleepiness evaluated through the Epworth Sleepiness Scale demonstrated a statistically significant improvement, yielding a pooled mean difference of minus 1.43 (95% confidence interval minus 2.49 to minus 0.36; P equals 0.008), which was supported by moderate-certainty evidence under the evaluation framework.
In contrast to insomnia severity and daytime sleepiness, subjective sleep quality showed no significant overall improvement across the primary pooled comparisons. As measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, the difference between Tai Chi and combined control conditions was not statistically significant in the main analysis, showing a mean difference of minus 0.60 (95% confidence interval minus 1.75 to 0.55; P equals 0.31; very-low-certainty evidence), although the effect did become statistically significant following sensitivity analysis. Furthermore, the meta-analysis revealed no significant effects on objective sleep architecture parameters, including total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep onset latency, or wake after sleep onset, nor were significant changes observed for psychological outcomes.
Across the twenty outcome estimates assessed using the GRADE framework, the authors found that certainty of evidence was moderate for six outcomes, low for three outcomes, and very low for eleven outcomes, with imprecision and inconsistency identified as the principal reasons for downgrading. The authors concluded that Tai Chi may represent an accessible alternative non-pharmacological intervention to manage sleep disturbances by improving insomnia severity and daytime sleepiness in middle-aged and older adults. However, because objective sleep architecture was largely unchanged, the number of included randomized trials was small, sample sizes were limited, and evidence certainty remained low or very low for most outcomes, the authors emphasized that findings must be interpreted cautiously, noting that further high-quality randomized trials are needed to confirm whether these observed benefits are sustained and clinically meaningful.
