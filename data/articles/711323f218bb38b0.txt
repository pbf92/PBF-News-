TITLE: Single-Plant Supplements Show Small Strength and Testosterone Gains in Weight-Lifting Men
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Nutrition indicates that orally administered supplements derived from a single identifiable botanical species can yield modest adjunctive improvements in dynamic maximal strength and resting circulating total testosterone concentrations among adult men concurrently participating in structured resistance training. Conducted by an international team of researchers from Jiangsu Normal University in China and Universiti Sains Malaysia, the investigation pooled quantitative data across 16 randomized controlled trials comprising 17 independent randomized comparisons and 564 healthy male participants. By restricting eligibility exclusively to randomized designs wherein both the intervention and control cohorts completed identical or rigorously matched physical training programs, the study specifically evaluated whether botanical products provide measurable physical performance and endocrine advantages beyond the proven adaptations driven by mechanical resistance exercise alone, rather than contrasting supplementation against an inactive sedentary lifestyle.
However, because individual physiological adaptations to structured lifting protocols exhibit substantial inter-individual variability, athletes and active adults frequently seek nutritional adjuncts capable of augmenting strength gains. Botanical products represent a widely utilized yet biologically complex class of commercial supplements, ranging from crude powdered plant parts and standardized botanical extracts to isolated, highly enriched phytochemical constituents. Prior literature reviews frequently combined multi-ingredient commercial formulations, synthesized disparate cohorts that pooled men and women without sex-disaggregated controls, or included trials lacking structured exercise regimens. Consequently, attributing functional gains to specific botanical sources remained challenging. To address these methodological gaps, the authors designed a focused synthesis evaluating whether single-source botanicals offer demonstrable, additive physical or physiological benefits under matched lifting conditions in nonclinical adult male cohorts.
The methodological framework of the investigation was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO international database under identifier CRD420261468034 and adhered to the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines alongside methodological procedures established in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Literature searches systematically queried PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library from their respective database inceptions through July 1, 2026, without linguistic restrictions. The eligibility criteria mandated that included trials be randomized controlled trials evaluating orally consumed preparations derived from a single identifiable plant species administered for a minimum continuous duration of four weeks. To isolate the independent contribution of the botanical agent, trials had to incorporate identical or matched resistance-training protocols between experimental and control arms, with control arms receiving placebos, matched excipients, or non-supplemented matched training controls. Eligible studies were required to report dynamic repetition-maximum strength outcomes, while trials evaluating multi-plant blends or confounding active ingredients that could not be experimentally isolated were excluded.
Across the literature search, an initial pool of 915 database records was retrieved, yielding 492 unique records following automated and manual duplicate removal. After screening titles and abstracts, the researchers excluded 462 non-relevant citations and retrieved 30 complete full-text publications for detailed eligibility assessment. Following the exclusion of 14 full-text reports that failed to satisfy strict inclusion criteria, 16 peer-reviewed clinical trials published between 1997 and 2025 were selected for final quantitative meta-analysis. These 16 trials encompassed 564 adult male subjects and generated 17 independent cohort comparisons, as one trial independently randomized distinct cohorts of untrained and resistance-trained participants. Across the synthesized evidence base, individual trial sample sizes ranged from 16 to 98 men. Ten comparisons evaluated untrained or recreationally active individuals, while seven focused on dedicated resistance-trained lifters or competitive collegiate athletes. The training regimens averaged three to four supervised sessions weekly, spanning intervention durations between 4.3 and 12 weeks.
The meta-analytic pooling revealed statistically significant, small-to-moderate overall enhancements favoring botanical supplementation across both upper-body and lower-body dynamic maximal strength. For upper-body dynamic maximal strength, informed by 17 comparisons encompassing 563 analyzed participants, the random-effects restricted maximum likelihood model yielded a standardized mean difference expressed as Hedges’ g of 0.32, with a 95% confidence interval spanning from 0.06 to 0.58 and an associated p-value of 0.01. Statistical heterogeneity for this upper-body outcome was moderate, demonstrating an I-squared value of 53%. For lower-body dynamic maximal strength, synthesized across 15 comparisons involving 529 men, supplementation demonstrated a slightly larger pooled effect size of Hedges’ g equal to 0.44, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.20 to 0.67, an associated p-value of 0.0003, and an I-squared value of 39%. The authors prioritized lowest available repetition maximums, using one-repetition maximum bench press and squat assessments wherever available to ensure standardized biomechanical comparisons.
Secondary functional evaluations examined muscular endurance, defined operationalized repetitions completed to voluntary muscular failure or total repetitions performed at predetermined submaximal percentages of baseline strength. Supplementation elicited a statistically significant beneficial effect on upper-body muscular endurance across eight comparisons involving 252 participants, generating a pooled Hedges’ g of 0.64 with a 95% confidence interval ranging from 0.22 to 1.06 and a p-value of 0.003, alongside substantial statistical heterogeneity of 60%. Conversely, analysis of lower-body muscular endurance, derived from four independent comparisons encompassing 162 participants, yielded no statistically significant distinction between supplementation and control groups, displaying a pooled Hedges’ g of -0.05, a 95% confidence interval of -0.41 to 0.30, and a p-value of 0.77. The researchers highlighted that testing protocols for muscular endurance exhibited notable procedural variability across original trials, including differing relative percentages of maximum load and divergent task termination rules, which likely restricted direct cross-study comparability.
Endocrine profiling demonstrated a consistent and statistically significant elevation in circulating total testosterone concentrations among participants allocated to botanical supplementation compared to matched exercise controls. Synthesizing six comparisons involving 261 adult men, the standardized mean difference demonstrated a pooled Hedges’ g of 0.47, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.22 to 0.73, an associated p-value of 0.0002, and zero observed statistical heterogeneity, as indicated by an I-squared value of 0%. In an additional clinically interpretable absolute metric analysis wherein post-intervention hormone concentrations were converted directly to nanograms per deciliter, botanical supplementation produced an absolute weighted mean difference of 67.51 ng/dL above control conditions, with a 95% confidence interval between 30.02 and 104.99 ng/dL. Fenugreek and ashwagandha extracts contributed prominently to this endocrine evidence set. However, the authors explicitly emphasized that original investigations rarely controlled blood-sampling timing relative to diurnal rhythms, fasting status, or proximity to acute exercise sessions, precluding definitive mechanistic conclusions regarding whether elevated circulating testosterone directly mediated observed muscular force adaptations.
In marked contrast to the performance gains and circulating androgen elevations, single-plant supplementation exerted no statistically discernible adjunctive influence on objective body composition alterations over the studied training periods. Whole-body lean mass or fat-free mass, quantitatively synthesized across eight independent comparisons encompassing 219 participants, demonstrated an inconsequential weighted mean difference of 0.19 kilograms favoring the intervention groups, with a wide 95% confidence interval extending from -1.81 to 2.20 kilograms, an associated p-value of 0.85, and an I-squared of 0%. Similarly, relative body fat percentage, evaluated across 11 comparisons representing 430 participants, revealed a non-significant mean reduction of -0.54 percentage points under the primary random-effects framework, with a 95% confidence interval between -1.64 and 0.56 percentage points and an associated p-value of 0.34.
To investigate potential moderators contributing to clinical and statistical heterogeneity, exploratory subgroup analyses categorized trials by botanical preparation type, participant baseline training status, and intervention duration. Categorization by preparation format compared 11 comparisons examining crude whole-plant or standardized extracts against six comparisons assessing isolated or highly enriched phytochemical constituents such as capsiate, gamma-oryzanol, or ecdysteroids, yielding non-significant differences for upper-body strength (p = 0.73) and lower-body strength (p = 0.10). Likewise, baseline training classification contrasting untrained individuals against competitive athletes or experienced weightlifters revealed no significant subgroup divergences (p = 0.35 for upper body, p = 0.20 for lower body). Stratifying intervention exposure across short durations of four to eight weeks versus extended durations exceeding eight weeks similarly failed to identify modifier effects (p = 0.71 and p = 0.63, respectively). However, because many individual botanical species were represented by only single comparisons, the statistical power of these exploratory moderator analyses remained inherently limited.
Extensive sensitivity analyses and uncertainty evaluations were executed to probe the robust character of the meta-analytic calculations. Applying fixed-effect modeling and sequential leave-one-out sensitivity iterations maintained the statistical significance of primary findings across upper-body strength, lower-body strength, upper-body endurance, and testosterone. However, body fat percentage proved acutely sensitive to analytical choices; the outcome achieved statistical significance under fixed-effect assumptions (mean difference of -0.82 percentage points) and became statistically significant in random-effects modeling upon omitting a single influential trial by Moura and colleagues.
Methodological quality appraisal using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool classified 15 of the 16 included randomized trials as presenting some concerns overall, while one study demonstrated low risk of bias and none was graded at high overall risk. Primary methodological vulnerabilities stemmed from incomplete reporting regarding random sequence generation, concealed allocation procedures, and a lack of prospectively registered trial protocols or prespecified analytical plans to rule out selective reporting.
