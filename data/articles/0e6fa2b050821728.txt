TITLE: The People of God Are a People Who Feast
OUTLET: Christianity Today

With November comes the commencement of an all-too familiar American holiday tradition: the decadent food indulgences of our Thanksgiving and Christmas festivities followed by the anxious dieting of our New Year’s resolutions.
I came of age at the height of the diet culture of the ’90s and early 2000s. I was never diagnosed with an eating disorder, but my relationship with food edged near pathological. My life ordered itself by the meticulous counting of calories and a rigorous regimen of exercise—habits that frustratingly never seemed to budge my body from its God-given shape and size.
Thus, the holiday season for me was often a time of intense shame: shame that I’d never managed to reach the weight loss goals I’d set at the beginning of the year and shame that my delight-starved body just couldn’t seem to say no to that shimmering slice of pumpkin pie, the crumbly cornbread stuffing, or the crispy Christmas cookies.
I figured God might tolerate my dietary misdeeds in the weeks between Thanksgiving and Christmas (this was, after all, the lead-up to his Son’s birthday party), so long as I fully committed myself to painstaking restraint after the new year hit. In the evangelical subculture of my childhood, I’d developed a few misguided ideas about what God thought of food and the body. “The flesh” (its hungers, its desires, its proclivities) was to be tamed; thus, the calorie counting. Eve’s taste of forbidden fruit had ushered sin into the world; thus, food was suspect. Biblical writers from the wise King Solomon to the apostle Paul warned against gluttony; thus, I should order the salad.
These wrong assumptions were helped along by the many faith-based diet books that lined the shelves of Christian bookstores and church libraries, books like I Prayed Myself Slim, Devotions for Dieters, and Slim for Him (“Him” being God). Disdain for the body (and all its imperfections) was shared, woman to woman, in whispered tones during coffee hour and at potlucks, as they poked at their plates.
The implicit (and sometimes explicit) message was that a slimmed-down body was a sign of a sanctified soul. Jesus presumably didn’t want frumpy, sloppy servants. I needed to coerce, quiet, and starve my body into submission.
From the other side, secular diet and wellness culture has often leveraged religious language. We refer to foods that are particularly pleasurable as “sinfully delicious.” If we eat lots of vegetables on any given day, we declare that we have been “good.” Eating dessert evokes a confession that we have been “bad” or “cheated.” Diet food products are often described as “guilt-free.” We proudly display “before” and “after” photos of body transformations much like we tell a conversion story of being born again.
Wariness of the origins and impact of diet culture has grown in recent years. But Christian Wellness influencers have stepped into the gap, branding their ideology as faithful stewardship of the temple of God. Their emphasis is readying the body for service to the gospel rather than a number on the scale, but their tactics—what to eat, how to hydrate, how much to lift—are just as restrictive.
All this better-body rhetoric can make it hard to believe that feasting is actually a vital and necessary part of a life of holiness.
That so many Christians have failed to cultivate a robust theology of food is mystifying given the important role food plays in our sacred text. Yes, there was Eve’s ill-conceived meal with the snake, and we can’t deny the admonitions against gluttony. But the Bible begins and ends with delight in food. The story of God and his people starts with a garden full of luscious fruits and concludes with a wedding feast in heaven. The psalmist tells us to “taste and see that the Lord is good” (Ps. 34:8). Food as idiom, imagery, symbolism, and parable shows up again and again in the Old and New Testament.
The “flesh” (the Greek word sarx) is indeed fallen, with easily corrupted desires. But the “body” (the Greek word soma) isn’t its synonym—and the body was designed by God to hunger, to receive pleasure and satiation from food. Jesus himself often embraced the pleasures of the body, and was rumored to be a drunkard and a glutton because he so enjoyed a convivial dinner party (Matt. 11:19).
When churches do talk about spiritual formation and food, we often focus on fasting. Fasting is indeed a holy habit, often practiced by the people of God in times of trial or communal confession.
But what we fail to recognize is that in Scripture, the people of God are found feasting far more often than they are found fasting! The Passover meal and the Feast of Unleavened Bread commemorate the Israelites’ flight from slavery. The Feast of Weeks celebrates the giving of the Ten Commandments to Moses on Mount Sinai. The Feast of Tabernacles memorializes the Israelites’ dependence on God during their 40-year wilderness journey and corresponds with the harvest. These annual communal feast days, commanded by God, forged the bonds of love between the Lord and his people, and among the people themselves.
They were also acts of remembrance for God’s faithfulness in the past and an act of hope that his promises would be fulfilled in the future. Scientists note that the part of the brain that interprets taste, the gustatory cortex, has strong connections to the parts of the brain that process emotions, form memories, and retrieve those memories later. Our brains integrate the taste and smell of food with the experiences we have while eating those foods.
In other words, taste is tied to memory. And memory is tied to truth: the truth of what God has done for us, the truth of what his presence is like, the truth of what he has promised for our future. This is perhaps why Christ, when he set for his followers one sacred ritual by which to remember him, gave them bread and wine. Bread (an emblem of basic provision) and wine (a signifier of abundance and joy) teach us that God is present in both the mundane and the magnificent, in both our surviving and our thriving.
Whether by savoring the meat sacrificed on ancient altars or breaking bread with his followers, God seems to make a habit of dining with his people. It is a habit that will continue into the eschaton. “Here I am! I stand at the door and knock,” the Son of Man says in Revelation 3:20. “If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in and eat with that person, and they with me.”
To be reconciled to God, it seems, is to eat with God. And maybe being saved by God feels an awful lot like being fed by God.
And so, as the holidays approach, may we trust that our feasting need not be clouded by shame. It can be a holy act of remembrance. By savoring the foods God made good (perhaps with the help of Grandma’s recipe), we commemorate and offer embodied praise for the provision of both sustenance and delight. We embrace the love of family, the shared hardships and triumphs of communal life. We look back in awe at the presence of the incarnate Christ, breaking bread and pouring wine. And we look forward to the day when the whole family of God will gather around a wedding feast and together “taste and see” that the Lord is good indeed!
Amanda Held Opelt is a speaker, songwriter, and author of three books, including her latest, Provisions: Finding Home through the Food Folklore of the Appalachian Table. She lives in Boone, North Carolina, with her husband and two young daughters.
