TITLE: Meta-Analysis Finds Ramadan Fasting Does Not Increase Overall Migraine Frequency
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis published in The Egyptian Journal of Neurology, Psychiatry and Neurosurgery indicates that Ramadan fasting among adult Muslim patients diagnosed with clinically verified migraine is not associated with a statistically significant change in monthly migraine days, attack counts, episode duration, or headache pain intensity. Although the pooled paired comparison revealed a statistically significant increase in the monthly number of days on which acute abortive medications and analgesics were utilized, the overall body of evidence failed to establish a consistent exacerbation of primary migraine symptoms. The authors stressed that extreme statistical heterogeneity and pervasive methodological limitations across the contributing studies prevent definitive clinical conclusions, underscoring that current scientific data do not justify assertions that intermittent diurnal fasting systematically worsens the clinical trajectory of migraine disorders.
Ramadan fasting represents an annual religious observance undertaken by a substantial portion of the global Muslim population, characterized by complete abstinence from all food, liquids, and oral pharmacological agents from dawn until sunset. Daily fasting intervals vary substantially depending on geographic latitude and the astronomical season, spanning anywhere between eleven and twenty hours. These rigorous dietary restrictions precipitate profound alterations in circadian biology, sleep architecture, meal scheduling, daytime alertness, systemic hydration, metabolic balance, and therapeutic compliance. In susceptible individuals, physiological disruptions such as fluid deficits, delayed sleep onset, altered hypothalamic regulation, and caffeine withdrawal can act as recognized triggers for cranial pain. The International Classification of Headache Disorders recognizes headache attributed to fasting as a distinct secondary disorder, typically presenting as mild-to-moderate bilateral pain that swiftly resolves following nutritional intake. However, distinguishing this secondary phenomenon from the acute exacerbation of an underlying primary migraine remains clinically vital, given that migraine affects an estimated 14.0 to 15.2 percent of the global population and ranks as the second leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide.
To evaluate the empirical relationship between intermittent diurnal fasting and migraine outcomes, the investigators executed a rigorous search protocol adhering strictly to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines and methodological principles established in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The systematic review protocol was registered prospectively in the PROSPERO database under registration number CRD420261359701, proceeding without subsequent deviations regarding eligibility parameters or analytical workflows. Five principal electronic repositories—specifically PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library—were interrogated from their respective inception dates through March 2026. The initial bibliographic retrieval yielded 450 distinct citations. After removing 192 duplicate records, two reviewers independently appraised 258 titles and abstracts using the Rayyan web-based screening platform, which resulted in 128 exclusions. Full-text examinations were subsequently conducted on 18 candidate articles, resulting in 14 exclusions due to ineligible study populations, conference abstracts, or non-peer-reviewed correspondence, while one additional relevant paper was identified via backward citation tracking.
Ultimately, five observational investigations encompassing 759 unique adult participants satisfied the pre-specified inclusion criteria. Eligible investigations were required to enroll adult Muslim patients aged 18 years or older who presented with clinically verified migraine diagnoses established according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders second or third editions. All included studies were observational in design, consisting of two prospective cohort evaluations and three cross-sectional investigations executed across the Middle East and North Africa region between 2009 and 2025. Specifically, the geographic distribution encompassed cohorts assessed in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Algeria, and Israel, reflecting regions where Muslim communities represent over 90 percent of the resident demographic. Individual study sample sizes ranged from 30 to 293 individuals, with mean participant ages spanning 30 to 37 years. Female participants predominated across every evaluated cohort, accounting for 73 to 90 percent of participants, thereby aligning with recognized worldwide epidemiological patterns. Episodic migraine was identified as the predominant clinical subtype, followed by chronic migraine and migraine without aura, while reported daily fasting durations ranged from 14 to 15 hours.
Methodological quality across the primary literature was systematically appraised using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, which evaluates observational literature across the discrete domains of participant selection, cohort comparability, and outcome ascertainment. The two included cohort studies were classified as possessing fair methodological quality, each receiving intermediate scores of five and six stars out of nine. Evaluators noted that one cohort enrolled exclusively from a single specialized neurology clinic serving Bedouin settlements in the Negev desert, utilizing a crossover design where 30 patients acted as their own historical controls without a separate unexposed comparison group. Furthermore, neither cohort established baseline stability prior to Ramadan, with one study documenting that 22.9 percent of enrolled patients exhibited active migraine symptoms during pre-fasting assessments. The three cross-sectional studies were classified as satisfactory, garnering scores between five and six points.
Quantitative meta-analyses were executed using R software version 4.5.2 via the meta package, applying random-effects models incorporating the Sidik-Jonkman estimator for between-study tau-squared variance along with the Hartung-Knapp adjustment for calculating confidence intervals. Where continuous trial data were documented exclusively as medians and interquartile ranges, values were mathematically converted into sample means and standard deviations utilizing the validated formulas formulated by Wan and colleagues. For studies comparing the same individuals across non-fasting control months—such as Shaban, Shawal, or Ragab—and Ramadan, within-participant paired differences were derived using Cochrane generic inverse-variance procedures. Because standard deviations of paired differences were absent from all published primary reports, standard deviations of change were derived using an assumed within-participant correlation coefficient of r equals 0.5, alongside sensitivity evaluations testing r values of 0.3 and 0.7. When studies reported repeated outcome assessments across consecutive ten-day intervals, measurements were pooled into composite parameters using Cochrane formulaic guidelines to eliminate duplicate participant counting.
The primary outcome synthesis regarding the monthly number of migraine days demonstrated no statistically significant difference between Ramadan fasting and non-fasting comparison periods, reporting a pooled paired mean difference of 3.28 days with a 95 percent confidence interval ranging from negative 1.54 to positive 8.10 days and a p-value of 0.1189. However, this metric exhibited considerable between-study statistical heterogeneity, quantified by an I-squared value of 98.3 percent and a Cochran Q-test p-value below 0.001. After excluding Gabr and colleagues in a sensitivity analysis, the pooled paired estimate was 4.68 days (95% CI 1.98 to 7.39; p = 0.0176) with heterogeneity decreasing to 81.2 percent, though researchers considered this finding exploratory given the small number of studies and its post hoc nature.
Similarly, the pooled paired analysis of discrete migraine attack frequency failed to reveal a statistically significant difference between fasting and non-fasting periods, yielding a paired mean difference of 0.74 attacks with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning negative 7.71 to positive 9.19 attacks and an associated p-value of 0.7418. This outcome was marked by extreme heterogeneity, reaching an I-squared of 99.7 percent, which profoundly compromised the clinical interpretability of the numerical summary. Subgroup analyses comparing prospective cohort studies against cross-sectional designs revealed no statistically meaningful divergence between methodologies, yielding a non-significant subgroup p-value of 0.9086. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses affirmed the mathematical stability of this null finding, as recalculated mean difference values fluctuated between negative 0.89 and positive 2.51 attacks, with every recalculated confidence interval intersecting zero. Planned assessments of small-study effects and publication bias via funnel plots and Egger linear regression were precluded because fewer than ten primary articles contributed to each individual outcome domain.
Analyses evaluating secondary headache parameters displayed similarly mixed patterns. Attack duration demonstrated no significant alteration between Ramadan and the non-fasting baseline month of Shaban, reporting a paired mean difference of 0.30 hours with a 95 percent confidence interval of negative 8.53 to positive 9.13 hours, accompanied by minimal heterogeneity at an I-squared of 25.9 percent. An exploratory single-arm pooling of raw episode durations generated a mean length of 14.49 hours across three studies, though constrained by immense heterogeneity at 99.8 percent. Subjective headache intensity measured via the 10-point Visual Analog Scale likewise showed no significant divergence, reporting a paired mean difference of 0.13 points with a 95 percent confidence interval of negative 1.23 to positive 1.48 points and an I-squared of 81.6 percent. Excluding a Kuwaiti cross-sectional investigation resolved this statistical heterogeneity to an I-squared of zero percent, yet the recalculated effect remained non-significant at a paired mean difference of negative 0.15 points.
In marked contrast to symptom counts, the pooled analysis of acute pharmacotherapy across two investigations demonstrated a statistically significant increase in medication utilization during Ramadan. Patients recorded an average of 5.00 additional days of analgesic and abortive medication intake compared to baseline non-fasting periods, producing a pooled paired mean difference of 5.00 days with a narrow 95 percent confidence interval of 4.61 to 5.40 days and a significant p-value of 0.0040. Crucially, no statistical heterogeneity was detected across these cohorts, reflected in an I-squared of zero percent. Concurrently, a single-arm synthesis evaluating fasting continuity established that approximately 21 percent of participating individuals broke their religious fast prematurely as a direct consequence of intolerable migraine episodes, with individual cohort proportions ranging broadly from 2 to 79 percent under substantial heterogeneity measuring 88.0 percent.
Individual study findings underscored notable behavioral and temporal variations across patient groups. In one prospective investigation, participants experienced a paradoxically significant decline in attack counts, headache days, and total pharmacological requirements during Ramadan, which authors hypothesized might stem from structured nocturnal meal timing reducing hypoglycemic precipitating events, noting peak attack onsets occurred between 2:00 PM and 4:30 PM.
