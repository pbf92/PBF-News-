TITLE: Character.ai faces 35 states' questions under oath on users' data
OUTLET: PPC Land

The National Association of Attorneys General today served notice that 35 states and the District of Columbia will depose Character Technologies, the bankrupt company behind Character.ai, on November 30, and listed 15 matters its witnesses must be prepared to answer under oath, from how the company checks users' ages to whether the AI models it plans to sell "can reproduce or reveal users' personal information." The five-page notice of a Rule 30(b)(6) deposition, filed as Docket No. 199 in the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, sets the examination for 9:30 a.m. Eastern, the day before the auction for the company's assets. It is also the first filing by the states to name Smirks and Pangs, the buyer lined up to pay $10 million for the Character.ai app, its models and its users' chats. The two state objections filed on November 18 did not mention that buyer; today's notice names it four times.
In Short
Thirty-five states and Washington, DC, want Character.ai to put a witness under oath on November 30 to explain what data it holds on its users, including children, and where that data has ended up inside the technology it is selling to Disney and to a newly formed company called Smirks and Pangs. The questions go to the center of both deals: whether the tools licensed to Disney were built or tested on users' chats, and whether the AI models offered for $10 million could give users' personal information back out. The testimony is scheduled one day before the December 1 auction and three days before the judge takes up the sales on December 3.
What the notice requires
The notice is short and procedural, and its force comes from the rule it invokes. It is brought under Rules 26 and 30 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, which apply in bankruptcy through Federal Rules of Bankruptcy Procedure 7026, 7030 and 9014. The last of those governs contested matters, the category of dispute that a sale motion becomes once parties object to it, as the states and Texas did on November 18.
According to the notice, the NAAG Client States "will take the deposition of the Debtor, Character Technologies, Inc., by oral examination of its designee(s) under Rule 30(b)(6), on November 30, 2026 at 9:30 am (ET), or at some other time mutually agreed upon by the parties." The examination will take place "remotely via a mutually agreed videoconferencing service and recorded stenographically by a person authorized by law to administer oaths." The notice provides for a stenographic record and does not mention video.
Rule 30(b)(6) is the mechanism for questioning an organization rather than a person. The party serving the notice must describe the matters for examination with reasonable particularity, and the organization must designate one or more officers, directors, managing agents or other consenting people to testify on its behalf about information known or reasonably available to it. Since a 2020 amendment to the rule, both sides must also confer in good faith about those matters. The notice puts it in one sentence: "Under Fed. R. Civ. P. 30(b)(6), Character Technologies, Inc. should designate one or more persons to testify about the following matters."
The deposition is tied to the company's sale motion, Docket No. 15, filed on October 13. That motion sought, in two stages, an order approving bidding procedures and Disney Worldwide Services as stalking horse for the "Disney Target Assets," with authority to designate additional stalking horse purchasers, and then an order approving the sale itself. The first stage is done. The second is what the court will consider on December 3 and 4.
The 15 matters
The full list follows, in the notice's own words.
The list moves in a clear order. The first five matters ask what the company collected, what it told users and how it used the data. The next two ask where the data physically sits and who else holds it. Matters 8 through 10 turn to the two sales and to whether users and parents have been told. The last five go inside the technology itself.
Smirks and Pangs enters the states' filings
Until today, the states' objections had been aimed at Disney. Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC was formed in Delaware on November 13 and designated the next day as the stalking horse for every asset outside Disney's license, at $10 million in cash plus assumed liabilities, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai users' chats faced a $10m sale to Smirks and Pangs. Its asset schedule takes in the website and apps, all AI models and weights, account data, characters, chat content, advertising and analytics data and training datasets. The signed agreement, filed on November 16, requires a sale order approving the transfer of that data without any further consent, and the privacy and age statements the company made in it end at closing, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai's $10m sale was shown to hinge on moving chats without further consent.
Objections to the designation were due by 9 p.m. Eastern on November 18. The docket lists none. The coalition's 32-page objection, filed that afternoon, concerned the Disney license only, and neither it nor Texas's separate filing mentioned the second buyer, as PPC Land noted when 39 states and DC asked the court to deny Disney's $20m Character.ai deal. The states' first step on Smirks and Pangs is therefore a request for testimony, not for a ruling.
Four of the 15 matters name the buyer. Matters 8 and 9 ask what user data, models and related assets, and what information on users under 13 and under 18, are included in the sale to each buyer. Matter 13 goes to the models themselves. Matter 15 asks about anything both buyers would receive.
Can a model give back what it learned?
Matter 13 is the most technical question in the notice. It asks about "the AI models and weights to be sold to Smirks and Pangs, the user data used to train them, and whether those models can reproduce or reveal users' personal information." The notice does not claim that they can. It asks the company to say, under oath.
The concern has a research basis. In July 2025, PPC Land covered a study arguing that large language models qualify as personal data, which described experiments in which personal details stored in a model's parameters could be retrieved with targeted queries. A model's weights are the numbers it learns during training; if a model has memorized fragments of the conversations it was trained on, selling the weights can move those fragments with them, even where the chat logs themselves are excluded.
Matter 14 asks whether the problem can be fixed before any sale closes. It covers "the Debtor's ability to identify, remove or delete user data, including data of minors and users who opted out, from models, training datasets and other technology before any transfer." Deleting a record from a database does not remove what a model has already learned from it, and the notice asks the company to explain what it can actually do.
The question has been building in the case. In their notice of interest on October 14, the 39 states and the District of Columbia asked for limits on training with chat content without consent and for consumers to be able to delete their data before any sale, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai faced 40 attorneys general over who could buy its users' chats. On November 12, the Consumer Federation of America wrote to the judge that users' chats were built into the company's models and cited legal arguments for deleting models trained on improperly obtained data, as PPC Land reported in what the therapist bot heard. Matters 13 and 14 put both concerns into a form the company must answer on the record.
The Disney license, tested from the inside
Disney's side of the deal was built to stay away from personal data. The license filed with the sale motion excludes users' data, inputs and outputs, as well as "Unprocessed Data," defined as publicly sourced data not yet cleaned, de-identified or onboarded into training databases, and training data and "Finished Models," including each user-selectable conversational model, as PPC Land reported when Disney budgeted $180m for Character.ai staff and $20m for the bankrupt company. What Disney would license is code behind the company's training, safety and serving systems, plus nine patent entries. Disney would also own whatever it builds from that code, including models, weights and synthetic data, according to the same agreement.
Matters 11 and 12 test whether the exclusions hold. Matter 11 asks whether any "technology, models, classifiers, tools or patents" to be licensed to Disney "were developed, trained, tuned or tested using user data, including data of minors." Classifiers, the systems that sort content into categories such as allowed or prohibited, are usually trained on labeled examples; a safety classifier for a chatbot service would be trained on examples of the content it is meant to catch. Matter 12 asks for the company's "interpretation and application" of the exclusions "and how the Debtor will ensure that user data and models derived from it are not provided to Disney."
These follow the argument the coalition made on November 18: that licensed training infrastructure, evaluation frameworks and safety tools were likely built on real conversations, and that the license's definition of outputs could leave chat material embedded in licensed technology outside the exclusion. Advocacy groups made a version of the same point earlier. On November 12, the Heat Initiative and ParentsTogether Action asked the judge to keep chat-derived data out of the technology license, as PPC Land reported when groups asked the court to treat every Character.ai chat as a child's.
Matter 15 links the two deals. The Smirks and Pangs schedule includes the same nine patent entries that Disney would license, four issued, three pending and two not yet published. Smirks and Pangs would own them, and Disney would hold a perpetual, non-exclusive license to the same nine, drafted to survive a later change of control. The notice asks about "any safeguards for user data that will apply to each."
Databases, buckets and vendors
Matters 6 and 7 ask the company to identify "which databases and buckets hold users' personal data" and "which third-party vendors hold and/or process users' personal data." A bucket is the storage container used by cloud services such as Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud.
The contracts the company proposes to hand to Smirks and Pangs give a partial map. Exhibit B to the November 14 designation listed 55 contracts. Compute comes from DigitalOcean, Nebius and Amazon Web Services, and OpenAI appears under an AI services agreement. On the marketing side, AppLovin holds a services agreement, Moloco a software agreement, and AppsFlyer, a mobile measurement partner, a software agreement with a $16,401 cure cost. Amplitude holds a user analytics agreement and Persona a user verification agreement. DigitalOcean alone carried $2,646,136 of the $2,684,462 in listed cure costs, by PPC Land's calculation. On November 18, DigitalOcean filed a limited objection and reservation of rights to the company's notice of possible contract assumptions (Docket No. 193); the docket entry does not describe its terms.
A list of contracts does not show which vendors hold personal data, which is what matter 7 asks. Answered under oath, it would establish which ad networks, measurement companies, analytics providers and cloud hosts have held Character.ai users' information, and which of those relationships would move to a new owner.
Minors, age checks and notice to parents
Three matters concern children. Matter 4 asks about "age-verification practices" and the handling of data of users under 13 and under 18. Matter 9 asks how their personal information is included in the assets for sale. Matter 10 asks about "Notice of the proposed sales to users and their parents if the users are minors."
