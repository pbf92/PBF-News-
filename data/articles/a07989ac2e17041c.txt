Study unpicks benefits and risks of prostate screening
It reinforced benefits of PSA screening, but found gains are balanced with a greater risk of overdiagnosis and unnecessary follow-up.
    A new systematic review has highlighted both the benefits of prostate cancer screening, and given more detail on the long-term risk of overdiagnosis and false positives.
Carried out by researchers at the University of Alberta in Canada, the work found repeated screening of men aged 55–69 years is likely to reduce prostate cancer mortality, as well as the likelihood of a metastatic cancer diagnosis.
However, the study published on Tuesday in the US-based Annals of Internal Medicine also found men who had multiple prostate-specific antigen (PSA) tests were diagnosed with cancers that may never have caused symptoms, as well as identifying significantly more false-positive results.
Looking at 13 randomised controlled trials comprising 792,034 participants as well as three uncontrolled cohort studies involving 5593 men, researchers concluded screening every two to four years is likely to lead to around two fewer prostate fatalities in every 1000 men in that age category.
They also found around six fewer diagnoses of metastatic cancer.
Those findings were rated as having ‘moderate certainty’, alongside another that found around 20 extra cases of prostate cancer overdiagnosis and 150 further false positive cases.
The study comes after an updated guideline for detecting prostate cancer was endorsed by both the RACGP and the National Health and Medical Research Council earlier this year.
The guideline includes expanded detail on people at higher risk of ‘clinically significant’ cancer, while encouraging ‘active surveillance’ rather than intervention for those at lower risk.
Fremantle-based GP Dr Brett Montgomery represented the RACGP on the steering committee for the guidelines, and was also on the expert advisory panel.
He noted a Cochrane Review published earlier this year made similar findings to the new study.
‘Those authors were trying to do a very similar job to these authors in this new paper,’ he told newsGP.
‘It’s actually reassuring to see that two independent research teams have looked at the same body of evidence and come to very similar conclusions, it just makes me feel like someone’s double checked the homework and we can trust the numbers we’re seeing.’
The new study found no convincing evidence of any benefits to one-off screening, or from including digital rectal examination alongside PSA testing, while there was less certainty around the benefits of testing an older age-group.
Dr Montgomery said the updated guideline, which now recommends an MRI scan following a test result with raised PSA levels instead of going straight to a biopsy, is likely to be having an impact on preventing harms that is not reflected in the results of the recent study.
‘The MRI is roughly halving the number of people needing the next step, which is biopsy, compared to past practice, which probably means it’s roughly halving the number of over-diagnoses,’ he said.
‘It’s not just the MRI that’s changed, also urologists now are using different biopsy approaches, putting the needle through the perineal skin rather than the rectum – that’s a bit cleaner and it reduces infective risk.
‘The harms of over-diagnosis are still real but probably significantly smaller in magnitude than they were in these studies.’
