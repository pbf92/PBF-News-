TITLE: IAB Europe gives publishers a 90-day plan for AI bots, deals and ads
OUTLET: PPC Land

IAB Europe today released the Publisher Playbook for the Agentic Web, an interactive tool that takes publishers through scoring their exposure to AI systems, setting separate access rules for seven kinds of AI use, choosing among six ways to charge for content and running a first 90-day pilot. The association's Publisher Content & AI Task Force and its AI Working Group developed it, according to the announcement on IAB Europe's website.
The premise is a change in how people reach content. "AI is changing how people find and use content online," according to IAB Europe. "Instead of browsing, users increasingly ask AI interfaces for answers or delegate tasks to agents that research, compare and buy on their behalf. As a result, content, user intent and advertising can now be monetised in places publishers do not control."
The playbook runs to 22 interactive slides. Its cover asks one question - "Who gets the value?" - and answers that the issue "is not only traffic: it is who captures the value, and how publishers keep their share." It names four sources of that value: the user (attention, loyalty, first-party relationships), the content (authorized use of what is published), intent (leads, recommendations, transactions) and trust (editorial governance, identity, context). What the document does not do is just as explicit. "It sets out options and questions to consider. It does not recommend a single strategy, prices or contract terms," according to IAB Europe.
In Short
IAB Europe released an interactive playbook today, open to anyone online, that helps publishers decide how AI crawlers, chatbots and shopping agents may use their content, and how to get paid for it. It matters to any website that lives on ads, subscriptions or affiliate links, because more people now get answers from AI tools instead of visiting pages, and the money can follow them to whoever runs the AI tool. What changes is the method: you score yourself on 12 areas, set rules for each of seven AI uses instead of one site-wide "AI policy", check 13 questions before signing a deal and run a 90-day pilot, with no prices or standard contract terms supplied.
Who wrote it and who it is for
IAB Europe aims the document at "publisher commercial, editorial, legal, product and ad operations teams, and anyone responsible for decisions on AI access, licensing and advertising." After an opening section, the slides move through five chapters: Know your position, Decide access, Get paid, Run it, and Industry asks.
The tool is built to be used rather than read. Scores, sliders and checklists change as a reader works through them, and IAB Europe says nothing entered into the tool leaves the browser. The interactive parts include a 12-part self-assessment, a calculator comparing AI crawler requests with visits sent back by AI services, a set of sliders that suggests how open or closed to be, a checklist of 13 contract questions and an eight-item "recipe" for advertising.
The work came out of discussions in the two IAB Europe groups, and IAB Europe also thanks "BVDW's Working Group for its extensive feedback." BVDW is Germany's digital economy association. The AI Working Group is co-led by Katharina Schneeberg, Head of Innovation & Technology at BVDW, and Tobias Kellner, Industry Relations Lead DACH at Google. Dimitris Beis, Data & Innovation Strategist at IAB Europe, is the contact for the group, which holds monthly calls for IAB Europe members. Notes attached to several slides mark elements that were added in answer to comments from BVDW and its online marketers' group OVK, or to requests from the working group: the calculator comparing crawler requests with referral visits, the order in which access rules apply, the section on being found by AI, the safeguards for autonomous agents and the governance forum.
Four industry figures are quoted in the playbook. Carlos Bracho, VP Global AdTech at Axel Springer and unit lead for Lab AdTech & Programmatic at BVDW, described the change AI brings to discovery as "a huge opportunity, if we get it right" before adding a condition. "But being part of an AI-generated answer isn't enough. Publishers need to understand where their content creates value and how they can benefit from it." He said the work of IAB Europe and national associations helps shape "an AI ecosystem that works for everyone, not just the biggest platforms."
Mohsin Pervez, AVP Solutions Engineering, EMEA at PubMatic, said the company "was glad to contribute" and described the questions the playbook tackles, "from setting AI access purpose by purpose to getting paid for content," as "among the hottest topics we hear from publishers." Tom Peruzzi, SVP Publishers at Adform, said publishers "have to participate in the changes on the market driven by AI adoption on their client's side." Petri Kokkonen, a board member of IAB Finland and CEO of Relevant Digital, called it "an excellent collection of practical guidance, views and ideas for publishers to work with bots visiting their media."
Browse, ask, delegate
The playbook starts with three ways a person can reach a publisher. In browsing, the user "navigates results, feeds and links, and judges sources directly," and the publisher still controls presentation, ads, registration and subscription prompts. In asking, the user "requests a synthesised answer or recommendation from an AI interface," and the publisher's presence shrinks to "a citation, a link, an excerpt, or no visible source at all." Brand exposure and visits may fall, according to the playbook, and the publisher's contribution to the answer then needs its own measurement and licensing.
In delegating, the user "asks software to research, compare, book, buy or act." The publisher's touchpoint becomes a data feed, an API, a tool, a transaction endpoint or a page read by automation. Here the playbook warns that intent and transaction value move to the agent or platform unless the publisher is integrated into it.
These modes are not stages that replace one another. A note on the slide describes one user who asks an AI service, checks the answer on a cited source, subscribes and later hands monitoring to an agent, and adds: "The goal is not to force every journey back to a pageview."

Seven layers where value can move away
The first chapter breaks the problem into seven layers. Each comes with a question, the risk it carries and a possible response.
The roles named under layer 2 are destination, supplier, context, data or commerce. The slide ends with what a note calls the one idea to keep if nothing else: "Rule: Decide visibility, access and monetisation separately for each content category, rather than one 'AI policy' for the whole site."
A 36-point self-assessment
The self-assessment scores 12 areas from 0 to 3, for a maximum of 36. "Score on evidence, not aspiration," the slide says. A note explains that the four levels expand a simpler split between scores of 0-1 and 2-3 in the longer playbook text the slides draw on, and that the lowest scores should be read first because "a high total can hide a blocking weakness."

Once all 12 are scored, the tool names the weakest areas and suggests a first action, built on five example profiles in that longer text. If eight or more areas score 0 or 1, the suggestion is: "Avoid irreversible rights grants; establish governance, a baseline and an asset map before selecting a posture." A publisher strong on direct relationships but weak on licensing readiness gets: "Protect direct journeys, improve bot measurement and create a rights inventory before negotiating broad content use." One with distinctive assets but a weak direct relationship gets: "Productise authenticated access, define service levels and test enterprise or marketplace demand."
Two more profiles cover commerce and advertising. Strong intent and commerce with weak measurement leads to: "Instrument source IDs, lead quality and outcome reporting before expanding agent distribution." Strong advertising resilience with weak agent readiness leads to: "Define source and context signals and test publisher-owned or negotiated AI experiences." Any other mix points to the weakest area first.
Five classes of traffic, and a ratio to watch
Before any rule is set, the playbook asks publishers to know who is visiting. It sorts traffic into five classes, each with its own counting rule and commercial treatment.
The same slide carries a calculator for the crawl to referral ratio, which the playbook defines as "How many times AI crawlers fetch your pages for every visit an AI interface sends back." Its default example uses 120,000 AI crawler requests in a month against 800 AI referral visits, a ratio of 150 to 1. The slide labels these "Illustrative numbers" and asks for tracking "per content category and crawler: the trend matters more than the level." A note adds that bot share should be measured by more than one yardstick: requests, bytes, origin cost, content category, linked revenue and failures.
Real figures run far higher for some companies. Cloudflare data reported by PPC Land put Anthropic's ratio at 38,065 to 1 in July 2025 and OpenAI's at 1,091 to 1, against 5.4 to 1 for Google in the same month.
Seven AI uses, seven decisions
The slide IAB Europe calls "the centre of the playbook" splits AI access into seven purposes. Each gets a question to ask the AI operator and one posture to consider.
Each purpose also carries a privacy line and a measurement line. For model training, personal data in content "may enter model weights; hard to withdraw," and the use is "Usually invisible: no exposure, no referral; rely on contract reporting." Grounding is the term for an AI service fetching live pages to support an answer, and there "Retrieval logs may carry user query context: agree what the operator records." Entity extraction is "Hard to observe: extraction rarely sends traffic back."
Agent visits get the sharpest wording. An agent reading a page for one person is "Not a human impression: keep it out of audience and billable counts," and consent and ad signals "may not reach a human." For advertising use, "Targeting or personalisation needs its own lawful basis." The slide closes with a caution that applies across the chapter: "A thinking aid, not a formula. The right posture depends on your content, rights and partners; decide it per content category."
Seven settings between open and blocked
The playbook does not treat blocking as a yes-or-no choice. It sets out a seven-step spectrum of postures: Open, Declare, Allow list, Rate limits, Metered, Contract only and Block.
To place one content category and one use on that scale, the tool offers eight sliders, each running from "favours opening" to "favours control":
- Visibility: will the control reduce indexing, citations or referrals the publisher values?
- Substitution risk: could the use satisfy the user without identity, visit or payment?
- Cost to serve: what bandwidth, compute, security and support cost does it create?
- Commercial leverage: does the publisher have differentiated assets and a credible alternative?
- Rights confidence: can the publisher grant the relevant rights for every included asset?
- Enforcement: can identity, breach and use be established in practice?
- User impact: could it impair accessibility, browsing or logged-in services?
- Operational burden: can the rule be maintained as crawlers and standards change?
The average of the sliders points to a posture. A clear lean toward opening returns "Leaning open: keep discovery, declare preferences and measure." A clear lean the other way returns "Leaning control: gate or meter access, and record the evidence for the decision."
