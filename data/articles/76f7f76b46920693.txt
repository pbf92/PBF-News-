TITLE: You aren’t just what you eat
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Everyone agrees that a healthy diet and regular exercise together are important for a healthier life.
However, Glenn Gaesser, a professor at Arizona State University’s College of Health Solutions, suggests that exercise outweighs diet when it comes to longevity.
Gaesser and Robert Sallis, a family medicine and sports medicine physician at Kaiser Permanente, compared decades of research on the correlation between diet, exercise and cardiorespiratory fitness with all-cause mortality.
Their analysis, published this week in Mayo Clinic Proceedings, found that physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness were more strongly associated with lower mortality risk than diet quality.
“We basically wanted to set the record straight about quantifying the impact of physical activity and dietary quality with health outcomes,” Gaesser said. “We compared — in a number of different ways — dietary quality with physical activity to see which had the stronger relationship.
"In every instance, it was physical activity or cardiorespiratory fitness that had a much stronger relationship to your longevity prospects.”
It’s the first time the research has ever been compared head-to-head.
“This analysis is about the quality of years, not just number of years,” Sallis said. “Most of us would choose quality years, and exercise adds that. I see many patients with a healthy BMI who are unhealthy. It’s very hard to find a patient who exercises regularly and is physically fit, but is not healthy.”
The duo searched PubMed for relevant meta-analyses and cohort studies. No date limitations were applied; however, Gaesser said nearly all of the data was published in the last 10 to 20 years.
Results from several meta-analyses found that 11,000 to 16,000 steps a day were associated with a 45% to 67% lower all-cause mortality risk compared with those averaging roughly 2,000 to 5,000 steps.
The intensity doesn't seem to be nearly as important as just getting steps in, Gaesser said.
In studies that measured diet and physical activity together, high activity or fitness levels also seemed to largely eliminate the excess risk associated with a lower-quality diet.
Gaesser emphasized that these findings don’t diminish the importance of a healthy diet but indicate that the disproportionate focus on diet in current public health priorities isn’t supported by the comparative evidence.
Below, Gaesser and Sallis go into more detail about the findings and what they hope comes next as a result.
Question: What did the findings show?
Gaesser: We found that the all-cause mortality risk for individuals with the lowest diet quality, or the highest consumption of ultra-processed foods, red and processed meat, sugary foods and beverages, or fried foods, was (increased by) 3% to 20%. The risk for someone who doesn’t exercise and sits almost all day was up by 46% to over 163%.
We asked what would happen if sedentary people increased their number of daily steps by 2,000 because it's approximately 1 mile and is consistent with current public health guidelines. That was associated with about a 25% to 50% reduction in mortality risk.
By contrast, increasing consumption of fruits and vegetables, whole grains and dietary fiber from current levels to those recommended by the U.S. dietary guidelines was associated with only a 6% to 20% lower risk of all-cause mortality.
Q: Can exercise offset the harm of an unhealthy diet and sedentary lifestyle?
Gaesser: We looked at several studies that required adult volunteers to consume a variety of unhealthy foods for one to two weeks, but also exercise daily or maintain between 11,000 and 12,000 steps a day. They found that there were no adverse health effects whatsoever. Their physical activity seemed to compensate for a poor diet.
Q: Why do you think physical activity has such a strong influence on health and longevity compared with diet?
Gaesser: An adult human has about 30% to 40% of their total body mass as muscle. Sedentary muscle is unhealthy muscle. Whenever a person exercises, the muscle releases hundreds, if not thousands, of chemicals that go to all other cells in the body. Those chemicals result in numerous positive effects on the health of all other tissues and organs, making them less likely to succumb to the effects of stress, inflammation and poor diet. We don't know that diet does the same thing.
Q: How much movement do people need to do to achieve these benefits?
Gaesser: Based on our results, we recommend that people increase their step count by 2,000 steps a day as a guide. That was associated with about a 25% to 50% reduction in mortality risk. But when people ask me what’s the best exercise, I always say the one you stick with.
Q: What do you hope these findings will mean for health care professionals and public health initiatives?
Sallis: Everybody in clinical medicine should be asking about exercise and encouraging their patients to do it. This should be a centerpiece of the “Make America Healthy Again” movement — not just “Food Is Medicine” but “Exercise Is Medicine.” They’re both medicine. Exercise is the more powerful medication, but you should do both together.
Gaesser: I hope people will see that both a healthy diet and physical activity or fitness are good, but the data from our review shows pretty compelling evidence that fitness and activity are more important if you want to live a long life.
Ultimately, I hope our findings will stimulate lots of discussion and lead to policy decisions that give physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness the prominent place they deserve in public health.
