TITLE: Review Finds Heart Rate Variability Feasible for Tracking Maritime Crew Strain
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic scoping review examining physiological monitoring on the high seas has established that heart rate variability serves as an informative, non-invasive indicator of occupational strain among seafarers, while highlighting substantial methodological heterogeneity across published investigations. Commercial maritime operations represent a uniquely demanding occupational environment where ship crews face sustained physical, cognitive, and environmental pressures that place continuous demands on the human autonomic nervous system. Because maritime transport accounts for approximately ninety percent of global trade, crew fatigue and autonomic dysregulation carry direct consequences for vessel safety, maritime logistics, and navigational accident prevention. Emerging geopolitical stresses, disrupted transit routes, and intensive watchkeeping regimes have further escalated onboard workloads in recent years, driving the search for objective strain parameters. Analyzing beat-to-beat variations in cardiac cycles offers a window into autonomic modulation under real working conditions, but field implementations on operational vessels encounter serious environmental and procedural hurdles that existing occupational guidelines fail to address specifically.
To rigorously assess the current state of maritime cardiac monitoring, researchers executed a comprehensive scoping review adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and the Joanna Briggs Institute synthesis framework. The investigative team systematically queried four major biomedical and scientific databases, consisting of PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Library. Their standardized search syntax combined three targeted categorical clusters encompassing maritime keywords, heart rate variability and related electrophysiological metrics, and occupational stress or fatigue outcomes. From an initial yield of six hundred and fifteen records, duplicate screening and thematic evaluation eliminated five hundred and fifty-four entries. The authors retrieved and critically appraised thirty-nine full-text reports against rigorous inclusion criteria requiring primary cardiac interval measurements collected aboard operational or semi-operational vessels rather than simulated environments. Ultimately, eleven maritime field studies met all qualifying benchmarks, providing the empirical foundation for detailed evaluation across recording hardware, signal quality controls, analytical software packages, metric choices, and investigated operational covariates.
Across the eleven qualifying investigations, observational study designs exhibited considerable diversity, comprising five cross-sectional investigations, four longitudinal studies, and two experimental crossover protocols. The temporal observation periods ranged from isolated, single-event evaluations during specific nautical cargo watches or clinical shipboard exams to multi-day continuous monitoring protocols lasting several consecutive days or weeks. Ten of the eleven reviewed studies explicitly integrated operational event profiling, detailing precise activity frames, scheduled work shifts, and shipboard duties performed during cardiac data collection. The recorded target groups encompassed merchant marine personnel, military naval crews, and polar expeditioners deployed across a wide variety of vessel classes. Specifically, investigations were conducted aboard merchant container ships, navy polar vessels, passenger vessels, and military patrol boats. The methodological quality of study designs and confounding controls was systematically appraised using the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network grading scale, establishing a clear baseline of procedural validity for this emerging research sector.
Demographic and cohort characteristics revealed marked constraints in sample architecture, with participant cohorts ranging from seven to three hundred and twenty-three individuals and presenting an overall median of twenty-four participants. Ten investigations documented participant age, but crew compositions were overwhelmingly or exclusively male, directly reflecting long-standing industry demographics while creating an acute underrepresentation of female seafarers whose cardiac autonomic profiles differ. Occupational background descriptions distinguished participants by hierarchical ranks, such as officers and ratings, or by specialized operational assignments, including deck watchkeepers, engine room personnel, technical crew, and naval personnel. Only four studies documented maritime working experience, and nationality distributions were reported in just six studies, ranging from single-national crews to highly multinational ship complements. Compounding these sampling constraints, several published articles relied on shared participant cohorts drawn from the same underlying dataset, such as three distinct studies published by Oldenburg and colleagues, which concentrated analytical weight on specific crew samples.
Hardware deployment patterns showed a clear preference for portable, non-invasive recording equipment, with chest strap systems utilized in eight of the eleven investigations. Chest strap monitors manufactured by Polar Electro Oy, including the Polar H10, S810i, V800, and RS800 models, represented the predominant instrumentation, capturing electrical cardiac activity at the chest and transmitting interval data wirelessly to wrist-mounted receivers. These systems avoid optical pulse measurements at the wrist, which capture pulse rate variability rather than true cardiac interval variations and lose comparability outside resting conditions. The Polar H10 in particular demonstrated robust agreement against clinical twelve-lead electrocardiography, boasting intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.95 during resting states and exceeding 0.93 throughout physical exercise. A single investigation by Myllylä and colleagues deployed the Firstbeat Bodyguard 2, an adhesive electrode configuration aligned with electrocardiogram lead II that fulfilled occupational health guidelines recommending full electrocardiographic recordings. Only one study employed an optical photoplethysmography sensor combined with electroencephalography via the Omnifit Mindcare device.
Scrutiny of technical recording fidelity and pre-analytical signal handling exposed prominent reporting omissions across the literature. Established occupational health recommendations from Sammito and colleagues emphasize that reliable heart rate variability extraction requires high-resolution sampling rates exceeding two hundred and fifty hertz, rigorous participant screening, resting baseline recordings, and systematic artifact correction. However, only four of the eleven maritime studies explicitly documented their hardware sampling frequency, which ranged from one thousand to five thousand hertz. Nine studies addressed circadian rhythmicity, eight reported medical pre-screening procedures such as maritime health certificates or questionnaires, seven described evaluation intervals appropriate for their chosen metrics, and six incorporated resting baseline assessments. Crucially, while six studies reported artifact correction procedures, four investigations relied on automated three percent threshold-filtering algorithms without specifying the exact correction strength selected within the software, obscuring whether individual intervals were overcorrected or undercorrected.
Analytical software utilization was anchored primarily by Kubios HRV, which was employed in six of the reviewed field investigations, while the remainder utilized proprietary manufacturer programs or custom software solutions. Studies implementing Kubios software consistently evaluated a broader array of mathematical metrics and reported a higher frequency of statistically significant findings, reflecting the utility of dedicated analytical suites for handling complex field data. Ten investigations reported traditional time-domain and frequency-domain parameters. Among time-domain indices, the standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals appeared most frequently across seven studies, capturing combined sympathetic and parasympathetic influences over short and long recording epochs. The root mean square of successive differences, reflecting parasympathetic vagal modulation, was evaluated in five studies. In the frequency domain, the ratio of low-frequency to high-frequency power was examined in seven studies, accompanied by high-frequency power in five studies and low-frequency power in four studies.
Operational and voyage-related covariates emerged as the primary external drivers of autonomic strain, appearing far more frequently than isolated environmental or personal factors. Sailing schedules, including transit stages, port calls, and overall voyage duration, were investigated as primary covariates in five studies, while work-rest organization, encompassing shift rotations and watchkeeping systems, was examined in four studies. Hierarchical rank, maritime occupation, subjective fatigue surveys, worker age, electroencephalography, and electrodermal activity were also assessed alongside cardiac metrics. Significant statistical associations were repeatedly documented between operational stressors and key cardiac metrics, particularly the low-frequency to high-frequency ratio, the root mean square of successive differences, mean RR intervals, and overall interval standard deviations. For example, longitudinal monitoring by Moraes and colleagues demonstrated a biphasic autonomic response across maritime voyages, where parasympathetic activity dropped by 47.8 percent during demanding passage stages before recovering toward journey completion, alongside elevated urinary catecholamines.
Statistical modeling approaches in three recent field investigations highlighted the predictive utility of cardiac intervals for detecting maritime fatigue and operational impairment. An ordinal logistic regression model published by Yang and colleagues identified mean RR intervals and the low-frequency to high-frequency ratio as the strongest predictors of seafarer fatigue, achieving an overall classification accuracy of 81.1 percent. In a related analytical framework, Ma and colleagues integrated multimodal cardiac parameters into a machine-learning classification model that predicted fatigue states with 85.7 percent accuracy, identifying the low-frequency to high-frequency ratio as the single highest-weighted predictive feature. Furthermore, multiple linear regression conducted by Özsever and colleagues revealed that the frequency of circadian rhythm shifts served as a significant determinant of altered low-frequency to high-frequency ratios and electrodermal responses. These computational architectures demonstrate that continuous autonomic monitoring can support algorithmic fatigue detection, although the physiological validity of spectral ratios remains subject to ongoing scientific debate.
Despite encouraging findings, the review emphasized that shipboard environments introduce substantial physical confounders, including continuous vessel heaving, mechanical vibrations, ambient acoustic noise, confined spaces, and acute sleep deficits that directly distort physiological readings or induce recording gaps. Spectral frequency-domain analyses proved especially vulnerable to procedural shortcuts: while guidelines prescribe continuous five-minute recordings for reliable spectral decomposition, multiple investigations applied spectral algorithms to one-minute or four-minute recording windows, constraining the validity of their conclusions.
