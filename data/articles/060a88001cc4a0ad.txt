TITLE: Off-topic videos can draw 10 views or 10 million, YouTube tells creators
OUTLET: PPC Land

A YouTube channel that averages a million views a video has no claim on a million views when its next upload leaves the usual subject. Rene Ritchie, YouTube's Creator Liaison, made the point in a Creator Insider video published on November 24, 2026: a 23-minute conversation with Kyle Monahan, whom the video description identifies as YouTube's head of content strategy. The pair took on a belief the video calls a myth: that variety content does not work on the platform.
The answer on the algorithm was blunt. "There's no sort of like channelwide score in the algorithm," Ritchie said. "Like if you're getting a million views per video with your standard topic and you do an off-topic video like there's nothing that says you're going to be getting a million views for that. you might get 10 views or you might get 10 million views."
In Short
YouTube's Creator Liaison said in a video published on November 24, 2026 that the platform's algorithm keeps no channel-wide score, so a creator's usual view count does not follow them to a video on a new topic. That matters to creators who want to branch out and to brands that pick creators by their track record, since an off-topic upload might draw 10 views or 10 million. What changes is the framing: each new subject means building a new audience, and the two YouTube staff said a common thread, such as a voice, a format or a mission, is what lets a varied channel hold together.
A myth put on trial
The video opens with one speaker putting a claim to the other: "You cannot do variety content on YouTube." The reply was that the claim is "cap", slang for a lie. The belief, as set out in the first minute, holds that top-end growth in subscribers and views requires "a repeatable format so that people who loved your last video will also love" the next one, and that any deviation brings a penalty from the algorithm.
The verdict over the following 23 minutes was that "it's a lot more nuanced than that." Neither speaker argued that a channel can change subject at no cost. Both argued that the cost depends on what viewers came for in the first place.
The video sits on Creator Insider, the YouTube channel through which the company's staff talk to creators, which had 939,000 subscribers at the time. By the evening of November 25 it had 4,884 views, 238 likes and 71 comments. Its description lists 10 chapters.
No channel-wide score
The core of the video arrives in its seventh chapter. Monahan named "the question behind the question": whether a creator who changes direction will be "punished by the algorithm" or will "alienate my audience." Monahan's answer started from a familiar Creator Insider point. "As we've said many times in many videos audience or the the algorithm is meant to find the best videos for the audience," Monahan said. "So by exploring you may find entire new chasms of content that make sense for a new audience who are getting exposed to you."
Ritchie said YouTube hears both sides of the same complaint. Creators whose off-topic videos fail and creators whose off-topic videos succeed both write in, and both blame the algorithm.
Ritchie's explanation for both was the absence of any channel-level baseline. "It really is up to like what like the audience gets to decide that," Ritchie said, before describing what a change of subject means for the creator making it: "if you are changing topics you are in some sense rebuilding an audience every time and that audience may have like bigger potential smaller potential you might have better execution worse execution and there might be better comp like more competition or less competition in each of those topics."
Put plainly, YouTube's recommender system, as Ritchie described it, does not grade a new video against the channel's history. The viewers who see it settle the result, and how far it travels depends on how many people want that subject, how well the video is made and how many other videos compete for the same viewers.
How an off-topic upload is handled, according to the video
Ritchie's remarks across chapters 7 and 8 describe the process in five parts. The video presents them as conversation rather than as documentation, and no Help Center page is cited.
- No baseline from the channel. A channel's typical views on its standard topic set no expectation for a video on another topic. "You might get 10 views or you might get 10 million views."
- The audience decides. The outcome "really is up to" the viewers who are shown the video.
- Viewing patterns are tracked by video, not only by channel. According to Ritchie, "the algorithm does understand when people watch like certain videos but not others," and it recommends on that basis.
- A one-off hit can send viewers to the wrong videos. Ritchie's example was a viewer who loved a creator's single cheerleading video. With no further cheerleading videos available, the system recommends the creator's other uploads, "and they don't like it."
- Each topic has its own ceiling. The size of the potential audience, the quality of execution and the level of competition differ from one subject to the next.
Anchor points, from vloggers to speedrunners
If there is no channel score, what holds a varied channel together? Both speakers used the same phrase: an anchor point. For Ritchie, a channel that wants growth needs "an anchor point where the channel makes sense to an audience," so that a viewer who has seen a few videos has "a general sense of like what makes this a distinct voice." Ritchie separated that goal from creators who are not chasing "peak top right chart growth of views and subs." An artist who wants to upload anything, by that account, faces no such question.
The examples covered a wide range.
Monahan described Speed's output as "every spectrum of content you could post," held together by tone. "It's still unified under a like basically this is my older brother or my older brother's best friend telling me about this thing," Monahan said.
Monahan also brought in the film director David Lynch, whose work moved between noir soap opera and horror while remaining recognisably Lynch's own. "It was never mainstream successful," Monahan noted. Creators who use YouTube purely as an outlet for an artistic vision can work that way. Those who want growth face a different calculation, and Monahan did not treat it as a lesser one: "that's not a wrong approach to content right that is how you build a business that's how you get brand advertisers."
The knitting problem
Chapter 4 turned on expectations. Ritchie contrasted the biggest vloggers with the biggest challenge-video creator. A viewer who clicks on the challenge creator's face expects a challenge. If one week the creator is knitting and the next week cleaning a fish, the viewer may skip the third video entirely. "The expectation for like the challenge video you wanted is not getting realized and that's when the disconnect happens," Ritchie said.
Viewers have explicit tools to act on that disconnect. A TeamYouTube help forum FAQ, covered in March 2026, listed "Not interested" and "Don't recommend channel" among the controls viewers can use on their recommendations.
Monahan pointed to a related change on the platform: "the growth of like these shows right that creators are building around and there is like a branding to it." Monahan named Challenge Accepted as "the chief example of trying a variety of things but under one unifying framework." The suggestion for creators was to "interrogate what is the thing that is behind the thing that like the audience is connecting with." A challenge channel's viewers might be after inspiration; a speedrunning audience might be after thrill and technical skill. "I would not be surprised if like speedrunning channels all of a sudden got into like hyper technical metal guitar playing," Monahan said.
Storytellers and formula-builders
Chapter 6 split creators into two groups. Some are gifted storytellers. Ritchie described being "so emotionally bought into that arc" of a creator trying to become a cheerleader, with no interest in cheerleading itself, "from the sheer value of the storytelling." Others work from a structure they know holds attention, and as long as they followed it, "they could put any sort of subcategory they wanted into that formula."
Monahan was more interested in the crossover: putting the jazz player into a regimented system, or removing the rules from someone who relies on them, to see what comes out.
The audience decides, the creator chooses
The eighth chapter returned to what creators do with a surprise result. "The audience gets to decide what they want and how much they want of it, but you get to decide whether you care about that or not," Ritchie said. A creator whose off-topic video fails can drop the idea or make more such videos to find that audience. A creator whose off-topic video succeeds can follow it or ignore it. "All of that choice stay like stays with you as the creator."
Monahan compared the choice to actors. Jason Statham, in Monahan's account, has embraced a formula and is content with it. "There's nothing wrong that he's taking a formulaic approach because it's authentic to him and he loves it and it works." Daniel Craig, by contrast, "was tired of being James Bond and was in one of the most formulaic series possible and wanted to branch out into doing things like Knives Out."
One brand, two mountains
Ritchie gave the trade-off its sharpest form when speaking as a technology creator. Covering a single brand, Ritchie said, brings a "higher likelihood that more people will watch more of the videos because people have high degree of brand affinity." Covering several brands splits the audience. "It's like climbing two different mountains," Ritchie said. "You have to understand that every choice you make have trade-offs."
Monahan said running several strands of content is possible but hard: "you will come to a point where you either need to choose one or need to expand to be able to run multiple businesses." Monahan pointed to creators who "have stood up media companies that then have a set of shows or a set of formats that have new voices attached to them."
Inspiration without copying
Monahan raised a strategy described in the video as "hotly recommended": websites that aggregate top-performing videos in a category for creators to replicate in their own voice. Ritchie pointed instead to YouTube Studio's audience tab, where creators can see "other videos that your audience is watching." It is the same area of Studio where YouTube added "casual" and "regular" viewer metrics in July 2025. YouTube has since built a comparable discovery feature of its own: the Research tab, launched on November 13, 2026, scores videos with an outlier figure measured against a channel's earlier uploads in the same format.
On copying, Ritchie said "it's not helpful to just try to duplicate someone else's voice," adding: "Like people can always tell." Formats are another matter. Ritchie cited the "$1, $10, $1,000, $10,000" comparison video, of which "there's a version of that video that crosses almost every demographic." Paraphrasing the line about great artists stealing, Ritchie put it as "bad artists just copy good artists copy forward."
Monahan drew the line with reference to YouTube's own enforcement: "there's a fine line between yeah fully ripping something off and obviously we have like content ID systems and systems in place for that." Ritchie described a test applied to every new idea for the channel: "could this video exist on other channels? Could it exist on a lot of other channels? And if it could, there is nothing that is building affinity with me and my channel."
