TITLE: Free will is not an illusion but a control achievement of the brain, and it can be tested in machines
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Is free will real, or is it an illusion created by our brains? For neuroscientist Paul Verschure, Distinguished University Professor at Miguel Hernández University of Elche (UMH), in Spain, free will is real—and measurable. In two papers published in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, he argues that free will is a product of evolution and introduces the concept of Responsible Autonomy: six control capacities that allow an agent, human or otherwise, to govern its own behavior. These capacities form the basis of ESRA, a proposed evaluation framework that moves beyond the Turing test—which asks whether a machine can pass for a human—to a more demanding question: does an AI system actually possess the mechanisms required to act autonomously?
Much of contemporary neuroscience and philosophy regards free will as an illusion, while law and ethics treat people as agents who can be held accountable for their actions. Prof. Verschure calls this mismatch the “agency schism.” “Lawmakers are trying to regulate a phantom in machines and an illusion in humans,” he says. “Neither side can define what an agent is.”
His proposal seeks to turn a philosophical debate stretching back more than two thousand years into a testable scientific problem. At the same time, it addresses an increasingly urgent question: how can we tell whether an artificial intelligence system is acting as an autonomous agent or remains a tool whose goals and rules are set by others? As AI systems become capable of operating with less human supervision, this approach could provide researchers, technology developers, and regulators with concrete criteria for assessing their autonomy.
What makes a choice our own?
Drawing on philosophy, neuroscience and other disciplines that have sought to explain human agency, Prof. Verschure identifies six capacities that, together, distinguish an autonomous action from a reflex or an imposed response. To understand them, the UMH professor suggests imagining someone who finds a lost wallet while rushing to work.
The first capacity, Active Decoupling, allows that person to look beyond the immediate situation and imagine what cannot be seen, for example, the wallet's owner discovering the loss and worrying about what might be inside. Endogenous Drive means that a person's behavior is determined not only by external stimuli but also by their own needs, motivations, and goals. In this case, they might consider whether returning the wallet will make them late for work.
Leeway allows them to generate different courses of action, including alternatives they have never considered before. They could search for the owner, hand the wallet in to the police, or post a notice. Normative Self-Governance enables them to infer what other people expect and restrain behavior that would violate social norms. Finding a wallet does not, after all, give someone the right to keep its contents.
Diachronic Integrity connects the present decision to the commitments and values a person maintains over time. Someone who has always valued honesty is unlikely to decide, on this occasion, to keep the money and throw the wallet away. Finally, Experiential Ownership allows people to experience their thoughts, decisions, and actions as their own. In simple terms, they recognize themselves as the person making the choice.
“Freedom is not a property a system has or lacks,” Verschure explains. Each of these capacities can be present to varying degrees, and each can fail in different ways. Since none of these capacities work like a switch that is either on or off, Prof. Verschure therefore understands Responsible Autonomy as a gradual control capacity: an action is more or less free depending on the extent to which it has been generated, evaluated and embraced by the agent's own control mechanisms.
Prof. Verschure traces these capacities back to evolution. As animals learned to navigate relationships with predators and prey, and later within increasingly complex social groups, they faced a world in which others' intentions could not be directly observed. According to the neuroscientist, the brain evolved these capacities to anticipate what others might do and decide how to respond.
This changes the terms of a familiar debate about our free will. Rather than asking whether our decisions are completely independent of the causes that shape us, the framework asks how those causes are organized within a system capable of imagining alternatives, weighing consequences, and committing to a course of action. In this account, freedom does not require escaping causality: we still do things for a reason. What matters is how those causes are organized to produce our choices.
From a theory of the brain to a test for AI
In a second paper, Prof. Verschure brings these six capacities together in an architecture called Distributed Adaptive Control of volition and agency (DACva). It extends a theory of mind and brain that Prof. Verschure has developed over more than three decades. The underlying DAC theory already forms the basis of rehabilitation technologies that have been used to treat thousands of stroke patients. A model of how the brain generates decisions and actions—and commits to them—can help identify what is lost after brain injury and how those capacities might be recovered. From this perspective, pathology can be understood as a failure within an embodied, socially situated control architecture, rather than simply as a “broken brain.”
DACva organizes control into five levels, ranging from bodily regulation to the representation of other people and the self. The underlying DAC theory has been tested in over 370 studies spanning computer models, animal and human experiments, robots, and clinical and educational applications.
At the center of the system is a process that generates possible courses of action, evaluates them, and ultimately commits to one. The six capacities contribute at different stages of this process. The model expresses these functions mathematically, relates them to possible brain mechanisms, and proposes experiments to test them. The aim is to turn concepts such as freedom and volition into scientific hypotheses that can be supported or refuted by evidence.
From this architecture comes ESRA (Evaluation Suite for Responsible Autonomy), a proposed set of tests designed to assess the same six capacities in artificial systems. Rather than asking whether a machine can convincingly imitate a human, as in the famous Turing test, ESRA looks at what lies behind its behavior: whether it can generate its own alternatives, maintain commitments, infer social norms or distinguish internally generated goals from instructions imposed from outside. The question is no longer whether a machine can appear to be an autonomous agent, but whether it actually has the capacities to be one. In order to do so, ESRA deliberately perturbs the system's mechanisms, starting from the assumption that it possesses neither autonomy nor responsibility, and requiring each capacity to be demonstrated through tests of its underlying mechanisms.
According to Prof. Verschure, no current AI system has yet achieved Responsible Autonomy. He has applied the proposed framework to two AI-related security incidents that occurred in 2026. “The systems fell short on at least three of the six capacities, pointing to failures by their developers rather than to an AI with a will of its own,” the neuroscientist concludes. In other words, AI systems' goals and norms are still supplied externally and can be modified or overridden by the people who design or operate them.
Why the difference between a choice and a command matters
In 1943, Noor Inayat Khan was working as a British wireless operator for the resistance in Nazi-occupied France. When the network around her was compromised, she was offered the chance to leave. She chose to remain, continuing to transmit despite the danger. She was eventually captured and, in 1944, executed at Dachau concentration camp. According to a witness, her final word was liberté—freedom.
Prof. Verschure invokes her story to illustrate what is at stake in the debate over free will. Inayat Khan’s decision was not a reflex nor a response forced by her surroundings. It involved imagining alternatives, anticipating consequences, remaining faithful to commitments, and acting according to values she recognized as her own, even under mortal threat.
These are precisely the kinds of capacities that Responsible Autonomy seeks to explain. They also help us understand why we regard some actions as deserving of respect and others as deserving of blame. The question is not merely whether an action occurred, but how it came about and who—or what—controlled it.
For the neuroscientist, this distinction has direct consequences for AI regulation. If a machine lacks the capacities required to govern its own behavior, attributing responsibility to it could conceal the responsibility of the people and organizations that develop, deploy, or use it.
“It is a problem analogous to assigning responsibility for war crimes: we need to determine how much responsibility lies with the person carrying out an action and how much with the chain of command. As a society, we need methods that allow us to know whether an AI is merely following instructions or acting autonomously,” Prof. Verschure explains.
The issue becomes particularly important as systems capable of intervening in the world with less human supervision emerge, from software agents to technologies used in military settings. A machine's apparent independence does not necessarily mean it can be held accountable for its actions. Establishing that distinction requires examining its internal control mechanisms rather than relying on the sophistication of its behavior.
For Prof. Verschure, artificial intelligence is forcing an ancient philosophical debate to become a practical question of science, engineering, law, and governance. “Free will is not a metaphysical ornament, but a biological reality and, by implication, a technological possibility,” the expert argues.
An interdisciplinary investigation of the self
