TITLE: Rockstar Kept One Of GTA 6’s Biggest Early Spoilers Under Wraps For Years
OUTLET: Kotaku

Inevitably, my day job of keeping up with the biggest video game release of the past decade is going to put me in spoiler territory, but I’m pretty pleased that I was able to avoid one of Grand Theft Auto 6’s big secrets until I started playing the game when it went live this morning. Spoilers ahead if you haven’t been able to start the game yet, but this is something you learn in the opening hour, so we’re not talking endgame reveals here. This fact has now made it nearly impossible for me to even consider a hyperviolent run, when I already felt like these games don’t really do the legwork needed to make that playstyle feel “correct” in the grand scheme of the satirical soap-opera stories they’re telling.
Jason and Lucia, the criminal couple at the center of GTA 6, have a son. His name is Tomás and he’s just a baby at the beginning of the game. Lucia, desperate for cash, left him at a diner—she planned to come right back!— during the stickup that landed her in prison, which is where we find her when the game proper begins. GTA6 goes to some lengths to obfuscate Tomás’ existence in these opening segments with strategic camera angles that ensure you never see him in his carrier, and frantic dialogue between Lucia and a server that gets interrupted just before either of them can reveal that the little man is there. So when you inevitably get caught after your attempted heist, you’re not sure why Lucia is begging the cops to let her go back to the restaurant.
Then, it’s revealed, a group of staff and patrons are all gathered around the crying baby whose mama left him and is now in the back of a cop car. This whole mess is explained in a flashback as Lucia tells a social worker about why she was arrested, and why she’d be a good candidate for a monitored release program as the prison seeks to open up a bed or two to account for an overflow of incoming inmates. Though Lucia is told that getting out with an ankle monitor on her leg is a separate matter from her being able to see her son again, she swears that this arrest has been a wake-up call for her and Jason, and that they’re ready to live an honest life.
Well, this is Grand Theft Auto, so an “honest life” is all relative, I suppose. But the knowledge that Jason and Lucia could be responsible for someone else’s livelihood does recontextualize things. Like most folks, I’m still very early in the game, but GTA 6 has a morality system that tracks how you carry out your criminal activities. Thus far in my game, Lucia is still incarcerated and I’m playing as Jason, doing odd jobs until she gets out, but in these early missions, I’ve been making a point to do things as quickly and quietly as I can.
At first, this decision wasn’t even grounded in any narrative or roleplaying; it’s how I tend to play action games that have a stealth option. Treating thugs and cops like clockwork puzzles to be solved comes more naturally to me than going in guns blazing. Despite mayhem and carnage being one of the most famous features of Grand Theft Auto, I’ve never really felt that the framework of these games supports you opening fire on a group of civilians. It feels like a glitch in the Matrix, though I understand that pushing those boundaries and having a virtual excuse to behave badly is part of the cathartic appeal for some folks.Personally, however, I don’t usually feel the compulsion to wreak havoc on passersby, so I don’t spend time in games like Grand Theft Auto or Cyberpunk ruining strangers’ days.
But now, knowing that reuniting a family is a major piece of this puzzle, that disconnect feels more prominent than ever. In some ways, I can see the argument that Jason and Lucia could be willing to go to great lengths to reunite with their son and give him a comfortable life; I’m just not sure I see explosions and constant carnage on the path to that future now. Hey, maybe something will happen later in the game that will change my mind, but for now, I’m about to try and keep these two’s hands (relatively) clean.
