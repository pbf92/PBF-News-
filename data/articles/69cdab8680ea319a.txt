TITLE: Amazon bans pork ads in 10 countries from Morocco to Oman
OUTLET: PPC Land

Amazon Ads on Sunday, November 15, 2026 updated a Support Center policy page that sets out what advertisers may show when their products involve animals, from pork in the Middle East and North Africa to rodent poison on Prime Video. The page, titled "Animal related products and services," prohibits pork content in ads across ten countries Amazon groups under the label MENAT: Bahrain, Egypt, Jordan, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Morocco, Oman, Qatar, Turkey and the United Arab Emirates.
The rules reach well beyond pork. Cow products, which the page names as leather and beef, are prohibited in India. Veterinary products fall under three different regimes in Australia, Brazil and Japan. In the United States, ads for rodenticide are allowed on sponsored ads and Streaming TV, but advertisers buying Prime Video Ads must exclude content rated G or PG - and content that carries no rating at all. Above the country rules sits one global line: Amazon's content policies "prohibit images of animals killing or consuming other animals due to their graphic nature."
The page has no changelog, effective date or transition period, so it does not say what was revised on November 15. Amazon's older sponsored ads policy, which is still published and also undated, offers a point of comparison. On pig imagery in Egypt and Saudi Arabia, the two documents now say different things.
In Short
Amazon has published country-by-country rules for ads that involve animals: pork content is banned in ten Middle East, North Africa and Turkey markets, cow leather and beef are banned in India, and veterinary products are restricted in Australia, Brazil and Japan. Food, fashion, pet health and pest control brands are the most directly affected, along with any advertiser whose creative shows animals hunting or eating other animals, which the page bans everywhere. US rodent poison ads can run on Amazon's sponsored ads, streaming TV and Prime Video, but Prime Video buys must exclude G, PG and unrated content, and Amazon's older rules for Egypt and Saudi Arabia still say pig images are not permitted at all.
One global rule, six regional sections
The page opens with a one-line summary: "Learn how to advertise animal-related products and services while meeting regional content and placement requirements." Its general section then sets the baseline. "Ads must follow applicable laws and comply with our content policies, which prohibit images of animals killing or consuming other animals due to their graphic nature," according to Amazon. A short sentence follows: "Incremental regional restrictions apply."
The global ban concerns imagery, not products. It does not stop a pet food maker or a pest control company from advertising. It stops creative that shows one animal killing or eating another. The page gives no examples of what crosses the line.
The regional layer comes in six collapsible sections: Australia, Brazil, India, Japan, the ten-country MENAT group and the United States. By a PPC Land count, 15 countries carry specific rules.
The rules by market
Only two of the six sections name the ad products they govern. Australia draws a line between Sponsored Products and Brand Stores, and the US section names sponsored ads, Streaming TV and Prime Video Ads. The Brazil, India, Japan and MENAT sections do not say whether they apply to sponsored ads alone, to Amazon's video inventory, to display campaigns, or to everything Amazon sells.
Pork, pig imagery and the MENAT ten
The MENAT section packs three rules into two sentences. "Pig imagery: only neutral, non-offensive depictions are allowed; pork content is prohibited. Non-edible pig-related items are allowed with placement restrictions," according to the page.
The first rule permits pictures of pigs, but only of a certain kind; neither "neutral" nor "non-offensive" is defined. The second is a flat prohibition on pork content. The third allows items connected to pigs that are not food, subject to "placement restrictions" the page does not list. What counts as a non-edible pig-related item is not explained either.
Amazon's older sponsored ads guidelines and acceptance policies cover part of the same ground. That document prohibits products derived from pork in Egypt, Saudi Arabia and the UAE, and it states that images of swine are not permitted in Egypt and Saudi Arabia. Set side by side, the Support Center page widens the pork ban from three countries to ten, adding Bahrain, Jordan, Kuwait, Morocco, Oman, Qatar and Turkey. Six of those seven do not appear anywhere in the older policy; Turkey appears only in an unrelated rule.
On imagery, the direction runs the other way. For Egypt and Saudi Arabia, the older text rules out swine images altogether, while the newer page allows neutral, non-offensive depictions across all ten countries. A neutral illustration of a pig would pass under one Amazon document and fail under the other. Which one decides? The Support Center page does not say that it replaces the sponsored ads policy, and the older policy remains online without a note pointing elsewhere.
Two more older rules have no counterpart on the new page. The sponsored ads policy prohibits dog food and supplements in Saudi Arabia, and it prohibits pesticide and fertilizer products in Egypt. Neither appears in the MENAT section.
India: leather and beef
"Cow products (leather and beef) are prohibited," the India section states. It is the shortest section on the page and among the widest in commercial reach, because leather is a material rather than a product type. The rule reaches any ad for a product made from cowhide, whether that is footwear, a handbag, a belt or a sofa.
The parenthesis reads as a definition rather than a set of examples. Dairy, which a literal reading of "cow products" would include, is not mentioned. Leather from other animals is not addressed, and the page does not describe how Amazon tells cowhide from other hides in a product listing or a creative.
The older sponsored ads policy has no India rule on animal products, although India appears in it under other categories. Against that baseline, the restriction is an addition.
Veterinary products: three countries, three regimes
Australia's rule is the most specific. "Veterinary and medical devices permitted on Sponsored Products only; prohibited on Brand Stores," according to Amazon. The word "only" implies that other formats, such as Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Display, are closed to these products in Australia, though the page does not name them. Brand Stores, the multi-page brand storefronts on Amazon, are named and excluded. They have their own moderation regime: two days before this update, Amazon revised separate guidance under which Brand Stores face rejection over "Prime Day" wording, even in images.
Brazil takes the opposite approach. "Veterinary and medical devices are prohibited; vitamins and supplements are permitted," the page states. The rule follows the line of the older sponsored ads policy, which prohibits over-the-counter medication products and devices in Brazil, and adds an explicit veterinary reference. Whether the permitted vitamins and supplements are those meant for animals, for people or both is left open.
Japan's section is a single classification: "Veterinary products treated as medical devices." The page does not restate what medical device status means in practice. The older sponsored ads policy does: in Japan, it permits ads for medical devices in Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands, and it requires Sponsored Brands videos for over-the-counter drugs and medical devices to match the video on the product detail page. Read together, veterinary products in Japan would follow those terms, although neither document makes the link directly. The Support Center also lists a separate "Healthcare products and services" page among the related topics.
One older global rule is missing from the new page: the sponsored ads policy requires drugs and supplements intended for pets to be clearly identified.
How the rodenticide rule works
The US rule is the most detailed on the page, and it appears twice, once in the general section and again under the United States heading. "Ads for rodenticide are allowed on sponsored ads and Streaming TV (US). On Prime Video Ads (PVA), advertisers must exclude PG-and-below content (i.e., content rated G, Unrated, and PG). Permitted in US only, off homepages," according to Amazon. The US section adds: "Non-US Rodenticides require local legal review; generally restricted."
According to the page, the rule has four layers:
- Eligible inventory. Rodenticide ads are allowed on sponsored ads and on Streaming TV in the United States.
- Content exclusion on Prime Video. On Prime Video Ads, the advertiser must exclude "PG-and-below content," which the page defines as content rated G, Unrated and PG.
- Geography and placement. The permission applies in the US only, and the ads must run "off homepages."
- Other countries. Rodenticides outside the US "require local legal review" and are "generally restricted."
Several details stand out. The page groups unrated content with G and PG, so a title without a rating is treated like a family title for this purpose. The exclusion stops at PG; by the page's own definition, titles rated PG-13 or higher fall outside it. G and PG are film-style labels, and the page does not mention the TV Parental Guidelines labels, such as TV-G and TV-PG, used for US television series. Nor does it define "homepages" - the Prime Video home screen, Amazon's retail homepage, or both.
The phrase "Non-US Rodenticides" has two possible readings: rodenticide advertising outside the US, or rodenticide products made outside the US. Placed next to "Permitted in US only," the first reading looks intended, but the page does not settle it.
Prime Video began carrying ads in the United States in January 2024, when Amazon launched Prime Video Ads, with the UK, Canada, Germany and Austria following in February. Advertisers have had some visibility into the content their ads run against since then: Amazon offered genre and rating reports from January 2024 and added show title reports for Prime Video and Freevee on August 6, 2024. When Amazon DSP added content exclusion categories on December 18, 2025, they covered Twitch and third-party web and mobile inventory in 43 markets, with no mention of Prime Video. The animal page does not say which control in Amazon's buying tools applies a G, PG and Unrated exclusion.
The older sponsored ads policy contains no rodenticide rule.

Amazon's display policy offers less still. Its creative acceptance policy, also undated, lists negative depictions of animals, such as actual or implied abuse or mistreatment, among unacceptable ad content. It has no country rules on animal products, and it names no country where pork, leather or veterinary products are restricted.
A policy library rebuilt in public
The animal page follows two other Amazon Ads Support Center policy revisions in the preceding three days. On November 12, Amazon forced prediction markets through gambling ad verification in a rewritten Gambling and Games page with sections for 22 countries. On November 13, it revised the Brand Store guidance for peak events. The animal page's sidebar lists 16 related policy topics, from Alcohol and Charities to News and journalism, Baby formula and feeding accessories, and Event days.
The structures differ. The gambling page requires advertiser verification and a category declaration, so that publishers and exchanges filtering sensitive categories receive an accurate signal.
