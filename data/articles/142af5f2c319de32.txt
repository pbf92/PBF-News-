TITLE: UK app makers get four months to collect from Google's £260m settlement
OUTLET: PPC Land

Claims opened on November 24 in the £260 million settlement that ended a UK legal case against Google over its Play Store commissions, giving app developers until March 24, 2027 to register for a share of the money. According to a press release issued in London on behalf of Professor Barry Rodger, the law academic who brought the case for developers, more than 10,000 UK businesses are eligible, and every eligible developer is guaranteed at least £200.
In Short
Google agreed to pay £260 million to end a UK case that said its Play Store commissions of up to 30% were too high, and since November 24 UK developers who sold apps, subscriptions or in-app items on the store can claim their share. More than 10,000 businesses are eligible, the smallest awards start at £200, and the largest developers could receive millions of pounds. Payment requires a claim on the official website by March 24, 2027, after which the tribunal reviews the split before any money goes out.
What opened on November 24
According to the press release, dated November 24, the claims period "opens today for the £260 million settlement secured by Professor Barry Rodger against Google." The release calls the sum "the largest pay-out for members of a collective action ever approved by the Competition Appeal Tribunal." That description comes from the claimant's side, not from the tribunal.
Eligible businesses, according to the release, are those "that sold apps, games, subscriptions or in-app content through the Google Play Store since August 2018." The money is claimed through a single website, www.hub.googleplaystoredeveloperclaim.com. Its home page describes it as "the official claims portal for the £260 million settlement secured by Professor Barry Rodger with Google, on behalf of UK app developers and approved by the Competition Appeal Tribunal."
That page is brief. It states: "Claiming is quick, free and we will never ask you for any payments. The claim period closes on 24 March 2027." The site was designed by Reach LawTech, according to the portal.
The opening date landed inside the window set out at approval. When the tribunal signed off on the deal, PPC Land reported on September 20 that preparing the claims process was expected to take six to ten weeks. November 24 was 69 days after the September 16 approval, a PPC Land calculation - just under ten weeks.
Who can claim
The portal sets out three conditions. Quoted word for word, a developer can claim if:
- "You are a UK-based app developer, whether a business or an individual."
- "You sold apps, in-app purchases or subscriptions through the Google Play Store on which you paid commission to Google."
- "You made at least one of these sales between 22 August 2018 and 31 July 2026."
The notes to editors at the end of the press release phrase the rule differently and add a fourth condition. They state: "UK-domiciled third-party app developers that made one or more relevant sales through the Play Store, or within their apps to Android users, between 22 August 2018 and 31 July 2026, and that did not opt out of the claim, are eligible."
Three differences stand out. The portal says individuals can claim as well as businesses, while the press release speaks only of businesses. The release covers sales "within their apps to Android users", wording the portal does not repeat. And only the release mentions the opt-out, which means developers who formally left the case earlier are excluded.
Free apps can qualify. According to the press release, "A business can be eligible even if its app was free to download, as long as it sold subscriptions or in-app content and did not opt out of the claim." That brings in the freemium model behind much of the games and subscription market, where the download costs nothing and the money comes from purchases made inside the app.
The release lists the kinds of businesses in the group: "from independent games studios and fitness apps to educational software companies and news publishers."
How much each developer gets
The award depends on how much Google took from each developer. According to the portal, "The value of the awards is based on the commission you paid on your Play Store sales. Awards start from a minimum of £200."
The press release goes further. "Payments are calculated from Google's records of each developer's Play Store sales and the commissions it paid between 22 August 2018 and 31 July 2026," it states. Developers whose sales appear in those records "will be shown their provisional award when they claim." A provisional award is the figure the portal shows at the time of the claim, before the tribunal's final review.
The £200 floor exists to bring small developers in. According to the notes to editors: "To encourage eligible businesses to claim, the Tribunal approved a guaranteed minimum payment of £200 for those whose apps generated £7,500 or less through the Play Store during the relevant period, even where their proportionate share would have been lower." At the other end of the scale, compensation rises "to millions of pounds for the largest developers," according to the release.
The wording on the £200 has shifted since approval. PPC Land's September 20 report, based on the settlement papers, described developers with estimated revenue of £7,500 or less as able to choose a fixed £200 payment. The press release now calls it "a guaranteed minimum payment."
Not all of the £260 million goes to developers. PPC Land reported on September 2 that the deal divides into £160 million for eligible developers and £100 million for the litigation funder, insurers and legal team. The September 20 report added that the £260 million total includes interest and all the parties' fees, costs and expenses. Neither the press release nor the portal page mentions this split.
How a claim works
According to the press release and the claims portal, the process has five stages.
- Invitation. An invitation to claim is sent by email "to the address linked to their Google Play developer account," according to the release.
- Eligibility check. Developers who did not receive an invitation "can still check their eligibility through the official claims portal."
- Provisional award. When a developer claims, the portal shows a provisional award to those whose sales appear in Google's records. The figure is calculated from Google's own records of Play Store sales and commissions between August 22, 2018 and July 31, 2026.
- Submission. Claims are made on the portal by March 24, 2027. The release says checking and claiming takes "a few minutes," and the portal says claiming is free and that it "will never ask you for any payments."
- Tribunal review. After the window closes, "the Tribunal will review the distribution of the settlement before payments are made," according to the notes to editors.
Neither document gives a payment date. PPC Land's September 20 report, drawn from the settlement papers filed before approval, cited an estimate of 30 to 60 days to review claims, with first payments four to six weeks after that. Those were planning estimates, not fixed dates.
"It isn't spam"
The design of the case explains much of the message around the opening. Rodger's claim was brought as an opt-out collective redress action: one person sues on behalf of a whole group, and everyone who fits the description is included unless they actively leave. Members do not have to sign up for the case to go ahead. They do have to come forward to be paid.
That creates a practical problem. According to the press release, "Many eligible developers will be hearing about the claim for the first time." It adds: "Class actions are still relatively new in the UK, so some may be surprised to learn that they are entitled to compensation at no cost."
Rodger spoke to that directly. "If an email about a Google settlement has landed in your inbox, it isn't spam," he said in the release. "After more than two years of work, compensation is now ready for the UK businesses that sold apps and digital content on the Google Play Store, from one-person studios working at their kitchen tables to established games companies."
The people who planned the payout did not expect everyone to answer. PPC Land's September 20 report cited a planned take-up of 50 to 80 percent by value but only 8 to 15 percent by number of developers. Put simply, the large developers were expected to claim most of the money, while most small developers were expected not to claim at all. The guaranteed £200 is aimed at that gap.
Damien Geradin, founding partner of Geradin Partners, the law firm acting for Rodger, presented the opening as a test for the whole UK system. "For a decade, the big question about the UK's class action regime has been whether the money ever reaches the people it is meant for," he said. "Today, thousands of UK businesses have the opportunity to claim their share of this settlement, free of charge." He added: "The focus now is on ensuring eligible businesses are aware of the compensation available to them."
According to the release, the regime "was introduced in 2015 so that consumers and businesses who could not realistically go to court alone could still obtain redress."
How many developers are in the group?
The press release says more than 10,000 UK businesses are eligible. That is far more than the number in earlier tribunal material. PPC Land's September 2 report put the group at about 2,200 UK-based third-party developers, with 70 to 77 percent of them estimated to have lost less than £10,000 each. The same report noted that the period was extended to cover sales up to July 31, 2026, bringing in a newer group of developers. Neither the press release nor the portal explains the gap between the two figures.
The case behind the money
The dispute was about the cut Google takes when people pay for apps and digital content on Play. According to the press release, "The claim alleged that the commissions of up to 30% charged on Play Store transactions were excessive and in breach of competition law."
Google admitted nothing. The release states that "The settlement was entered into without any admission of liability by Google," and that the tribunal approved it on September 16, 2026, "finding its terms just and reasonable." PPC Land's September 20 coverage recorded that the settlement papers note Google's belief that it had strong defences, and that Google maintains it is not dominant in any properly defined market.
The case number is 1673/7/7/24. Rodger filed the claim on August 23, 2024, and the tribunal allowed it to proceed as a collective action on May 23, 2025, according to PPC Land's September 2 report. A settlement hearing followed on September 15, 2026. The deal arrived late: the tribunal approved it twelve days before a trial was due to open on September 28, 2026.
Rodger has been an academic at Strathclyde University Law School since 1993, where he teaches and researches EU and UK competition law, according to the release.
The claims process covers developers only. According to the release, "This claim is separate from the collective claim brought by Liz Coll on behalf of UK consumers, which is currently going through trial." PPC Land's September 20 report said the Coll case covers about 20 million UK consumers and was due to go to trial on October 5, 2026. People who bought apps on Play are not part of this claims process.
Play Store fees have already moved
The commission at the centre of the case is no longer what it was. Google split its Play service fee from its billing fee on June 24, 2026. From June 30, existing installs paid 25% on one-off purchases and new installs 20%, recurring subscription payments were charged a flat 10%, and a separate 5% fee applied to Google Play Billing in the US, UK and European Economic Area. Earlier, Google introduced user choice billing for UK developers on March 29, 2025, which cut the service fee by 4% when a buyer chose a different payment system.
