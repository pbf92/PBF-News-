TITLE: Drones offer a new view of wildlife health. But what are they missing?
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Woods Hole, Mass. (Nov. 26, 2026) — Drones are transforming how scientists study wildlife, allowing researchers to photograph and measure animals in their natural environments, monitor larger populations, and collect observations with less disturbance. While a photograph can reveal changes in an animal's size and shape, it cannot confidently tell researchers what those changes mean for its health.
A new scientific review by researchers at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI), Oregon State University, the University of Maryland Center for Environmental Science, and collaborating institutions examines how scientists can combine drone imagery and other emerging technologies with established physiological measurements to get a more complete picture of wildlife health.
Published in Science, the review, “Integrating established and emerging tools to assess wildlife condition from the ground up,” offers a framework for understanding what remote technologies can reveal about an animal's condition and potential limitations.
One important distinction is whether an animal is gaining or losing fat, muscle, or both. These tissues serve different biological purposes and contain different amounts of stored energy. For example, an animal that appears to be gaining weight may be building muscle rather than accumulating fat.
Without understanding these differences, researchers could misinterpret an animal's nutritional health and its ability to withstand environmental stress.
Drawing on more than a decade of research measuring body composition and energy reserves in Weddell seals, the review demonstrates why physiological knowledge is essential to interpreting newer, noninvasive monitoring techniques.
“Integrating traditional methods - weighing and handling animals on the ground - with emerging, non-invasive toolsets such as drone imagery means that we can now really tailor the scope of how we look at wildlife health,” said Michelle Shero, an associate scientist in WHOI’s Biology Department and lead author of the study. “From gaining an in-depth understanding of shifts in an animal’s metabolism to capturing indices of condition at unprecedented scales across whole populations, and everything in between, it’s here that physiologists can really help to ground truth and guide efforts as people begin reaching for new technologies to study species that are protected or otherwise less accessible.”
What a photograph can't tell us
Traditionally, researchers have assessed wildlife condition by temporarily capturing and weighing animals, measuring their body dimensions, collecting biological samples, and using tools such as ultrasound to measure fat stores. These methods can provide detailed information about an animal's physiology, but they are labor-intensive, can be stressful for animals, and are not always practical for monitoring large numbers of individuals within a population.
Drone imagery offers an alternative approach. Researchers can use aerial photographs to measure an animal's length, width, and body volume, and to track changes over time without having to handle individual animals. These measurements can be especially valuable for studying species that are difficult to access or observe. And because it can be done quickly, it is also possible to monitor more individuals than through direct handling.
But external measurements alone cannot reliably distinguish between fat and lean tissue. That distinction can be particularly important during energetically demanding periods such as reproduction, migration, or nursing. Weddell seal females, for example, lose substantial amounts of both fat and lean tissue while nursing their pups. Losing lean tissue can have more dire impacts for animals because it comes from the breakdown of muscle and working organs. So once pups are weaned, females regain their lean tissue first. However, a photograph that shows a change in body size can not, on its own, reveal how much of that change is due to lipid versus lean muscle tissue. Understanding these underlying changes can help researchers assess nutritional stress, reproductive success, and how animals are responding to environmental pressures.
Combining established and emerging tools
By pairing drone imagery with targeted biological sampling, researchers can validate photographic measurements and better understand what is happening beneath an animal's external appearance. Other tools, including hormone measurements, genetic analysis, tracking devices, and additional biological indicators, can provide clues about how food availability, disease, environmental stress, and behavior influence an animal's condition.
This integrated approach has important implications for monitoring vulnerable species. Research on North Atlantic right whales, for example, has used aerial imagery and repeated observations to document long-term changes in body size. These findings provide insight into how cumulative pressures, including fishing-gear entanglements, changes in prey availability, and vessel collisions, can affect the energy animals have available for growth and reproduction.
According to UMCES’s Professor Jennifer Burns, a co-author of the study, “Based on external appearance alone, seal pups don’t seem to grow much between the time they are weaned and their first birthday. However, physiological measurements reveal many internal changes: yearlings are much leaner than weaned pups, and their muscles have developed to support effective underwater foraging. Without information on mass, age, and body composition, it would be possible to conclude that seals don’t develop much over their first year of life, when really they are directing energy toward maturation rather than structural growth.”
“Pairing additional internal biomarkers, such as endocrine state, with external appearance, can also provide key insight into an individual's health state,” added KC Bierlich, a co-author who is an assistant professor, senior research from OSU’s Marine Mammal Institute. “For example, a baleen whale might be in average body condition, but may have high stress levels due to anthropogenic or environmental stressors that may be preventing them from further increasing their mass. Pregnant females often require greater mass gain in a feeding season to support the high energetic costs of gestation, which often overlaps with periods of migration and fasting. As such, failure to gain additional mass, particularly for pregnant females, may negatively influence reproduction and, in turn, have negative consequences to the population.”
A clearer picture for conservation
As wildlife faces mounting pressures from a changing climate, habitat degradation, shifting food supplies, disease, and human activities, understanding how animals respond to these challenges is increasingly important.
The review provides researchers with a framework for selecting and combining monitoring tools based on the species, the research questions, and the level of detail needed to assess animal health. By better understanding what remote observations can and cannot tell us, scientists can identify potential warning signs of nutritional stress, track changes in animal populations, and provide more reliable information to guide conservation decisions.

About Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution
The Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution is a private, non-profit organization on Cape Cod, Massachusetts, dedicated to marine research, engineering, and higher education. Established in 1930, its primary mission is to understand the ocean and its interaction with the Earth as a whole, and to communicate an understanding of the ocean’s role in the changing global environment. Top scientists, engineers, and students collaborate on more than 800 concurrent projects worldwide—both above and below the waves—pushing the boundaries of knowledge and possibility.
