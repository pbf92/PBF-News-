TITLE: Saudi Arabia Burn Review Identifies Domestic Scalds as Leading Injury Mechanism
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and proportional meta-analysis examining thermal injury epidemiology in Saudi Arabia has demonstrated that domestic scalds represent the predominant cause of burn trauma across the Kingdom, particularly among pediatric patients. Synthesizing clinical data from 21 observational studies encompassing 6,412 patients treated between 1986 and 2025, the research established that scald injuries accounted for an overall pooled proportion of 49.4 percent of admissions, rising to 64.6 percent within pediatric-only cohorts. In contrast, flame burns accounted for 30.3 percent of cases overall, demonstrating greater frequency in adult and mixed-age groups. Across all cohorts, male patients constituted 61.2 percent of all burn cases, and 80.5 percent of documented incidents occurred within home environments. Inhalation injuries affected 10.2 percent of patients, while intentional injuries remained infrequent at 2.3 percent. Overall pooled mortality was calculated at 3.4 percent, with significant variations observed across distinct publication periods.
Burn injuries represent a persistent global public health challenge, generating substantial morbidity, long-term physical disability, and mortality worldwide. Epidemiological estimates from 2019 indicated more than eight million incident burn cases and over one hundred thousand burn-related fatalities globally, with children and adolescents bearing an acute portion of the worldwide disease burden. Within Saudi Arabia, national burn epidemiology was previously characterized by a 2020 systematic review that synthesized 11 studies involving 3,308 patients. That earlier synthesis highlighted a preponderance of male patients, home-based injuries, and scalds, alongside a 6.9 percent mortality rate in mixed-age cohorts. Over the intervening years, additional published investigations expanded the empirical evidence base, prompting this updated quantitative synthesis to characterize demographic patterns, injury mechanisms, and clinical outcomes across age categories and historical periods.
To establish rigorous analytical standards, the authors conducted this investigation under a prespecified research protocol registered in the PROSPERO database under accession number CRD420261285542. The systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. Investigators executed comprehensive literature searches across PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from database inception through December 27, 2025. Search parameters coupled geographic descriptors for Saudi Arabia with diverse burn terms encompassing thermal, flame, scald, chemical, electrical, and contact mechanisms. Eligibility criteria required English-language human observational studies containing at least 30 patients that reported relevant demographic, clinical, or injury-related parameters. Studies restricted to single burn etiologies, isolated anatomical locations such as facial or hand burns, cadaveric or animal models, case reports under 30 patients, reviews, editorials, and conference abstracts were excluded. Two independent reviewers conducted title and abstract screening followed by full-text evaluation, extracting data on standardized collection forms with discrepancies resolved through consensus or third-party adjudication.
The systematic database search initially identified 1,098 citations, of which 205 duplicate entries were removed, leaving 893 records for title and abstract screening. Thirty-one full-text manuscripts underwent rigorous eligibility review, resulting in 12 exclusions, while citation screening of reference lists identified two additional eligible studies, culminating in 21 included investigations published between 1986 and 2025. Methodological quality was evaluated using design-appropriate appraisal instruments. Cohort studies were appraised using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, with all eight included retrospective cohorts scoring 8 or 9 points, indicating a low risk of methodological bias. Analytical cross-sectional studies were evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist, with all three included studies demonstrating moderate risk of bias due to inadequate confounding adjustment. Ten case series evaluated under the corresponding Joanna Briggs Institute framework exhibited moderate risk of bias relating to consecutive case inclusion and data completeness, while one early 1986 case series had high risk from limited reporting.
Geographical analysis revealed that the included investigations represented multiple administrative territories across Saudi Arabia, though academic output was concentrated in specific metropolitan zones. The Central region accounted for 38.1 percent of included studies, followed by the Western region at 19.0 percent and the Eastern region at 14.3 percent. Participating clinical centers were located in diverse cities including Riyadh, Makkah, Jeddah, Al-Khobar, Tabuk, Abha, Gazan, Al-Baha, and Buraidah. Across the 21 studies, individual sample sizes ranged from 60 to 787 patients, collectively providing data on 6,412 individuals. In terms of evidentiary hierarchy, 11 studies represented Level III evidence and 10 were categorized as Level IV evidence. Retrospective cohort studies comprised eight publications, case series accounted for ten investigations, and cross-sectional designs constituted three studies.
Quantitative statistical analyses were performed using the metaprop function within R software, employing random-effects models with inverse-variance weighting on the logit scale to calculate pooled proportions of patient characteristics and clinical endpoints. Gender analysis across all 21 studies demonstrated that male patients accounted for a pooled proportion of 61.2 percent of burn admissions, accompanied by marked between-study heterogeneity reflected by an I-squared statistic of 81.3 percent. Subgroup analysis by patient age revealed a significantly higher male proportion in mixed-age studies at 63.1 percent compared to 57.0 percent in pediatric-only cohorts, achieving statistical significance with a p-value of 0.019. When stratified by publication era, the pooled proportion of male patients reached 65.4 percent in studies published from 2018 onward, 58.0 percent in studies published between 2000 and 2017, and 58.5 percent in those published in or prior to 1999, though this temporal variation did not reach statistical significance, yielding a p-value of 0.068.
Synthesizing injury mechanisms demonstrated that scalds and flame burns constitute the overwhelming majority of thermal admissions nationwide. Scald burns represented the leading mechanism overall at 49.4 percent across 21 studies, followed by flame injuries at 30.3 percent across 21 studies. Contact burns accounted for 4.8 percent across 11 studies, electrical injuries represented 4.6 percent across 21 studies, and chemical burns comprised 3.8 percent across 18 studies, with substantial heterogeneity across all mechanisms ranging from 85.4 to 97.6 percent. Age-stratified analyses revealed that scalds were disproportionately prevalent in pediatric cohorts at 64.6 percent versus 41.9 percent in mixed-age populations. Conversely, flame burns were more frequent in mixed-age cohorts at 33.6 percent compared to 24.2 percent in pediatric studies. Electrical burns demonstrated a statistically significant difference between mixed-age and pediatric groups at 5.7 percent versus 2.5 percent, with a p-value of 0.044. Contact burns affected 5.8 percent of pediatric cases and 4.5 percent of mixed cohorts, while chemical burns remained comparable at 3.9 percent and 3.4 percent.
Temporal evaluations across publication eras demonstrated statistically significant shifts in specific burn mechanisms over time. Investigations published from 2018 onward recorded the highest pooled proportions of flame burns at 40.6 percent, contact burns at 8.4 percent, chemical burns at 5.3 percent, and electrical burns at 4.4 percent, whereas scalds peaked in literature published between 2000 and 2017 at 62.6 percent. Publication-period differences attained statistical significance for flame burns with a p-value of 0.0013 and for contact burns with a p-value of 0.0226. Analysis of injury settings confirmed the dominance of domestic environments, which accounted for a pooled 80.5 percent of burns across ten reporting studies, with an I-squared value of 97.1 percent. Domestic burns represented 85.4 percent in mixed-age studies and 65.9 percent in pediatric cohorts, without statistical significance between groups. In contrast, workplace and school burns accounted for 5.7 percent across eight studies, showing a significant elevation in mixed-age cohorts at 8.9 percent versus 0.3 percent in pediatric cohorts, with a p-value below 0.0001.
Clinical severity parameters and hospital utilization patterns revealed notable variations across reporting medical centers. Total body surface area involvement exhibited study means ranging from 13 percent to 27 percent and median values between 10 percent and 11 percent, while severe burns exceeding 40 percent total body surface area generally represented under 10 percent of cases. Analysis of eight studies reporting burn depth indicated that partial-thickness burns predominated at 65.5 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 50.7 to 77.8 percent, compared to full-thickness burns at 32.7 percent, with a confidence interval of 21.8 to 45.8 percent. Length of hospital stay across 14 reporting studies exhibited mean or median durations ranging from 7.0 to 27.2 days. Inhalation injuries occurred in 10.2 percent of cases across nine studies, with pediatric cohorts reporting 22.2 percent across two studies versus 8.3 percent across seven mixed-age studies. Intentional burn injuries accounted for a pooled 2.3 percent across four reporting studies.
Overall pooled in-hospital mortality across the 21 studies was 3.4 percent with an I-squared value of 94.8 percent, exhibiting higher estimates in mixed-age cohorts at 4.7 percent than in pediatric cohorts at 1.7 percent. Mortality differed significantly across publication eras, declining from 6.0 percent in studies published through 1999 to 1.6 percent in 2000 through 2017, before reaching 3.8 percent from 2018 onward, yielding a p-value of 0.024. The authors emphasized that substantial between-study heterogeneity, regional study clustering, and reliance on single-center retrospective observational cohorts without population controls represent notable limitations. Furthermore, single-arm proportional meta-analysis estimates caseload characteristics among hospitalized patients rather than population-level burn incidence or risk ratios. The researchers concluded that establishing standardized national burn registries, implementing validated severity-adjustment tools such as the Abbreviated Burn Severity Index and revised Baux score, and deploying targeted home-safety and workplace interventions are essential steps to improve epidemiological surveillance and outcomes across Saudi Arabia.
