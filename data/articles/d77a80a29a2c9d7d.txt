TITLE: New phrase-match searches cost 28% more per lead, New Path Digital finds
OUTLET: PPC Land

Searches that Google matched to phrase-match keywords for the first time cost 28% more per lead than the searches those same keywords were already matching, according to a study New Path Digital published on October 27, 2026. The High Point, North Carolina, advertising agency examined nearly 50,000 keyword-and-search-term pairs from three Google Ads lead-generation accounts it manages, comparing what each keyword matched in 2022-23 with what it matched in 2025-26. Exact match moved the other way: conversions from newly matched exact-match queries cost 7% less than those from existing ones. The study, written by Digital Marketing Manager Mary Rippe, was last updated on November 5, 2026.
In Short
New Path Digital compared the searches three of its clients' Google Ads keywords matched in 2022-23 with the new ones the same keywords picked up in 2025-26, and found that new phrase-match searches cost $209 per lead against $163 for the old ones. It matters for lead-generation advertisers because the extra cost came from new searches converting less often, not from pricier clicks, while new exact-match searches cost $109 per lead against $116. The agency calls the work a field report rather than a benchmark: three accounts, correlations only, and every figure a cost per form fill rather than per qualified lead.
What the study measured
New Path Digital drew the data from three accounts it manages: a law firm, a higher-education advertiser and a local B2B service business. The figures were pooled, so every number in the study blends the three accounts and is split only by match type. Performance figures cover April 2025 through March 2026. The query comparison sets two periods against each other, labelled 2022-23 and 2025-26; the study does not give the start and end months of the earlier period.
The comparison rests on two labels. "We call queries 'existing' or 'new' throughout," the study states. An existing query appeared in the search terms report for the exact same keyword in both periods. A new query triggered that keyword only in 2025-26. A keyword qualified for the analysis only if it had impressions in both periods, with the account, campaign, match type and keyword all unchanged. The design holds the keyword constant and asks what Google started matching it to.
Three further conditions shape the numbers. The leads are form fills generated by non-brand keywords. Every campaign ran on Maximize Conversions, one of Google's Smart Bidding strategies. And each keyword-and-search-term pair was counted separately, so one search term can appear more than once if it matched several keywords.
The headline cost figures are not simple pooled averages. The three accounts have very different costs per lead, and new queries made up a different share of each. According to the study, pooling everything let that mix distort the answer: one pricier account supplied 24% of new exact-match conversions but only 6% of existing ones, which made new exact-match queries look more expensive in the pooled data even though they were cheaper in both accounts that ran exact match. To correct for this, the agency weighted each account's new-query cost per lead by its share of existing-query conversions. In the study's own words, the adjustment answers the question: "what would new queries have cost if they'd come from the same account mix as existing ones?" Click price and conversion rate were adjusted in the same way. The study does not publish the unadjusted pooled figures.

Phrase match: same click price, fewer conversions
The phrase-match result is the larger of the two. Conversions from new phrase-match queries cost $209 each, against $163 for queries the same keywords already matched. The gap did not come from what Google charged per click. Clicks on new and existing phrase-match queries cost the same, about $16. The difference came from conversion rate, which fell from 10% on existing queries to 8% on new ones.
New queries also dominate phrase-match spending. According to the study, conversions from new phrase-match queries "ate up ~75% of phrase match cost." The pair counts point the same way. New phrase-match pairs number 38,332, or 92% of all phrase-match pairs and 77% of every pair in the study, by PPC Land's calculation. Most of them never spent anything: only 3,234 of those pairs, or 8.4%, recorded any cost. The 375 conversions they produced amount to 66% of phrase-match conversions in the raw counts, a smaller share than the roughly 75% of phrase-match cost the study assigns to them. Those raw counts are not adjusted for account mix, so the comparison is indicative rather than exact.
The study gives one example of the drift. The phrase keyword "veterans legal services" matched the new query "jag fort bragg nc." The two share no words. The agency's explanation is that JAG offices, the military's legal service, typically provide free legal help to service members, so the search comes from someone looking for a free option rather than a paying client. "This suggests semantic phrase matching may be a rougher fit for lead generation businesses," the study concludes.
Exact match: cheaper clicks, slightly lower conversion
Exact match moved in the opposite direction. Conversions from new exact-match queries cost $109, against $116 for existing queries, a 7% reduction according to the study. Here the click price did the work. New exact-match clicks averaged $10, compared with $11 for existing ones, and that saving more than covered a slightly lower conversion rate of 9% against 10%.
The agency's example is a legal keyword. "Military disability lawyer" matched the new, longer query "va lawyer for disability," and those clicks cost about $13 each, which the study puts at roughly 40% below the keyword's average. "Our read: when Google stretches an exact keyword to a query with the same intent," the study states, the result may be "a similar searcher at a lower price."
The dollar figures as published are rounded. Taken at face value, $109 against $116 is a 6% difference by PPC Land's calculation; the study's 7% is consistent with unrounded values within half a dollar of each published figure.
How far each match type strays
The second half of the study asks how far Google stretches each match type. The agency sorted every impression in both periods into five buckets by comparing each query with the closest keyword of the same match type in its campaign. That covered 75,698 exact-match and 119,808 phrase-match impressions in 2022-23, and 74,786 exact-match and 145,453 phrase-match impressions in 2025-26. Phrase-match impressions in the analysis rose 21% between the periods while exact-match impressions fell 1%, by PPC Land's calculation.

"Surprisingly, exact match didn't loosen across our client accounts," the study states. Queries identical to the keyword rose from 29% to 35% of exact-match volume. Almost half of exact-match volume, 46%, shares only some of the keyword's words, but 54% is the keyword itself, the keyword with extra words, or a one- or two-word synonym swap.
"Phrase match is where the drift shows," according to the study. Queries sharing some or no words with the keyword grew from 70% to 79% of phrase-match volume, while queries that were the keyword with extra words or a synonym fell from 24% to 15%. The share with no words in common rose from 2% to 9%. Phrase match, in its original design, showed an ad when the keyword appeared inside a longer search; by the study's own count, only 5% of phrase-match volume in 2025-26 was the keyword word for word, and a further 10% was the keyword with extra words.
The choice of the closest keyword matters for these shares. Google credits each query to a single keyword even when several could have matched, so the agency compared each query with the nearest keyword of the same match type in the campaign rather than only the credited one. "Using only the credited keyword, 31% of exact-match volume was identical," the study states, against 35% under the closest-keyword method. The 2022-23 shares use the same method.
Close to the retired broad match modifier
The study's most quotable comparison is with broad match modifier, which Google retired in 2021. Google announced on February 4, 2021 that phrase match would absorb broad match modifier traffic, starting on February 18, 2021, and from July 2021 new BMM keywords could no longer be created. The study defines the old rule plainly: "BMM required every keyword word (or a close variant) to appear in the query, in any order, with extra words allowed." Run against that rule, 75% of exact-match query volume passes, compared with 41% of phrase-match query volume.
The remaining 25% of exact-match volume did not contain every word of the keyword. The study describes that slice as "Google reading meaning where BMM would have needed the words." Its conclusion is that exact match now sits in a middle ground: "So think of exact match as looser than literal exact, tighter than phrase, and BMM-like with some semantic overflow."
The agency also restates the 2025-26 exact-match volume in terms of the jobs the match types were built to do. About 45% is the keyword or a near twin, meaning the same words or a swap such as "attorney" for "lawyer." About 9% is the keyword inside a longer search, which the study calls the phrase-match job. The remaining 46% shares some of the keyword's words without the same shape.
The result was not uniform
The pooled figures hide large differences between the three accounts. New phrase-match queries cost about 75% more per lead in one account, 29% more in another and about 3% more in the third. The study says the phrase result is concentrated in two of the three accounts. Only two of the three accounts ran exact match, and in both of them new exact-match queries were cheaper, by about 7% to 8%. The smallest account has only about 100 conversions behind its figures. The study does not say which account is the law firm, the higher-education advertiser or the B2B service business in this breakdown.
The agency is explicit about the limits. "These are correlations. Search behavior, negatives, budgets, bidding, and Google's own matching changes could all have shifted between periods, and three accounts make this a field report rather than a benchmark," the study states. Its own FAQ answers the question of whether the findings are a Google Ads benchmark with: "No."
A second caveat concerns what counts as a lead. "Every figure here is cost per form fill, not cost per qualified lead, enrollment, sale, or signed deal," the study states. A form fill from the JAG search and a form fill from a paying client carry the same weight in every number above.
A third concerns visibility. "Nearly 43% of eligible search terms were hidden by Google, mostly on phrase match," according to the study. The agency could analyse only the queries visible in the search terms report, so it notes that the "shares some words" and "no words in common" shares for phrase match could be larger than reported.
