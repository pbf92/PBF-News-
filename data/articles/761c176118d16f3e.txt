TITLE: Mailchimp sells for $3.68bn as Walmart's ad growth slows to 25%
OUTLET: PPC Land

Thursday, November 19, 2026 was a day of price tags and fine print. Bending Spoons agreed to buy Mailchimp from Intuit for $3.68bn in cash, about a third of what Intuit paid five years ago. The two largest American retail media businesses after Amazon both reported slower advertising growth: Walmart's global ad sales rose 25%, its weakest pace in two years, and Target's ad revenue grew 23% to $296m. A holiday study found Amazon draws a quarter of retailer searches but appears in only about a tenth of AI shopping answers, and a SparkToro analysis put the share of ChatGPT conversations showing purchase intent at 6.9%. Microsoft said its new Discover feed carries 35% fewer ads per page than MSN, Google kept testing how ads look inside AI Mode, and Nielsen will soon credit a radio listener after a single minute. Apple let apps send the Global Privacy Control signal, Minnesota settled its first privacy case, Spain told companies that marketing chiefs should not be data protection officers, and a Colombian court upheld orders against Google over teenagers' data. Nike explained why it wants creators to build its brand, a Daily Mail sales unit set out to represent its own creators, and Spanish advertisers paused TikTok after reports of abuse material on the platform. The thread running through the day was proof: buyers, regulators and courts asked for evidence where they used to accept assurances.

Retail media loses some speed
Walmart's advertising engine is still growing fast, just less fast than before. PPC Land reported that Walmart's global ad growth dropped to 25% as Flipkart's sale slipped a quarter. In the third quarter of fiscal 2027, the three months to October 31, global advertising grew 25%, down from 38% in the previous quarter. Most of the slowdown came from abroad: international advertising fell 15% because India's Flipkart Big Billion Days sale moved into the fourth quarter. In the United States, advertising grew 37% including VIZIO and Vibe.co, and Walmart Connect on its own rose 36%, down from 43%. Adweek reported that Walmart saw its weakest global ad sales growth in two years, the slowest since the first quarter of 2024 and well below the 53% recorded a year earlier.
The numbers around advertising explain why it matters so much to the company. Total revenue reached $188.8bn, up 5.2%, while net income fell 19.5%, mainly because of an investment loss tied to Symbotic. US ecommerce sales grew 23% and marketplace net sales 49%. John David Rainey, the chief financial officer, said US ecommerce was close to paying for itself without ad income: "we are actually getting to a point where we are pretty close to break-even without advertising, which was pretty remarkable." Seth Dallaire, chief growth officer, pointed to televisions as a new front: "The TVs that are powered by VIZIO OS have been the number one sellers in the U.S." Walmart raised its full-year guidance for the second time and launched a $120 bundle of Walmart+ and Sam's Club Plus on October 20, but it still does not disclose advertising revenue in dollars.
Target, which does disclose a figure, showed a similar shape. PPC Land reported that Target's ad revenue climbed 23% to $296m in the third quarter, up from $241m, for the three months to October 31. Over nine months, advertising revenue reached $821m, up 32.2%, so the third quarter grew more slowly than the first half. Net sales rose 7.1% to $27.1bn and comparable sales rose 5.6%. Diluted earnings of $2.65 a share included $0.50 from $301m in tariff refunds, which flatters the underlying profit picture. Target also books some activity from Roundel, its ad network, as cost reductions rather than revenue, which makes clean comparisons with rivals harder. "Our investments are building on one another," said Michael Fiddelke, the chief executive.
The holiday data suggests the retailers' search dominance does not carry over neatly into AI assistants. PPC Land reported that Amazon draws 24% of retailer searches but 11% of AI shopping answers, in a 30-page US holiday report from Verve, the ad tech company that owns Captify, covering September 1 to November 16. Walmart had 12% of searches and 9% of answers, Target 5% and 8%, and Best Buy and eBay 2% and 3% each. The report did not explain the gap, named no AI platforms and included no sales data, and it cautioned that "a mention is not a recommendation." AI prompts were also far longer than searches: the median AI gift prompt ran 24 words against nine for a gift search, and 7.2% of AI gift prompts stated a budget, against 0.49% of searches, with a median stated budget of $50.
Amazon kept documenting its new buying tools. PPC Land reported that Amazon's DVA+ frequency caps count Netflix and Prime Video as one. A Support Center page updated on November 19 says a single counter tracks each viewer's impressions across every publisher a campaign buys. "Frequency caps apply across multiple publishers and supply sources," the page says. Private auctions and preferred deals are fully enforced, but some programmatic guaranteed deals count towards the cap and keep delivering after it, and Twitch share-of-voice lines and Fire tablet ads sit outside caps altogether. PPC Land found Amazon's worked example off by one and two of its three savings formulas inconsistent with their stated units.
A second page sets the rules for what a campaign is for. PPC Land reported that Amazon's DVA+ primer lists 15 KPIs, with one goal per campaign. Each campaign has one goal, one budget and one KPI inherited by every ad group, so an awareness push and a sales push need separate campaigns. The three goals are awareness, consideration and conversion, and Amazon warns that changing a KPI after launch resets a campaign's performance learnings and may affect optimisation. The page names five display ad types, caps standard display at ten image assets and uses both "DVA+" and "Amazon Ads Agent" for the same product in different places.
Brands get a simpler tool in their own stores. PPC Land reported that Amazon's free Brand Store quiz narrows up to 50 products with four questions. The Product Selector module, listed in 25 marketplaces, asks shoppers up to four questions with up to six answers each and shows only products that match every answer. "Product Selector is free, self-service, and requires no minimum spend," Amazon says. The logic is rule-based, since brands assign products to answers by hand, which is a contrast with Rufus, where Amazon's models choose. Early agency write-ups described a beta capped at 35 products and three questions.
Walmart also published a reminder that its stores double as clinics, and clinics bring shoppers. PPC Land reported that nearly three in four Walmart test-and-treat visits come after 5 p.m. or on weekends. The service, where pharmacists can test for flu, strep throat and COVID-19, prescribe and fill in one visit, runs at more than 1,500 of roughly 4,600 Walmart pharmacies in 24 states. "Our pharmacists are often one of the most accessible healthcare professionals in the communities we serve," said Kevin Host, senior vice president of pharmacy for Walmart US. PPC Land noted that pharmacy visits are also store visits that feed Walmart Connect, while health records remain subject to HIPAA.
Deals, prices and people
The biggest transaction of the day reset the value of an email brand. PPC Land reported that Bending Spoons agreed to buy Mailchimp from Intuit for $3.68bn in cash. The Milan-based company, which already owns Vimeo, AOL, Eventbrite and WeTransfer, will pay an enterprise value of $3.68bn, and Intuit will reinvest $368m of the proceeds in new Bending Spoons shares. The price is about 31% of the roughly $12bn Intuit paid in 2021. Bending Spoons describes a playbook of reorganising teams, overhauling technology and pushing monetisation, and says it "has never sold a material business." Mailchimp's domain share fell from 72.68% in January 2022 to 67.54% in May 2024 as Klaviyo climbed, and its current revenue was not disclosed. Closing is expected in early 2027.
Software buyers are getting pickier about growth. PPC Land reported that an ex-Semrush president set a 10% growth floor for martech firms he would buy. Eugene Levin, who led most of Semrush's acquisitions before Adobe's $1.9bn purchase, told the Ahrefs Podcast that he wants companies with $3m to $10m in annual recurring revenue growing at least 10% a year, and will accept breakeven or a 10% margin. He said businesses now trade at two or three times recurring revenue and warned that founder-led firms carry key-person risk: "Smaller companies have much higher dependency on key people, right?" He valued Backlinko's founder-led brand at nothing: "We had to multiply it by zero and that's it."
HubSpot decided how it will charge when AI agents use its software. PPC Land reported that HubSpot won't charge extra when Claude or ChatGPT works its CRM. Everyday use through its connectors and MCP server is included with each seat. "This everyday connector use isn't an extra charge. It comes included with a HubSpot seat," wrote Duncan Lennox. Third-party agents calling existing APIs draw on daily allowances of 250,000 calls for Free and Starter, 650,000 for Professional and 1,000,000 for Enterprise, with capacity packs at $500 a month. Large-scale agent work in a governed environment, still in development, will be billed in credits at $0.010 each. "Every software platform is facing the same decision right now: what to do when AI agents show up at the door," Lennox wrote.
Small advertisers are getting agents of their own. PPC Land reported that InMobi lets seven AI agents run small firms' Google, Meta and ChatGPT ads. InMobi Accelerate plans, builds, launches and measures campaigns across Google Ads, Meta Ads, Microsoft Advertising, TikTok Ads and ChatGPT Ads, and is free to start. "Every plan, creative asset and budget is presented for approval before anything goes live," InMobi says. A commissioned NewtonX survey of 800 US small advertisers and agencies found 93% cannot confidently measure performance across platforms and only 4% can launch a campaign the same day. "AI agents change the economics of expertise," said Rohit Dosi, who runs the product. InMobi did not explain how its single "True ROAS" figure is attributed.
LinkedIn hired a familiar face to sell its ads. Adweek reported that LinkedIn tapped a former Trade Desk executive to lead global ad sales. Anders Mortensen, who left The Trade Desk in June after about seven months as chief revenue officer and joined it from Google, is now vice president of global sales for LinkedIn Ads, reporting to Matthew Derella, senior vice president of LinkedIn Ads and Microsoft Advertising. In his own post he wrote that "AI is changing how people discover brands and make decisions," while "the importance of trust, relationships, and human connection isn't going anywhere."
Search after the AI answer
Google spent the day adjusting the furniture of its results pages. Search Engine Roundtable reported that Google is placing emojis in the AI-generated "What people are saying" box. The emojis come from Google's own AI descriptions rather than the websites it references, and a user named Will spotted the behaviour on Bluesky. Barry Schwartz could replicate it but was cautious about its novelty: "I looked back at my old screenshots of What people are saying and I didn't see emojis in them, but it might not be 100% new."
