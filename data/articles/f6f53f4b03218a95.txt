TITLE: Paternal antidepressant use before conception linked to children's metabolic risks
OUTLET: News-Medical

Use of antidepressants by fathers in the months just before pregnancy is associated with a slightly increased risk of metabolic disorders, particularly high blood pressure, in their children, finds a large study from Denmark published by The BMJ today.
For this study, metabolic disorders were defined as having any of the following conditions: type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure (hypertension), high cholesterol levels (hypercholesterolaemia), and overweight or obesity.
However, the researchers point out that the increased risks were not statistically significant for type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol levels, and overweight or obesity, and were not seen when comparing children whose fathers used antidepressants only before pregnancy with children whose fathers used antidepressants only during pregnancy.
These findings suggest that the slightly increased risk may be due to underlying paternal mental health conditions, rather than the drugs themselves. They also provide some reassurance for men treated with antidepressants who are planning a family.
The use of antidepressants among men of reproductive age has risen in recent years. Previous studies have examined the association of paternal antidepressant use before conception with birth outcomes and neurodevelopmental outcomes, but studies focused on metabolic disorders in offspring are limited.
To fill this knowledge gap, researchers set out to investigate the associations between paternal antidepressant use during spermatogenesis (the period of sperm development; about three months before pregnancy) and risk of metabolic disorders in offspring.
They used Danish health registers to identify 1,365,887 children born in Denmark between 2000 and 2023 who were followed from birth for up to 23 years. Of these, 23,572 (1.7%) had fathers who filled at least one antidepressant prescription during spermatogenesis. These children were compared with 1,342,315 children whose fathers did not use antidepressants during this period.
To account for underlying paternal mental health conditions, 4,156 children whose fathers used antidepressants only during spermatogenesis were compared with 17,318 whose fathers used them only during pregnancy.
Other potentially influential factors in both the mother and father at or around the time of delivery were also taken into account. These included age, education level, psychiatric history, other medication use, and history of metabolic conditions.
During an average follow-up of 12 years, 18,353 (1.3%) of children were diagnosed with a metabolic disorder, including 364 whose fathers used antidepressants during spermatogenesis and 17,989 whose fathers did not use antidepressants during this period (1.39 v 1.11 events per 1,000 person years, respectively).
Children whose fathers used antidepressants during spermatogenesis had a slightly higher risk of developing high blood pressure compared with unexposed children. No increased risks were seen for type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol levels, and overweight or obesity.
When compared with offspring of fathers who took antidepressants during the pregnancy period only, no increased risk of any metabolic disorder or of specific metabolic disorders was found, suggesting that the associations are more likely due to underlying paternal mental illness than to the drugs themselves.
In other words, initiating treatment during pregnancy likely signals worsening of mental illness that was already present during the spermatogenesis period, explain the authors.
They acknowledge that the observational design of their study and limited follow-up to early adulthood preclude definitive conclusions about causality and long term risks. But say their findings are based on several rich data sources from preconception to early adulthood, therefore minimising recall and selection biases.
As such, they conclude: "The slightly increased risk of metabolic disorders in offspring of fathers using antidepressants during spermatogenesis was probably attributable to underlying paternal mental health disorders. These findings offer reassurance for men treated with antidepressants before pregnancy."
