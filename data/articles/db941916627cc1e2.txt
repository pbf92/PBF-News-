TITLE: Robotic Pancreaticoduodenectomy May Reduce Blood Transfusions in Older Patients
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy may reduce the need for blood transfusions in patients aged 70 years and older compared with open pancreaticoduodenectomy, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis published in Langenbeck’s Archives of Surgery. The study evaluated short-term outcomes in older surgical patients to determine whether procedural differences persisted after accounting for patient selection. While the robotic approach showed a lower rate of blood transfusion, other clinical advantages were not robust across sensitivity analyses.
The research investigated whether previously reported advantages of robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy over open surgery apply to older populations. Surgical teams led by Eitan Dines, Danhui Heo, and Ahmad Mahamid sought to clarify these short-term outcomes in patients aged 70 and older. Because patient selection often skews surgical comparisons, the authors focused on whether clinical differences between the two techniques remained evident once patient selection was addressed.
Following PRISMA 2020 guidelines and registered under PROSPERO CRD420251116563, the authors searched PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library for studies published through 1 November 2026. The search focused on comparative studies evaluating robotic versus open pancreaticoduodenectomy in patients aged 70 years and older. Methodological quality and risk of bias were evaluated using ROBINS-I, while the certainty of evidence for each outcome was rated using the GRADE framework.
The review identified seven retrospective cohort studies encompassing 3,183 total patients, including 579 who underwent robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy. All included studies were classified as having a moderate or serious risk of bias. Random-effects meta-analyses showed that robotic surgery may reduce blood transfusions, yielding a risk ratio of 0.60 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.42 to 0.84, with the overall certainty of evidence rated as low.
A reduction in blood transfusions was the only outcome that persisted across all sensitivity analyses. When random-effects meta-analyses were repeated in propensity score-matched cohorts to account for patient selection, the benefit remained stable, showing a risk ratio of 0.60 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.39 to 0.91. This consistency established reduced blood transfusion as the single robust finding favoring the robotic procedure.
Other measured outcomes failed to demonstrate consistent advantages. Robotic surgery was associated with fewer major complications, with a risk ratio of 0.77 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.62 to 0.95 at low certainty, and a reduced hospital stay, with a mean difference of minus 2.44 days and a 95% confidence interval of minus 4.30 to minus 0.58 days. However, the evidence for hospital stay was very uncertain, and neither outcome persisted in propensity score-matched cohorts. Evidence regarding mortality and readmission was also very uncertain.
The authors concluded that non-transfusion differences were not robust, suggesting a smaller clinical benefit for robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy than previously reported. While advanced age alone should not preclude robotic surgery in experienced centers, the researchers emphasized that surgical effects in older patients must be better defined in randomized trials.
