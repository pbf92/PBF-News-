TITLE: Character.ai asks judge to make $10m bid for users' chats the one to beat
OUTLET: PPC Land

Character Technologies, the bankrupt company behind Character.ai, on November 25 asked a federal bankruptcy judge in Delaware to sign an order that formally makes Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC the opening bidder for the Character.ai app, its AI models and its users' chats, at $10 million. The draft order would also grant the buyer its bid protections, a $350,000 payment if a rival outbids it. According to the filing, the US Trustee, the court's consumer privacy ombudsman, the creditors' committee's proposed lawyers and the buyer's lawyers all saw the draft, and none of them objects to it being entered.
In Short
The company selling off Character.ai has sent the judge a ready-to-sign order that names Smirks and Pangs as the official first bidder for the app, its AI models and users' chats, at $10 million, with $350,000 for Smirks and Pangs if someone else wins. The government's bankruptcy watchdog, the court's privacy ombudsman and the lawyers for unpaid creditors say they do not oppose it, so the order would fix the price any rival has to beat at the December 1 auction. It does not approve the sale itself: that decision, and the objections of 43 states and the District of Columbia about what happens to users' data, belong to the hearing set for December 7 and 8.
What the company filed
The filing is a certification of counsel, Docket No. 255 in case 26-11603 before Judge Brendan L. Shannon of the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware. A certification of counsel is the route lawyers in that court use to send a judge a proposed order after a hearing, together with a statement of who has seen it, so the judge can sign without hearing the matter again. It was entered on the docket at 4:19 p.m. Eastern on November 25 and runs to six pages: three for the certification and three for the proposed order attached as Exhibit A.
Heather P. Lambert of Wilson Sonsini Goodrich & Rosati signed it for Character Technologies, which acts in the case as debtor in possession, the term for a bankrupt company that keeps running its own business and selling its own assets under court supervision. Erin R. Fay and Shane M. Reil of the same firm appear on the signature block.
The request is short. "WHEREFORE, the Debtor respectfully requests that the Court enter the Proposed Order at its earliest convenience," according to the filing.
The draft order carries the title "Order (I) Authorizing the Debtor to Designate Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, as Additional Stalking Horse Purchaser and (II) Granting Related Relief." A stalking horse is a bidder whose offer is agreed before an auction and sets the minimum price others must beat. In exchange, it usually gets a fee if it loses. Disney Worldwide Services already holds that position for a separate package, a license to Character.ai's technology, at $20 million with a $700,000 break-up fee. Smirks and Pangs is the stalking horse for almost everything else.
What the draft order says
The operative part has five numbered paragraphs.
The first two paragraphs carry the substance. Paragraph 1 reads: "To the extent not resolved or deemed moot, objections to the designation of Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC as Additional Stalking Horse Purchaser are overruled."
Paragraph 2 reads: "Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC is designated as Additional Stalking Horse Purchaser and is granted the Bid Protections. For the avoidance of doubt, paragraphs 1, 10, and 11 of the Bidding Procedures Order shall apply as to the Additional Stalking Horse Purchaser."
"Bid Protections" is not defined in the draft. A footnote sends readers to the definitions in the November 14 notice that first named Smirks and Pangs, and in the bidding procedures order. That notice described a termination payment of 3.5% of the price, or $350,000, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai users' chats faced a $10m sale to Smirks and Pangs. The draft does not restate what paragraphs 1, 10 and 11 of the bidding procedures order contain.
Paragraph 3 says the order's terms "shall be immediately effective and enforceable upon its entry." In plain terms, there would be no waiting period between the judge's signature and the designation taking hold.
The recitals list the papers the judge would be ruling on: the November 14 notice (Docket No. 164), the company's motion to keep the buyer's financial backer confidential (Docket No. 203), the US Trustee's objections raised at the hearing, the supplemental notice filed on November 24 (Docket No. 234) and the company's withdrawal of its confidentiality motion (Docket No. 235). They also state the usual grounds for the court's power to decide: 28 U.S.C. sections 157 and 1334, a "core proceeding" under section 157(b)(2), and venue under sections 1408 and 1409.
How the designation reached the judge
The certification recounts the path in nine numbered paragraphs. Read together with the docket, the process under the bidding rules ran in seven steps.
- The rules. On November 4, the court entered the bidding procedures order, Docket No. 106. Among other things, it approved Disney as stalking horse for the "Disney Target Assets" and authorized the company to designate additional stalking horse purchasers for the assets. The draft version of that order, filed on November 3, was covered when the judge said he would overrule 40 states' objections to Character.ai's Disney sale rules.
- The notice. On November 14, the company filed its notice designating Smirks and Pangs for "Assets Other Than the Disney Target Assets." Objections were due by 9:00 p.m. Eastern on November 18, "which was extended for certain parties," according to the certification.
- The objection. On November 19, the US Trustee filed a written objection, Docket No. 202. Its complaint was that the notice did not name the parent company or sponsor of a buyer formed the day before, as covered when Character.ai's $10m bidder faced objection over its unnamed backer.
- The confidentiality motion. The same day, the company asked the court to let it keep that sponsor's identity out of public filings, as reported when Character.ai asked the court to keep its $10m buyer's backer secret.
- The first hearing. The court heard the notice and the motion on November 23. The US Trustee "raised certain objections to the Motion," and the court-appointed consumer privacy ombudsman "likewise raised an objection to the Notice and Motion," according to the certification. "For the reasons set forth on the record at the Hearing, the Court continued the Hearing to November 24, 2026." That hearing was covered when Character.ai's banker won court approval for its $1.75m sale fee.
- The disclosure. On November 24, the company filed a supplemental notice and "subsequently withdrew the Motion." At the continued hearing that afternoon, the company's lawyer said the supplemental notice names the sponsor as Octave Fund I LLC, and the judge said he was prepared to enter an order approving the designation once the company submitted one.
- The order. On November 25, the company filed the proposed order and the certification. No signed order had appeared by the last docket entry that evening, a declaration filed at 6:40 p.m. Eastern.
Eleven days passed between the notice and the certification, by PPC Land's count. Under the original schedule, a designation that drew no objection by November 18 would have been treated as approved without a hearing.
Who saw the draft, and who is not on the list
Paragraph 9 of the certification names four recipients. "Prior to filing the Proposed Order, the Debtor shared copies of the Proposed Order with counsel to the Additional Stalking Horse Purchaser, the U.S. Trustee, the CPO and proposed counsel to the Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors, none of whom object to its entry."
CPO is the court's shorthand for the consumer privacy ombudsman, Lucy L. Thomson, whose appointment at $650 an hour was covered when Character.ai's planned data sale got a $650-an-hour privacy ombudsman. The committee represents creditors who are owed money and have no collateral.
State attorneys general do not appear among the four. A coalition of 42 states and the District of Columbia objected on November 20 to the Smirks and Pangs sale and, in its request for relief, asked the court to decline the designation and the termination payment on the record as it then stood, as PPC Land reported when 42 states and DC asked the court to keep chats out of Character.ai's $10m sale. Texas filed its own objection, then dropped its demand that the sponsor be named, as covered when Texas no longer asked the court to make Character.ai name its $10m buyer's backer. Neither filing is mentioned in the certification. Paragraph 1 of the draft overrules objections only "to the extent not resolved or deemed moot" and does not say which, if any, remain.
Google LLC is not mentioned either. It filed a limited objection at 3:43 p.m. the same afternoon, Docket No. 254, concerning the contracts and Google accounts that would move under the Smirks and Pangs agreement. That is a separate matter for the sale.
The two documents also use the word "Objection" in two ways. The certification defines it as the US Trustee's written filing of November 19, Docket No. 202. The draft order defines it as the objections the US Trustee raised at the hearing, and its list of referenced filings, Docket Nos. 164, 203, 234 and 235, leaves out Docket No. 202. The order's recitals do not mention the ombudsman's objection, which the certification records.
What the designation fixes, and what it leaves open
A signed order would settle two things. Smirks and Pangs' $10 million becomes the official floor for the package of assets outside Disney's deal, and Smirks and Pangs collects $350,000 if another bidder wins. Bids were due at noon on November 24. The certification does not say whether any arrived.
Combined, the two stalking horse prices come to $30 million and the two payments to $1.05 million, by PPC Land's calculation.
The package matters to the advertising business because of what is in it. The November 14 notice listed users' accounts, characters and chat content, advertising and analytics data, consent and ad opt-out records, the platform's ad monetization assets and its Google Cloud, Google Play, Apple and Stripe accounts. Character.ai is a companion chatbot service. Its advertising brought in about $2.5 million in August 2026, while the company ran an operating loss of about $8 million to $9 million a month, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai lost $8m a month despite $2.5m in monthly ad sales. The signed purchase agreement with Smirks and Pangs relies on moving users' chats without asking them again, as covered when Character.ai's $10m sale hinged on moving chats without further consent.
None of that is approved by this order. The designation decides who bids first and what the first bidder is paid if it loses. Whether any buyer may take users' chats, and on what terms, is for the sale hearing on December 7 and 8, where the states' objections will be heard. In its November 23 reply in the designation dispute, the company argued that refusing the designation would remove a $10 million price floor a day before bids were due, as covered when Character.ai said blocking its $10m bidder a day before bids protects no one.
A data sale of this kind has a recent marker. In August, Google won bankrupt Spirit Airlines' data for $10 million, a sale of corporate records rather than consumers' conversations.
What the filing does not say
The draft order does not name Octave Fund I LLC or anyone who has put money into that fund. It does not say whether rival bids were received, and it does not restate the bidding-order paragraphs it applies to Smirks and Pangs. It does not explain why the state attorneys general are absent from the list of parties consulted. And as of the docket's last entry on November 25, the judge had not signed it.
