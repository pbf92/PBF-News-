TITLE: Amazon's DVA+ frequency caps count Netflix and Prime Video as one
OUTLET: PPC Land

Amazon Ads today updated its Support Center page "Understand frequency in DVA+ campaigns," and the page describes a single counter that tracks each user's impressions across every publisher a campaign buys. An ad served on Netflix counts against the same limit as one served on Prime Video or Fire TV, according to Amazon, and the counting starts without any new setting, opt-in or targeting change.
The same page records where that limit stops short. Programmatic guaranteed deals from publishers other than Google AdX, Amazon Publisher Direct and Amazon's own properties keep delivering after the cap is reached, as do Amazon Audience Guaranteed campaigns, while Twitch share of voice lines and ads on Fire tablets and Kindle devices fall outside caps altogether. It also sets out three formulas for measuring what a cap saved, and limits each advertiser account to two advertiser-level frequency groups.
In Short
Amazon's help page says a frequency cap in DVA+, its tool for buying display, video and streaming TV ads, counts impressions from every publisher a campaign buys, so an ad seen on Netflix uses up the same allowance as one seen on Prime Video. That matters to brands buying streaming TV across several services, because the cap can only stop bids DVA+ controls: guaranteed deals with Netflix and most other outside publishers keep delivering after the limit is reached. In practice, those guaranteed impressions spend the allowance and push the stopping onto other ad lines in the same campaign, Fire tablet, Kindle and Twitch share of voice ads are not capped at all, and the page's printed formulas for savings mix units.
One counter across publishers
The page, dated "Updated on Nov 19, 2026," sits in the Support Center under Bidding and budget, below a header that reads Amazon Ads Agent, the name Amazon now gives its advertising console. Its contents are listed as five sections: what frequency is, how to manage it, cross-publisher frequency capping, measurement and limitations. The cross-publisher section carries the rule that changes how a multi-publisher streaming campaign behaves.
"Frequency caps apply across multiple publishers and supply sources," according to Amazon. "When your campaign or frequency group targets supply from more than 1 publisher, a single unified counter tracks impressions per user across all sources. This means an impression on 1 publisher counts toward the same cap as an impression on another."
Frequency capping has long existed inside Amazon's buying platform at the level of a line, a campaign or a group of campaigns. What the page spells out is that the counter does not reset at the boundary of a publisher. Under a heading "How it works," it lists four points, quoted here in full:
- "If you set a frequency cap of 5 per week on a campaign targeting Prime Video, Netflix, and Fire TV, all impressions across those publishers count toward the single shared cap."
- "When a user reaches the cap, additional impressions are suppressed regardless of which publisher would serve the next ad."
- "Budget is not reduced. Suppressed impressions are redistributed to reach additional unique users."
- "No new settings, opt-in, or targeting changes are required. Cross-publisher capping activates automatically."
The last point means there is no switch. A campaign or frequency group buying from more than one publisher is counted as one pool from the moment it does so. The page does not say when the behavior began, whether any campaign can be excluded from it, or whether redistributing suppressed impressions to "additional unique users" changes the prices a campaign pays.
Where the cap holds and where it does not
A shared counter is only as strong as the ability to stop the next ad. Here the page draws a line by deal type, under the heading "Enforcement by deal type":
- "Private auction (PA) and preferred deals (PD): Fully enforced. DVA+ suppresses bids once the cap is reached."
- "Programmatic guaranteed (PG) deals: Impressions count toward the frequency cap, but delivery cannot be suppressed on the PG line. PG impressions that consume the cap will initiate enforcement on other non-PG lines in the same campaign or frequency group."
The distinction follows from how the deals work. Private auctions, Amazon's name for what much of the industry calls a private marketplace, and preferred deals leave the buyer free to decline an impression, so DVA+ can simply withhold the bid. Programmatic guaranteed deals reserve a fixed volume at an agreed price, and the page states that delivery on such a line "cannot be suppressed." Those impressions are still counted. Once they use up a user's allowance, the stopping is done elsewhere, on whichever non-guaranteed line in the same campaign or frequency group would serve next.
Amazon's worked example, set in a blue box on the page, makes the mechanics concrete:
"Example: You run a single campaign with both Netflix and Prime Video ad groups and set a campaign-level frequency cap of 5 per week. A user receives 3 impressions on Netflix (PG deal) and 1 on Prime Video. When Prime Video attempts to serve a 5th impression, the cap is enforced and the bid is suppressed. The 3 Netflix PG impressions counted toward the cap but couldn't themselves be suppressed because Netflix PG does not support bid suppression."
The arithmetic in the example is off by one. Three Netflix impressions and one Prime Video impression make four, so under a cap of five per week a fifth impression would still fall within the limit; as printed, the example stops the fifth, which is how a cap of four would behave. The point the example is built to show is unaffected: the Netflix guaranteed impressions consumed most of the allowance, and the suppression landed on Prime Video, Amazon's own service.
The example concerns Netflix programmatic guaranteed buying only. When Netflix inventory became available in Amazon DSP from the fourth quarter of 2025, PPC Land reported that the arrangement covered both programmatic guaranteed and private marketplace buying across 11 countries. Under the page's own deal-type rules, a Netflix purchase made through a private auction would be "fully enforced"; the page does not say so for Netflix specifically.

Two statements on guaranteed deals
The cross-publisher section and the limitations list do not describe guaranteed deals in quite the same way. The enforcement section treats programmatic guaranteed deals as a single class whose delivery "cannot be suppressed on the PG line." The limitations list, lower on the same page, carves out three sources:
"Programmatic Guaranteed (PG) deals besides Google AdX, Amazon Publisher Direct (APD), and Amazon O&O deals: Impressions from PG deals other than these sources count towards the frequency caps, but impression delivery won't stop when frequency caps are met."
Read together, the limitations list implies that guaranteed deals through Google AdX, Amazon Publisher Direct and Amazon's owned and operated properties do stop at the cap, while the enforcement section's general rule does not mention the exception. The limitations wording is consistent with an earlier Amazon announcement. A launch note dated June 7, 2024 introduced frequency groups and frequency caps at the order and line item level for video programmatic guaranteed deals from Google AdX and Amazon Publisher Direct, across 34 markets for self-service Amazon DSP users, and said customers could see whether a given deal supported capping. Today's page adds "Amazon O&O deals" to that list without defining the term.
The Google route has grown since. In August, Amazon DSP gained publisher-hosted creatives for Google Ad Manager programmatic guaranteed deals in 33 markets, an option Amazon said would give access to inventory from broadcasters, news platforms and major publishers.
How frequency is managed, according to the page
The page does not present a click path; it describes the structure of the controls and points to two linked help topics, "set a frequency cap" and "create a frequency group." According to Amazon, the arrangement works as follows.
- Two instruments. "You can set a frequency cap or create a frequency group. (a frequency group is a frequency cap applied to multiple campaigns)."
- Three levels, three intervals. "You can set frequency caps or groups at daily, hourly, or 10-minute intervals at the campaign, ad group, or advertiser account level."
- Grouping by attribute. Amazon suggests "using frequency groups across multiple campaigns with similar attributes (like products from the same business line)."
- A ceiling on account-level groups. "An advertiser account can be in a maximum of 2 Advertiser-level frequency groups."
- Where groups are not recommended. Campaigns whose ad groups "are experiencing delivery issues or have experienced delivery issues in the past," and ad groups "with narrow targeting audiences. For example, geo=NYC and demo= M 21 to 24." The page's preferred alternative is campaigns with broad audiences, "for example, geo=US or demo= Age 18 to 54."
- Cross-publisher counting. Applied automatically whenever a campaign or frequency group buys from more than one publisher.
- Reporting. Results appear in "frequency cap insights," which the page says are available on the ad group, campaign and frequency group pages of an account.
The page illustrates the levels with a toothpaste company. Its table, reproduced below, assigns the frequency cap setting to the advertiser account, ad group and campaign levels, and the frequency group setting to multiple campaigns.
Two details sit awkwardly with the rest of the page. The listed intervals are daily, hourly and 10-minute, yet both worked examples use a cap of five "per week," and the page does not explain how a weekly window is expressed in those units. The table also labels the advertiser account setting a frequency cap, while the limitations list speaks of "Advertiser-level frequency groups."
Three DVA+ pages, three views of frequency
Today's page is the third DVA+ help document in a week to deal with frequency, and each covers a different slice. The general setup guide, updated on November 13, was covered by PPC Land the following day; the streaming TV guide followed on November 17 and was covered by PPC Land the same day.
None of the three documents explains how a frequency target of two to five weekly exposures interacts with a cap, or whether the "per user" counter on today's page also covers caps set at household level.
Measuring what the cap saved
The measurement section introduces three formulas behind the frequency cap insights. They are reproduced here exactly as printed.
The win rate, according to the page, "can be calculated by dividing the number of impressions by total bid requests." The logic of the second formula is plain: DVA+ ignored a number of bid requests because a user had reached the cap, and multiplying them by the campaign's usual win rate estimates how many of those requests would have turned into impressions.
The other two formulas, as printed, produce figures that do not fit the units they describe. A worked example with hypothetical inputs shows the difference; the figures are PPC Land calculations, not Amazon's. Suppose 200,000 bid requests were ignored and the campaign wins 10% of the requests it bids on. Impressions saved and reinvested come to 20,000. With an average impression frequency of four, the printed formula gives an incremental reach of 80,000 unique users, four times the number of extra impressions, which is impossible because each additional user needs at least one impression. Dividing instead of multiplying gives 5,000 users. For budget, a CPM is a price per thousand impressions: at a $30 CPM, the printed formula returns 600,000, while the same 20,000 impressions cost $600.
