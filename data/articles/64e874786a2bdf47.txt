TITLE: Spain's AEPD says marketing chiefs can't double as data protection officers
OUTLET: PPC Land

The Spanish Data Protection Agency (AEPD) today published a 59-page guide on the legal position of data protection officers, setting out how organisations should appoint them, how far they must be shielded from pressure, and which other jobs they should not hold. The head of the marketing department is among the senior posts the agency considers, as a rule, incompatible with the role.
The reasoning is structural rather than personal. A data protection officer, or DPO, advises and supervises; a marketing director decides. According to the guide, the head of marketing's functions "involve taking decisions relating to the determination of the purposes and means of the processing of personal data for, for example, campaigns, product development, customer management, market research, profiling of individuals, etc." Someone in that seat would end up supervising their own choices.
The document is titled "El estatuto del delegado de protección de datos. Orientaciones sobre el marco de actuación, independencia, garantías organizativas y conflictos de intereses" - in English, "The Statute of the Data Protection Officer. Guidance on the framework for action, independence, organisational safeguards and conflicts of interest". The AEPD published it in Spanish. Quotations in this article come from an English translation of that text.
In Short
Spain's data protection authority today published guidance on how organisations should appoint and protect their data protection officers, and which jobs clash with the role, naming the head of marketing among them. It matters to the advertising business because Spanish law already obliges firms that profile people for advertising, commercial prospecting or market research to have a DPO, and the guide now spells out who cannot fill that seat and what counts as pressure on whoever does. The text creates no new obligations, but it shows how the AEPD reads the existing ones, including when a lost budget, a broken reporting line or a profit-linked bonus can compromise a DPO's independence.
Interpretation, not new law
The guide is careful about its own status. Its stated purpose is "to offer interpretive criteria that facilitate the application of the safeguards provided for in Articles 37 to 39" of the General Data Protection Regulation and Articles 34 to 37 of Organic Law 3/2018 of 5 December, Spain's data protection act, known as the LOPDGDD. "Consequently, it neither introduces new obligations nor imposes a single organisational model," the foreword states.
That foreword is signed by Lorenzo Cotino Hueso, President of the AEPD, and Francisco Pérez Bes, the agency's Deputy. It addresses two audiences at once: DPOs themselves, and the controllers, processors and governing bodies that appoint them. "Independence in the performance of this function is an institutional guarantee whose effectiveness it is for each organisation to ensure," the text reads.
The legal footing is drawn widely. The guide relies on three judgments of the Court of Justice of the European Union - Leistritz (C-534/20, 22 June 2022), X-FAB Dresden (C-453/21, 9 February 2023) and KISA (C-560/21, 9 February 2023) - and on a Spanish Audiencia Nacional judgment of 18 March 2026. It also examines material from the European Data Protection Board, the European Data Protection Supervisor and supervisory authorities in France, Belgium, Italy, the Netherlands, Ireland, Finland, Denmark and Sweden, as well as ISO 37009:2025, the International Organization for Standardization's September 2025 standard on conflicts of interest in organisations.
The trigger for much of this work sits in Brussels. The EDPB's coordinated enforcement action on the designation and position of DPOs, whose report was adopted on 16 January 2024, stressed "conflicts of interest and the lack of independence of the DPO", according to the guide. In its press release, the AEPD said it took part in that action and that the report also identified budgetary dependence and difficult access to senior management.
Who must appoint a DPO in Spain
Article 37 of the GDPR makes a DPO compulsory for public bodies (courts acting in their judicial capacity excepted), for organisations whose core activities involve large-scale processing of sensitive data or criminal records, and for those that carry out "regular and systematic monitoring of individuals on a large scale". The guide gives two examples of the latter: video surveillance and "user profiling on social networks".
Spain goes further. Article 34(1) of the LOPDGDD, reproduced in full in the guide, names 16 categories of entity that must designate a DPO in any event. Two of them sit squarely inside the advertising business. Point (d) covers "information society service providers when they build profiles of service users on a large scale". Point (k) covers "entities carrying out advertising and commercial prospecting activities, including commercial and market research, when they carry out processing based on the preferences of data subjects or engage in activities involving the profiling of data subjects."
For a business in advertising, commercial prospecting or market research, preference-based processing or profiling is therefore enough on its own to make a DPO mandatory under Spanish law. Unlike points (c) and (d), point (k) carries no large-scale qualifier, and it applies independently of the GDPR's large-scale monitoring test.
Source: Article 34(1) LOPDGDD, as reproduced in the AEPD guide. Organisations outside the list may appoint a DPO voluntarily under Article 34(2), and are then subject to the same regime.
Article 34(3) sets a short clock. Controllers and processors must notify the AEPD, or the relevant regional authority, "within ten days, of the designations, appointments and dismissals of data protection officers, both where designation is mandatory and where it is voluntary." The guide reads that obligation as covering terminations too, warning that a failure to update the register leaves "the former DPO as the visible interlocutor with the supervisory authority, data subjects or third parties despite their relationship with the organisation having effectively ended."
How designation works, according to the guide
According to the AEPD, the designation process "must be formal, documented and transparent", whether the DPO is a single person or a collegiate body. The guide sets out seven phases.
- Identify the obligation. The organisation assesses whether Article 37 GDPR or Article 34 LOPDGDD applies to it.
- Define the profile. The guide links the profile to size (a part-time external DPO "may suffice" in an SME; a large company may need an internal team), complexity of processing, sector and knowledge of the organisation.
- Assess suitability. The suggested parameters cover knowledge and experience, independence, availability, resources and certifications. One of the independence questions asks whether any other team or unit handles data protection matters that do not belong to the DPO.
- Formalise the designation. The governing body approves the appointment. In the private sector, according to the guide, that means the board of directors, chief executive, general manager or a person empowered by special power of attorney. The written designation records the DPO's name and contact details, functions under Article 39 GDPR, scope, duration if temporary, resources (budget, team, tools) and "guarantees of independence". The organisation then communicates it to the AEPD, to employees and to data subjects, and publishes the contact details as Article 37(7) GDPR requires.
- Apply the Article 39 tasks. These are informing and advising, monitoring compliance, advising on and monitoring the data protection impact assessment, cooperating with the AEPD, and acting as contact point for supervisors and data subjects. The guide warns against the reading that designation makes privacy the DPO's exclusive responsibility: "Under no circumstances can the formal designation of a DPO be understood as a transfer of the responsibility for regulatory compliance that the GDPR and the LOPDGDD assign to the controller or processor."
- Plan for termination. Notifications cover dismissals as well as appointments, and the guide suggests a collegiate "privacy office" as a way to keep the function running through absences.
- Follow good practice. The EDPB and the AEPD list a high administrative level (levels 28 to 30 in Spain's General State Administration), a "Letter of Mission (Carta de Misión)" for internal DPOs or a clear contract for external ones, a place on the organisation chart, a data protection office or committee for continuity, a conflict-of-interest check before appointment, designation by the board or general management, direct reporting to senior management, no operational functions that may create conflicts, and sufficient budget, tools and team.
The guide anchors the last phase in case law. Audiencia Nacional Judgment 139/2026 of 18 March 2026 (ECLI:ES:AN:2026:1355), according to the AEPD, "confirms the need to examine the functions actually assigned to the person designated, especially when they take part in decisions that determine the purposes and means of the processing they must subsequently supervise". The judgment upheld an AEPD sanction of 19 July 2023 over the appointment of the secretary of a professional association as its DPO.
Independence, in five parts
Article 38(3) of the GDPR is the core text. It provides that the controller and processor "shall ensure that the data protection officer does not receive any instructions regarding the exercise of those tasks. He or she shall not be dismissed or penalised by the controller or the processor for performing his tasks." The guide breaks that guarantee into five parts.
Functional independence
A DPO cannot be told what conclusion to reach. "For example, if the DPO identifies and assesses a risk in a data processing operation, they cannot be pressured to minimise it, ignore it, avoid it or reconsider their assessment." Reports, recommendations and alerts must not be "modified or censored by the organisation".
Hierarchical independence
The DPO reports to the highest level - in the private sector, to general management or the board. Where an organisation has separate administrative and management bodies, the guide recommends reporting to both.
Conflicts of interest
The guide cites ISO 37009:2025 and asks controllers to "draw up internal rules that allow possible conflicts of interest to be identified and properly managed", ideally before the DPO is appointed. Its two headline examples are being head of a department that processes personal data, such as marketing, HR or IT, and taking "the final decision on the implementation and configuration of a video surveillance system".
Economic independence
This section matters for anyone who designs pay structures. "The DPO must have adequate remuneration and stability in the post to avoid economic pressure," the guide states. It then adds: "A bonus or variable remuneration linked to financial results or business profit could also affect their independence, as it could influence their objectivity." A DPO who was the majority shareholder of a company supplying data processing services to the organisation would face the same problem, according to the AEPD.
Organisational independence
The DPO needs "material access to all the data, processes and systems necessary", including records of processing, impact assessments and processor contracts, plus staff, tools and a budget for training and outside advice. Early consultation is part of it: the guide's example is that DPOs "must contribute their advice at the design stage of a new AI system that processes personal data." The DPO must also have access to the organisation's whistleblowing channel to report pressure on their own independence.
