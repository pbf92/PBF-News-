TITLE: Therapeutic play for children undergoing invasive medical procedures: one label, three functions
OUTLET: Frontiers

Abstract
Therapeutic play is widely used to help children through invasive medical procedures, but syntheses disagree about its effects. Most favor play; they differ chiefly in certainty, which tracks appraisal stringency, and in which outcomes improve, while effect sizes vary by rater. We propose that part of the outcome-level inconsistency arises because the label bundles at least three functions, defined by the mechanisms they target: attentional play, which competes with pain for attention during a procedure; preparatory play, which builds accurate expectations and coping confidence beforehand; and reflective play, which helps children process the experience and revise its memory afterwards. Adults delivering or accompanying play form a relational channel that may moderate and mediate all three. On mostly low-certainty evidence, distraction, much of it not play-based, reduces needle pain; preparation most consistently reduces observed distress and improves cooperation; and memory reframing can change memories but has inconsistent effects on later distress, while play-based reflection is barely tested. We relate the functions to instructional and dramatic therapeutic play and playful distraction, specify what the framework adds (measurable mediators, function-matched outcomes, falsifiable predictions), and examine competing schools of thought (directive medical play versus child-centered play therapy, distraction versus therapy, technology as substitute or amplifier). Six gaps follow, each paired with a direction: measure mechanisms; match outcomes to function and follow children to the next procedure; test developmental fit; trial play-based reflection; count trials, not papers, in syntheses; and report who delivers play, with what training and fidelity.
1 Introduction
Venipuncture (blood drawing), vaccination, swabbing, and surgery under anesthesia are routine in childhood and often feared. After surgery, 54% of 163 children aged 2–10 years showed new negative behaviors at 2 weeks, although only 7.3% still did at 1 year (Kain et al., 1996), and parents rated the postoperative pain of anxious children as higher (Kain et al., 2006). Most children fear needles (McLenon and Rogers, 2019), and 8% of children in one survey named needle fear as their main reason for missing vaccinations (Taddio et al., 2012).
Therapeutic play is a standard response. Pediatric nursing distinguishes three forms of therapeutic play (emotional outlet or dramatic, instructional, and physiologically enhancing) from normative play (Vessey and Mahon, 1990), and child life specialists, hospital professionals trained in child development, use play alongside psychological preparation and expressive modalities (Romito et al., 2021). The literature has grown quickly, and the label now covers many activities, including distraction (Gjærde et al., 2021). Syntheses range from favorable (Halemani et al., 2022; Goren et al., 2023) to inconclusive or questionable (He et al., 2015a; Silva et al., 2017).
We propose that part of this inconsistency, in which outcomes improve, for which procedures and in whom, arises because the label bundles functions with different mechanisms (Table 1). Unlike typologies based on form, ours defines each function by the mediator it targets, treats timing, format, and deliverer as separate dimensions, and yields predictions that the question “does therapeutic play work?” cannot test. We then re-read recent syntheses (Table 2) and derive gaps and directions.

2 A mechanism-based framework
We use therapeutic play broadly for play-based activities aimed at a medical procedure, whoever delivers them, and flag evidence borrowed from non-play interventions. We reserve play therapy for psychotherapy, although some trials use the term for preparation or distraction. Normative play, which supports development rather than a procedure, is outside our scope. An intervention’s function is the mediator it is intended to change; whether it changes it for a given child must be measured, because the same doll demonstration may inform one child and frighten another. Coping theory suggests why functions should differ by phase: problem-focused coping suits stressors appraised as controllable, and emotion-focused coping, such as distraction, those appraised as uncontrollable (Forsythe and Compas, 1987; Rudolph et al., 1995). Children can act on information before a procedure, but little is controllable once the needle is in.
2.1 Attentional play
Attentional play targets attention. Pain demands attention, and an engaging activity can compete for it, most effectively when pain is mild and its threat value low (McCaul and Malott, 1984; Eccleston and Crombez, 1999). Most supporting evidence concerns distraction in general. It reduced self-reported needle pain in a Cochrane review [standardized mean difference (SMD) − 0.56; low to very low certainty; Birnie et al., 2018], and toys, games, and virtual reality (VR) reduced vaccination distress on low- to very-low-certainty evidence (McMurtry et al., 2026). Across 13 trials, active beat passive distraction except on self-reported pain (Shen et al., 2023). More interaction is not always better: during venipuncture, a passive movie outperformed an interactive toy, perhaps because distress prevented interaction (MacLaren and Cohen, 2005). Bubble-blowing did not reduce pain or anxiety relative to video-watching in 2- to 5-year-olds (Postles et al., 2025). What play itself adds to distraction is therefore unproven, although breathing with a toy, but not without one, reduced vaccination pain (Birnie et al., 2015).
2.2 Preparatory play
Preparatory (instructional) play targets expectations and coping confidence through information, modeling and rehearsal. It draws on filmed modeling (Melamed and Siegel, 1975), self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1977) and coping-skills training (Jay et al., 1987), but control does not always reduce distress (Thompson, 1981). In a small trial, 3- and 4-year-olds without previous hospital experience who played with hospital-related materials were more cooperative and less upset than those who played with unrelated materials, suggesting that procedural content carried the benefit (Schwartz et al., 1983). Recent single-center trials of instructional play report less emotional distress during swabbing (Şengül and Erkut, 2025) and lower anxiety and fear around catheter insertion (Taşpinar et al., 2026). Educating vaccine recipients reduced fear and distress on low- to very-low-certainty evidence (Pillai Riddell et al., 2015b; Taddio et al., 2026). In a broader Cochrane review, by contrast, preparation or information showed no effect on needle pain or distress in four very-low-certainty trials (Birnie et al., 2018). Before surgery, a family-centered behavioral program did not differ significantly from oral midazolam, a sedative premedication, in anxiety at induction (Kain et al., 2007), and tailored web-based preparation reduced anxiety (Fortier et al., 2015).
2.3 Reflective play
Reflective play targets an experience already lived and bundles two candidate mechanisms that need separate tests. The first is emotional expression and mastery through child-led symbolic play, emphasized by emotional-outlet and child-centered traditions (Vessey and Mahon, 1990; Landreth, 2023); the second is revision of its memory, which then shapes expectations, a mechanism we derive from pain-memory research (Noel et al., 2012b). Children’s memories of pain predicted later pain better than their original reports in a laboratory study (Noel et al., 2012a), and exaggerated memories of lumbar punctures predicted distress at the next one (Chen et al., 2000). Interventions that target memory are verbal rather than play-based, and their downstream effects are inconsistent. Memory reframing reduced anticipatory and later procedural distress across repeated lumbar punctures (Chen et al., 1999) and made memories of postoperative pain more accurate or positive (Pavlova et al., 2022). However, a meta-analysis found that changed memories did not clearly reduce later fear or distress (Noel et al., 2018), and a vaccine trial found no effect on recalled or future pain (Pavlova et al., 2023). Controlled trials of play after procedures are rare and have not measured memory (Metlek and Çağlar, 2024), and repetitive re-enactment can fail to relieve distress (Terr, 1981).
2.4 The relational channel
We call the behavior and emotional state of the adults who deliver or accompany play the relational channel, and hypothesize that parental anxiety moderates, and adult talk mediates, each function’s effects. Evidence on adult talk is partly experimental. Mothers randomized to distract rather than reassure reduced their children’s distress during injections (Gonzalez et al., 1993), coaching nurses and parents to direct children’s attention increased child coping (Cohen et al., 1997), and children read parental reassurance as fear (McMurtry et al., 2010). At anesthesia induction, adult empathy and reassurance were associated with child distress and distracting talk with coping (Chorney et al., 2009). Across needle procedures, combinations of parental coping-promoting and distress-promoting behaviors most consistently predicted children’s coping and poorer outcomes, respectively (Campbell et al., 2017). Whether parental presence helps is contested. A Cochrane review found no effect on very-low-certainty evidence (Manyande et al., 2015), whereas later syntheses found benefits relative to no presence (López-Rodrigo et al., 2025; Alalikhan et al., 2026); the difference may reflect included trials and outcome metrics as well as what parents did. In older infants, structured parent involvement had little or no effect (low to moderate certainty; Pillai Riddell et al., 2023).
