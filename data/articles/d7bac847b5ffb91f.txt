Anthropic Mathematician Says He and Claude Cracked the Riemann Hypothesis; Proof Released Only as Lean Code, Pending Verification
Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, publicly claimed this week that he and the company's large model Claude have found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, a problem that has stood open for 167 years. If the academic community ultimately confirms the claim, it would mark the first time artificial intelligence has achieved a substantive breakthrough on one of the most central problems in pure mathematics. But as of now, no third party has publicly confirmed that the formalized code actually proves the Riemann Hypothesis.
According to a statement released by Alpöge, he believes not only that he discovered a path to a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis with Claude's help, but also that the same approach extends directly to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) — and he expects it can eventually be pushed to the Extended Riemann Hypothesis (ERH) as well. According to his statement, the arguments for both the Riemann Hypothesis and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis have been formalized in the Lean theorem prover without introducing any additional assumptions.
The Riemann Hypothesis was proposed by the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in 1859. At its core, it states that all non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function lie on the critical line where the real part equals 1/2. The conjecture is regarded as the "North Star" of number theory: if it holds, humanity's estimate of how prime numbers are distributed along the number line would reach optimal precision. The Clay Mathematics Institute has noted that the conjecture has been verified for the first 10 trillion zeros, but proving it holds universally for all zeros would unlock many mysteries of prime distribution. Precisely because of its extreme difficulty, many mathematicians have long been reluctant even to take it up as a research direction.

The key step: simulating "multiplicity" with a chain of theta lifts
Alpöge laid out the technical route in detail in his statement. In the function-field case, the mathematician Stepanov, in his proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, used divisors with "multiplicity." But back in the integer case, directly finding the corresponding multiplicity is extremely difficult — tantamount to taking a derivative of an integer.
The approach he and Claude took was to string theta lifts into a chain, using each lift to mimic one unit of multiplicity. When handling the von Mangoldt weighted sum of length X, Claude proposed taking the length of this chain to be √X. Alpöge described that single suggestion as enough to make colleagues fall off their chairs. He acknowledged that for the sections on automorphic forms on higher-rank groups, Claude is far more adept than he is, and that the timeline of progress in the statement is his retelling of Claude's summary — a retelling he admits may be "completely incoherent," with the Lean code to be treated as authoritative.

250,000-page manuscript withheld; only Lean code released
Claude's detailed proof draft runs to more than 250,000 pages, and no conventional, human-readable paper has yet been written. Alpöge argued that posting an ultra-long document no one could finish reading would be inappropriate, and that he plans to write it up formally only after he has thoroughly absorbed and greatly simplified it. For now, only the Lean formalization code has been released, split into 14 compressed volumes.

Alpöge also said he intuitively expects the current proof to be greatly simplified "possibly very soon," and predicted that someone will find a much shorter proof before long.
Notably, the news came in the same week that Anthropic went public on Nasdaq. Alpöge raised the timing question himself in the statement, saying "there is an obvious cynical reading, related to timing, but it is wrong." He disclosed that before publishing he messaged Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei to confirm whether he could make it public, and said there was one constraint he had to observe. The acknowledgments list mathematicians including Manjul Bhargava, Peter Sarnak and Maksym Radziwiłł.
Academic reaction and verification risks
Reactions from the academic community began emerging the same day the statement was released. MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland said he had independently compiled the Lean code Alpöge released. "This really is a historic moment," he said. "The world has changed... I know the mathematical community will have all sorts of reactions, but for me this is a triumph for humanity, a day worth celebrating." He stressed, however, that "there is still a great deal of work to do" to understand the mathematical details of the proof.
Hector Pasten, a mathematician at the Pontifical Catholic University of Chile, assessed it from the standpoint of the discipline's structure: "If this proof is correct and understood, it would complete a dictionary of correspondences between different branches of mathematics that until now was only partial. It would be like a Rosetta Stone." Carlo Pagano, a professor at Concordia University who is also a visiting researcher at Google DeepMind, said that even from a preliminary scan of the argument's summary, he believed "the new ideas in the proof are very likely to open up entirely new ways of thinking in number theory."
But the manner of publication — releasing only formalized code without a human-readable paper — lands squarely on a fault line that has been debated in the academic community in recent months. On Sept. 11, 25 Fields Medalists — including Terence Tao — jointly issued a declaration titled "Serious Misalignment of AI in Mathematics," warning that AI companies treating the solving of hard mathematical problems as a benchmark contest is harming the discipline, and calling for a slower pace and a return to a writing tradition that peers can understand.
Alpöge's academic background lends the statement some credibility. Born in 1992 and holding dual U.S. and Turkish citizenship, he earned his undergraduate degree in mathematics from Harvard, graduating with highest honors and winning the Morgan Prize, the top U.S. undergraduate award for mathematical research. He received his PhD from Princeton University in 2020 under Fields Medalist Manjul Bhargava, and from 2021 to 2025 was a junior fellow in the Harvard Society of Fellows. His representative work includes proving that the average number of integral points on elliptic curves is bounded, and, with Bhargava and Shnidman, studying non-monogenic generation problems for cubic and quartic fields of positive density.
He once wrote on his personal homepage that after GPT-4 appeared he considered it the most interesting thing humans had built, and so switched toward computing. He now works at Anthropic.
The statement itself, however, leaves room. Alpöge acknowledged that it will take time to confirm whether Claude really helped crack the Riemann Hypothesis, and said the discovery left him with a sense of loss: "It is natural to feel that this is an endpoint, that for me a major guiding light has gone out, but the vistas I saw along the way left me so astonished that I believe this is the scientific dawn of something greater." He added: "In an era of mathematical revolution, I hope this argument lets people glimpse a corner of the wonders ahead."
This is not the first time Anthropic has had Claude take on the Riemann Hypothesis. In August, the company disclosed an internal study in which an unreleased Claude research model did not prove the Riemann Hypothesis but raised the known lower bound on the proportion of non-trivial zeros lying on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%.
Nor is this the first time AI has been linked to a breakthrough in a major scientific field. Earlier this year, OpenAI helped produce a breakthrough approach to the Navier-Stokes equations, but the finding was subsequently overshadowed by plagiarism allegations.
Implications for markets and industry
If the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is ultimately confirmed by the academic community, its impact would extend far beyond pure mathematics. The Riemann Hypothesis is one of the theoretical cornerstones of modern cryptography and number-theoretic algorithms, and a precise characterization of prime distribution is of fundamental importance to assessing the security of the RSA encryption system. The proof itself, however, would not yield an efficient algorithm for factoring large integers, and so would not immediately undermine the practical foundations of current RSA encryption — what it delivers is a precise error bound on prime distribution, not a ready-made decryption key. At the same time, it would be the first time a large model has produced a complete, formally verifiable argument on a top-tier mathematical problem, potentially reshaping how the outside world prices AI's scientific research capabilities.
For Anthropic, the statement coincides with its IPO window, helping in the short term to reinforce its "frontier models plus scientific discovery" narrative, but it also carries reputational risk: until the Lean code is independently checked, any exaggerated interpretation could backfire. By academic convention, formalized code must undergo line-by-line peer review, a process that could take months or even years. Until then, the validity of the proof remains undetermined.
