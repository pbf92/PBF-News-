JPMorgan Chase & Co. is a financial holding company, which engages in the provision of financial and investment banking services. The firm focuses on investment banking, financial services for consumers and small businesses, commercial banking, financial transaction processing, and asset management. It operates through the following segments: Consumer and Community Banking (CCB), Commercial and Investment Bank (CIB), Asset and Wealth Management (AWM), and Corporate. The CCB segment originates and services mortgage loans. The CIB segment makes markets and services clients across fixed income, foreign exchange, equities, and commodities. The AWM segment provides initial capital investments in products such as mutual funds and capital invested alongside third-party investors. The Corporate segment manages its liquidity, funding, capital, structural interest rate, and foreign exchange risks. The company was founded in 1799 and is headquartered in New York, NY.

Companies issue bonds to raise capital without giving up ownership, as they would by selling shares. By issuing bonds, a company borrows money and agrees to pay interest (coupons) and repay the principal at a fixed maturity date.
Businesses use bond financing to:
– Fund growth (expansion, acquisitions, new projects)
– Refinance existing debt at better rates
– Manage cash flow without issuing new shares
Compared to stocks, bonds allow JPMorgan Chase & Co. to access large amounts of capital while maintaining full control of the business.
Bonds and stocks are two ways to invest in JPMorgan Chase & Co., but they work very differently.
Here are the key differences:
– Ownership vs. lending: Stocks represent ownership in JPMorgan Chase & Co., while bonds are a loan you give to the company
– Returns: Stocks may offer dividends and capital gains, while bonds pay fixed interest (coupon) and return principal at maturity
– Risk: Stocks are generally more volatile and can lose value, whereas bonds are considered lower risk
– Priority: Bondholders are paid before shareholders if the company goes bankrupt

A bond rating is an assessment of an issuer's creditworthiness — its ability to repay debt and make interest payments on time.
Ratings are typically assigned by credit rating agencies (e.g., S&P, Fitch) based on factors such as financial strength, debt levels, and economic conditions.
Common rating scale:
– Investment grade: AAA to BBB (lower risk)
– Speculative (high-yield): BB and below (higher risk, higher potential return)
Ratings help investors evaluate default risk and can influence bond yields — lower ratings usually mean higher yields.
