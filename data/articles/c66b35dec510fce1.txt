TITLE: ICE detention of Princeton scientist rattles academia
OUTLET: STAT News

A graduate student who is a scientific researcher at Princeton University was detained by Immigration and Customs Enforcement earlier this week while traveling home after presenting research at the annual Society for Neuroscience conference in Washington D.C.
The researcher, Eva Yi Xie, was detained Thursday. Although she has lived in the U.S. since 2016 after moving to the country from China, her visa had expired, according to the New York Times. Her lawyers told a reporter Xie had a pending asylum application and was thus allowed to remain in the country legally.
The news, first posted by a mentor of Xie’s on X, ricocheted across the academic circles on Friday, in part because it seemed to crystalize pervasive fears of non-U.S. academics and international students that traveling to conferences, to present new research and network with colleagues, may be too risky under a second Trump administration that has cracked down on immigration.
“There’s, I think, an open question about whether or not it’s safe for people who are international students visa to travel, because there have been these detentions, and that’s a very scary prospect,” said Caitlin Rivers, who similarly had a staff member of hers detained by ICE earlier this year. A number of conferences have reported smaller attendance at conferences in the U.S. over the past two years, and some have considered the prospect of hosting in other countries.
ICE did not respond to a request for comment as to why Xie was detained. But the administration has been vocal in its desire to reduce the number of foreign-born graduate students in the U.S. In its science blueprint released earlier this year, the White House wrote “America’s over-reliance on foreign students also creates a security challenge.”
Xie is not the first graduate student to be detained, either. Most notably, Tufts researcher Rumeysa Ozturk was detained by ICE in Cambridge, Mass., and later decided to leave the country. A report released earlier this week found that international graduate student enrollment is down by a third at academic medical centers.
“News of Eva’s detention is distressing for me and many members of the Princeton University community,” Rodney D. Priestley, the dean of Princeton University’s graduate school wrote in a statement, adding the university has provided Xie with legal representation. “We hope Eva will soon be able to resume her studies at Princeton.”
Xie’s work lies at the intersection of neuroscience and artificial intelligence; she uses a combination of experiments on mice and large datasets of neural recordings to understand how different portions of the brain work together to make decisions. While only a third-year PhD student, she is viewed as a rising star by some of her colleagues.
“I think it speaks to how exceptional of a researcher Eva is that she can work on projects that really span multiple disciplines, that require that she go and learn the foundations, not just of AI and machine learning, and not just computational models of the brain, but also understanding how do you run the experiment on the mice. She’s in the lab actually doing the surgery on the mice, gathering the data from the mice, analyzing those data, and running the AI experiments,” said Benjamin Eysenbach, a computer scientist at Princeton and one of Xie’s three advisers.
As an example of the respect she commands among peers, he cited a workshop she convened at last year’s NeurIPS conference, one of the premiere machine learning conferences. Not only did she present research of her own, but she brought together leaders in AI and in neuroscience to see how the two could collaborate.
It was an opportunity both to “introduce these important questions about the brain to folks in AI that might be equipped with new methods, and to help folks working on neuroscience questions define what are the right AI tools,” Eysenbach said.
Krithik Ramesh, a fellow researcher, is among those who has benefitted from her guidance. The two had overlapped at science fair competitions throughout high school, and when he was considering transferring to the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where Xie had completed her undergraduate degree, he turned to her for counsel. He learned about Xie’s detainment after a friend saw a post on social media. His immediate reaction was disbelief, followed by a sense of unmooring as a fellow researcher.
“We academics exist for the benefit of the public,” he said. “We are very privileged as a country to have people like Eva continue to pursue academia at the highest level, and to share that with other people. That’s literally the nature and reason why we go to conferences to present stuff. It is appalling to me that she cannot do that with reasonable certainty that she can’t be detained.”
