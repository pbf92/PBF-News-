Mythos changes the clock: What Anthropic’s cyber-capable AI models mean for OT, critical infrastructure security
Anthropic’s cyber-capable artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities are reshaping the speed and cost of finding and fixing software vulnerabilities across critical organizations. Claude Mythos Preview, announced in April 2026, proved so adept at exploiting vulnerabilities that access was limited to vetted organizations under Project Glasswing. That circle has since grown to 150 organizations across 15 countries, though not smoothly. Mythos 5 and Fable 5 launched in June, were suspended within days under U.S. export controls, but returned on July 1. In October, the Critical Infrastructure Defense Program launched, putting expertise and technical knowledge from Dragos, Nozomi Networks and Rockwell Automation behind the effort.
Across operational technology (OT) and industrial control systems (ICS) environments, the implications run beyond faster scanning, detection and remediation. Project Glasswing accelerated the rate at which organizations found vulnerabilities in their systems significantly. Between April and July 2026, partners discovered at least 129,000 known software vulnerabilities, and Anthropic’s own open-source scanning identified another 5,500 between April and October. More than 33,000 of the verified flaws have been flagged as critical or high severity, though the tally is an undercount as it uses survey data from a subset of Glasswing partners. The actual impact is likely at least five times higher.
Clearly, finding bugs or security loopholes became cheap; verifying and fixing them stayed slow and manual. In OT, that gap is the whole story.
Over the years, obscure firmware and scarce reverse engineers served as an accidental moat. But the emergence of AI tools that can discover and exploit software vulnerabilities at unprecedented speed should therefore be a considerable concern for industrial asset owners and technology suppliers. It has been argued that if AI substantially accelerates vulnerability discovery, asset owners may face an increasing number of vulnerabilities that require attention without the ability to address and appropriately patch them. Thus, making compensating controls especially important.
Data from TXOne’s 2026 report revealed that 96% of OT incidents begin in IT-level compromises, either through direct penetration (56%) or collateral damage (40%). While 90% of organizations perform frequent security updates, only 24% achieve asset coverage exceeding 90%. The report also cited compatibility issues and limited asset visibility as primary technical barriers to comprehensive patching. Dragos’ 2025 data identified that only 3% of vulnerabilities needed immediate action, 71% suited planned mitigation, 4% were exploited, and 25% of advisories had neither patch nor mitigation. Triage by physical consequence, not raw CVSS, starting with internet-exposed gateways.
Small utilities cannot staff frontier AI engineering as they lack the expertise that these models call for. Hence, these firms end up borrowing capacity instead of building it up, through trusted OT vendors and also through a bigger Cyber Verification Program.
When it comes to firmware, organizations remain traditional, treating AI output as an unverified lead. This comes amid warnings of false positives, and even Anthropic’s scanner expects over 90% true positives without human review.
ISA/IEC 62443 industrial cybersecurity standards already cover this ground. Anthropic expects defense to win within two years; however, for OT/ICS installations, the next three months can be productively spent to rework consequence assessments, inventory unsupported assets, rehearse manual recovery, and press vendors for realistic patch timelines.
Mythos & Fable: What actually matters for OT?
Industrial Cyber reached out to industrial cybersecurity experts to examine which of the disclosed capabilities of Mythos and Fable matter most for OT and ICS security, and which claims still require independent validation, particularly in industrial environments.
Clint Bodungen, director of AI/ML engineering at Arcova, recognized that Mythos (and in many cases Fable) changes the cost, expertise, and level of effort needed to find and exploit software vulnerabilities dropped significantly. “Anthropic claims Mythos found thousands of high-severity vulnerabilities, including some in every major operating system and web browser, and that for the cases it has published, the model found nearly all of them on its own and developed exploits for many. However, this is a bit over-hyped for the most part. What you won’t hear the media mention (because it doesn’t make for a good story) is that most of the vulnerabilities discovered would be considered low likelihood and/or low impact in the grand scheme of things. There is a difference between a high-severity CVSS score and that which has an actual high impact or likelihood in your specific environment.”
Bodungen added that several aspects of Anthropic’s disclosures still require independent validation, particularly their applicability to industrial environments. The vulnerability counts and autonomy claims are based on Anthropic’s own reporting, while many technical details remain undisclosed until fixes are available. Much of the independent testing conducted so far has measured autonomous attack chaining rather than validating the reported vulnerability findings. Statements from partner organizations are also presented on Anthropic’s own website, underscoring the need for independent assessments.
Another area requiring validation is the models’ ability to assess proprietary firmware and binaries. Most published results focus on source code and open-source projects, whereas much of the OT environment relies on closed firmware.
He mentioned to Industrial Cyber that Anthropic’s Fable 5.1 model, released in September and made generally available, can identify vulnerabilities but cannot build exploits. It relies on Anthropic’s Opus models for exploit generation and binary vulnerability scanning. In July, Anthropic also identified assessments involving ICS, SCADA, DCS, PLCs, RTUs, HMIs and safety instrumented systems, as well as OT protocol abuse involving Modbus, DNP3, OPC and IEC 61850, as high-risk activities blocked by Fable 5 safeguards. The company has not clarified whether Fable 5.1 changed those restrictions. Meanwhile, the more permissive Mythos 5.1 remains limited to vetted organizations, leaving few external researchers able to test these models against real industrial targets.
Bodungen also highlighted the gap between identifying software vulnerabilities and understanding their consequences for physical processes. Finding a vulnerability in a PLC’s web server does not necessarily demonstrate an ability to manipulate an industrial process or create unsafe operating conditions. Similarly, testing in a simulated environment does not replicate the complexity of an operational facility, where safety systems, interlocks and physical processes determine how an attack could affect operations.
“The biggest development is that AI models like Mythos and Fable are demonstrating the ability to find vulnerabilities and develop working exploits with far less time and expertise than was previously required,” Joseph Saunders, CEO of RunSafe Security, told Industrial Cyber. “That fundamentally changes the economics of cybersecurity. We’ve built security programs around identifying vulnerabilities, assessing their severity, and fixing them before they’re exploited. But as AI accelerates discovery and exploit development, the time available to respond becomes much shorter.”
He added, “We still need independent testing, but we don’t need to wait for those results to recognize that our current approach to protecting long-lived critical infrastructure is not going to scale.”
Cam Cullen, CMO at BlastWave, told Industrial Cyber that the capability to dissect open-source protocols and software is critical. “Because most industrial systems rely on this software, discovered vulnerabilities can be weaponized across multiple OT systems. Myhos will not be able to access the source code of these systems because the software is owned by proprietary vendors.”
AI and shifting barriers to ICS exploitation
The executives assess how specialized equipment, heterogeneous industrial environments, and the cost of exploit development have constrained attacks on industrial systems. They also consider how AI could lower these barriers and which assets and access paths are most likely to be affected.
Bodungen recognized that industrial equipment is too specialized, with security by obscurity being a common mantra for years, and debated for years. “Whether it holds weight, though, is almost a moot point, because so far, most attacks haven’t had to rely much on vulnerabilities in the actual industrial equipment. Most serious industrial incidents, such as the 2015 and 2016 attacks on Ukraine’s grid, relied on getting into the IT side, stealing credentials, abusing remote access and using legitimate engineering functions, not on exotic PLC zero-days. Stuxnet and TRITON are the well-known exceptions, with custom controller-level payloads, and both still started on the shared Windows layer.”
He noted that what specialized equipment and diversity did do was raise the cost of the last mile. A reliable attack on a specific control system usually needed the actual hardware or a faithful copy, knowledge of the vendor’s protocols and engineering software (not to mention the unique specifics of how each system and process works in that environment), and time. That kept the top end of OT attacks to a small number of well-funded groups. “The practical effect is that the middle tier of attackers gets more capable. Defenders should plan for more groups being able to do what only a few could do before, and for a much shorter time between a vulnerability being disclosed and being exploited.”
Industrial systems have historically presented unique challenges for attackers, requiring specialized expertise and significant time to understand and exploit, Saunders said. “Developing attacks was more expensive and time-consuming than it was worth. Complexity is no longer a shield. AI is making it easier and cheaper to analyze unfamiliar software and develop exploits, lowering barriers that previously made industrial systems difficult to attack. I’m particularly concerned about software connecting industrial operations to the outside world, including remote access systems, engineering workstations, and network-connected devices.”
He added that when an attacker has a path in, the time and expertise needed to exploit the software may be significantly reduced.
“In the past, these systems were disconnected and highly customized, so they relied on obscurity for security,” according to Cullen. “Now, many use common software modules and operating systems, making it easier for AI to discover exploitable vectors that don’t always rely on passwords or credential theft.”
Prioritizing OT flaws when patches can’t wait
When vulnerability discovery accelerates, but patches are unavailable or cannot be deployed promptly, the executives look into how operators should prioritize findings and choose compensating controls.
“OT has lived in this dichotomy for a long time,” Bodungen said. “Patching in a plant waits for maintenance windows and vendor certification, and some legacy equipment can’t be patched at all, due to how old it is or potential consequences that outweigh the mitigation. Faster discovery just ends up making the backlog bigger. So, prioritization is paramount.”
