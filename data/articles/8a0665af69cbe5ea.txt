Study Links Growing Wealth Inequality to Higher Death Rates
Five Decades of Data Reveal Particularly Strong Association Among Working-Age Women and Deaths Involving Suicide and Drugs
A new national study suggests that the growing concentration of wealth among relatively few households may have consequences for public health. Examining five decades of data, researchers found that as household wealth became more unequally distributed across regional economies, death rates among working-age adults in those regions tended to be higher than expected, even after accounting for poverty, income inequality, and other economic conditions.
The association was particularly strong among women and for deaths involving suicide and drugs. The researchers could not determine whether wealth inequality itself contributed to those deaths or whether other social and economic conditions associated with inequality were responsible.
Titled “Local Wealth Inequality and Working-Age Mortality in US Counties,” the study was published in the October issue of JAMA Health Forum.
In an LDI Q&A about the paper, co-authors LDI Senior Fellow and University of Pennsylvania Professor Atheendar Venkataramani, MD, PhD, and Manuel Schechtl, PhD, Assistant Professor of Public Policy at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, explained the details and implications of the work.
What did your study discover?
We followed nearly every U.S. county for five decades and asked a simple question: When local wealth inequality in a region grew, what happened to death rates among working-age adults in that region? The answer was that mortality rose. A county whose local labor market experienced the average increase in wealth inequality we observed between 1970 and 2020 had roughly 12% higher mortality among women ages 25 to 44 than it otherwise would have. Importantly, this is about places, not individuals — we cannot say anything about whether a particular person’s wealth affected their survival. What surprised us most was the specificity: The association was strongest for drug-related deaths and suicide and entirely absent for cancer.
A particularly strong association involving suicide and drug-related deaths, but no association with cancer mortality. What might that tell us?
We think about the cancer result almost as a built-in control: Cancer mortality should not respond to contemporaneous local economic conditions. That the association concentrates precisely in causes thought to be responsive to psychological distress and economic insecurity is consistent with the deaths-of-despair literature.
What is the difference between income inequality and wealth inequality, and why does that distinction matter for health?
Income is a measure of flow (what arrives each month); wealth is a measure of stock (what you have inherited or accumulated — property, assets, and savings minus debts). Picture two families each earning $70,000 a year. One owns their home, received help with the down payment from parents, and has a retirement account. The other rents, carries student and medical debt, and has essentially zero net worth. Same income, completely different capacity to absorb a job loss, an illness, or a rent increase.
Why would the concentration of wealth among a relatively small number of households have anything to do with whether other people in the same region live or die?
Importantly, our study does not identify the mechanism. What we can say is that this is not simply a story about more poor people living in a place. Our models adjust for poverty, median income, median wealth, employment, and education, and the association persists. That points toward something about the distribution itself — how unequal the local environment is — rather than its average level. Plausible routes include uneven public investment, weaker local institutions, housing and credit markets that price people out, and chronic psychosocial stress.
Your findings remained significant even after accounting for poverty, employment, income inequality, and other economic conditions. Why is that important?
Public health research has long measured local economic disadvantage through poverty rates, median income, and unemployment. Those capture the level of resources in a place. Our results suggest that how wealth is distributed carries information those measures miss.
One of your most striking findings is that the association between wealth inequality and mortality was substantially stronger among women than men. What might explain that difference?
We can only speculate here. Exclusion from local labor markets, caregiving burdens that intensify when community resources thin out, and greater reliance on the public goods and social infrastructure that erode in highly unequal places all sound like plausible explanations to me.
Your research examines changes in wealth inequality across regional labor markets. Why did you use these large commuting zones rather than simply comparing individual counties? For example, a regional economy like Philadelphia’s can encompass counties in Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware, with enormous differences in household wealth.
First, county-level measures of wealth inequality do not currently exist. Conceptually, commuting zones correspond to the economy people actually inhabit (which is why they are widely used in economics). Your Philadelphia example is exactly right: The labor market that sets someone’s wages, housing costs, and job prospects crosses three state lines, and county borders are administrative artifacts that cut through it. So we ask whether local mortality moves with inequality in the broader economy surrounding it. At the same time, we cannot tell whether the inequality that matters is within neighborhoods or within some other granular geographic scale.
How confident are you that increasing wealth inequality contributes to premature mortality, rather than simply occurring alongside other conditions that cause people to die younger?
We are confident that the association is real and robust. It’s not a causal estimate, though. We tried everything that can be done with existing data: We compare each county with itself over time, absorb state-specific shocks year by year, and adjust for changing local conditions. Several checks behave as they should — mortality was generally not already diverging before inequality rose, population turnover and net migration did not track inequality changes, and measurement error in the inequality estimates would bias us toward finding nothing.
What should policymakers take away from this research? If increasing wealth inequality is associated with higher mortality independently of poverty and income inequality, does that suggest public health policy needs to pay more attention to how wealth is distributed? What kinds of future research or policy interventions might help determine whether reducing wealth inequality could improve survival?
Absolutely they should. We think the distribution of wealth should belong on the list of things we monitor as a determinant of population health, alongside poverty and income.
