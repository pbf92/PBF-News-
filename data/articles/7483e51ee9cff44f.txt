TITLE: Adjunctive Breathing Exercises Modestly Improve Certain Childhood Asthma Lung Measures, Review Finds
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A newly published systematic review and meta-analysis of eleven randomized controlled trials indicates that incorporating breathing exercises alongside standard pharmacological treatment provides modest improvements in select physiological measures of lung function and respiratory muscle strength among children with asthma. Synthesizing data across 611 pediatric patients, investigators determined that adjunctive respiratory training generated statistically significant gains in forced expiratory volume in one second expressed as a percentage of predicted value, forced vital capacity percentage predicted, the ratio of forced expiratory volume to forced vital capacity, and maximal expiratory pressure. However, these beneficial shifts were largely confined to normalized, percentage-predicted parameters rather than absolute spirometric volumes, and the overall certainty of the underlying evidence ranged from very low to moderate across the assessed endpoints.
Asthma represents the most prevalent chronic respiratory disorder observed in pediatric populations, typically beginning in early childhood and characterized by recurrent episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and cough, alongside variable expiratory airflow limitation. Although guideline-directed anti-inflammatory regimens and bronchodilators serve as the cornerstone of disease management by suppressing airway inflammation and reversing bronchospasm, real-world therapeutic barriers persist. Approximately 44 percent of pediatric asthma patients encounter difficulties maintaining consistent, long-term medication adherence, which contributes directly to suboptimal symptom control. Furthermore, even when patients adhere strictly to standard drug regimens, residual difficulties frequently endure, including respiratory muscle fatigue, diminished exercise tolerance, subjective dyspnea, and impaired physical functioning that pharmaceutical agents alone cannot completely alleviate.
To investigate whether non-pharmacological modalities can address these ongoing clinical challenges, researchers from the College of Traditional Chinese Medicine and its Affiliated Hospital at Changchun University of Chinese Medicine conducted a comprehensive synthesis of published clinical trials. The research team searched four primary electronic literature databases—PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library—from their inception through May 2026. The search protocol adhered strictly to PRISMA guidelines and was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database. Screening focused exclusively on randomized controlled trials evaluating individuals under 18 years of age clinically diagnosed with asthma, comparing adjunctive breathing training modalities against standard medical therapy, usual care, or sham interventions, while excluding trials that integrated broader multi-component physical exercise regimens or unstandardized holistic programs.
The qualitative and quantitative synthesis identified eleven eligible randomized controlled trials meeting all inclusion criteria. Geographically, the studies were conducted across several countries, including three trials originating from Turkey, two from Saudi Arabia, two from India, and one trial each from Brazil, Germany, China, and New Zealand. Patient cohort sizes within individual trials were relatively modest, ranging from 12 to 60 children. The investigated breathing interventions encompassed distinct therapeutic techniques aimed at altering inherent respiratory patterns or conditioning musculature: five studies evaluated targeted inspiratory muscle training, four investigated the Buteyko breathing technique, one delivered home-based pulmonary rehabilitation encompassing diaphragmatic and mobilising exercises, and one implemented structured chest physical therapy regimens.
In the primary meta-analysis of spirometric parameters, adjunctive breathing training produced statistically significant elevations across several percentage-predicted lung indices. Pooled analysis of four trials involving 130 children revealed a mean difference increase of 4.92 percentage points in forced expiratory volume in one second percentage predicted, with an accompanying 95 percent confidence interval spanning 2.44 to 7.41 percentage points. Similarly, data pooled from three trials enrolling 96 children showed an increase in forced vital capacity percentage predicted of 5.18 percentage points, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 2.44 to 7.92 percentage points. Analysis of the forced expiratory volume to forced vital capacity ratio across four studies comprising 188 children demonstrated a standardized mean difference of 0.58, and an evaluation of mid-expiratory flow at 25 to 75 percent of vital capacity across two studies showed an improvement of 8.49 percentage points.
Despite these favorable percentage-predicted changes, the meta-analysis found no statistically significant improvements when examining absolute spirometric volumes. Pooled estimates for absolute forced expiratory volume in one second across two trials showed a mean difference of 3.69 with a 95 percent confidence interval crossing zero from negative 2.11 to positive 9.49, while absolute forced vital capacity yielded a non-significant mean difference of 0.14. Similarly, peak expiratory flow failed to demonstrate a statistically robust improvement under a random-effects model, which yielded a standardized mean difference of 1.25 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning negative 0.28 to positive 2.78. The authors highlighted that the divergence between percentage-predicted and absolute volumes may stem from the heightened sensitivity of percentage-predicted metrics in accounting for pediatric somatic growth and developmental variation.
Secondary functional outcomes yielded notable improvements in expiratory respiratory muscle performance alongside suggestive findings in overall disease control. Across four trials comprising 188 children, maximal expiratory pressure increased significantly in the breathing training groups compared with controls, producing a pooled standardized mean difference of 1.18 within a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.37 to 1.99. In contrast, maximal inspiratory pressure demonstrated high sensitivity to single-study mechanics, specifically differing sign conventions in recording negative pressures. Asthma control scores, evaluated across three trials involving 118 children using the standard Asthma Control Test and the Childhood Asthma Control Test, demonstrated an overall standardized mean difference improvement of 0.52; however, this effect exhibited substantial statistical heterogeneity and lost significance under certain model choices due in part to ceiling effects in cohorts with well-controlled baseline status.
Methodological quality assessments conducted via the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool revealed pervasive design vulnerabilities that qualify these quantitative outcomes. None of the eleven included trials achieved a rating of overall low risk of bias, with all studies categorized as having some concerns. While the domain of missing outcome data was well managed—with over 90 percent of studies demonstrating low risk—the measurement of outcomes proved highly vulnerable. More than 63 percent of studies raised concerns and over 18 percent exhibited high risk in outcome measurement, primarily driven by the absence of outcome assessor blinding and reliance on subjective self-reporting. Furthermore, more than half of the trials lacked prospective clinical trial registrations or pre-published analysis protocols, preventing investigators from definitively ruling out selective outcome reporting.
Safety considerations remain incompletely documented across the available literature, precluding definitive conclusions regarding long-term clinical safety. Among the eleven included trials, only four documented any adverse event monitoring, and none utilized a systematic adverse event reporting framework. Two trials explicitly stated that no adverse events occurred during the intervention window, and no serious incidents resulting in hospitalization or emergency department visits were identified. The only trial reporting discrete adverse events involved hospitalized severe asthma patients receiving chest physical therapy, wherein one participant experienced transient headache and vomiting. While the absence of severe signals is reassuring, the systematic review emphasized that the lack of systematic reporting cannot be conflated with verified evidence of safety.
Ultimately, the systematic review concludes that while breathing exercises demonstrate potential as an auxiliary, non-pharmacological rehabilitation tool capable of supporting select spirometric parameters and expiratory strength, they cannot replace standard medical management. Crucially, none of the included trials prespecified a validated minimally clinically important difference threshold for pediatric spirometry, leaving it unconfirmed whether the observed numerical improvements correspond to tangible everyday symptom relief. The investigators underscored that substantial clinical questions regarding long-term exacerbation rates, medication reduction, and sustained physiological benefits remain unanswered, necessitating larger, rigorously blinded, and methodologically standardized clinical trials before breathing training can be formally recommended within pediatric clinical guidelines.
