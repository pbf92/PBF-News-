TITLE: Automated Vasopressor Titration Improves Hemodynamic Control in Surgery, Systematic Review Finds
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Automated closed-loop vasopressor administration significantly increases the proportion of time surgical patients maintain their blood pressure within a prespecified target range while cutting time spent in hypotension, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating randomized controlled trials across perioperative and intensive care settings. Maintaining mean arterial pressure within predefined boundaries is central to hemodynamic stability in adults receiving vasopressor therapy, as even brief periods of hypotension are linked to acute kidney injury, myocardial injury, and elevated mortality, whereas excessive hypertension elevates myocardial oxygen demand and bleeding risks. Despite the widespread reliance on continuous infusion of agents like norepinephrine, conventional manual adjustment by clinical staff remains intermittent, reactive, and highly dependent on individual operator attention, often resulting in prolonged periods outside therapeutic windows. Investigators sought to determine whether autonomous algorithm-driven systems, which adjust infusion delivery rates in real time based on continuous hemodynamic readings, could resolve the mismatch between dynamic vascular physiology and manual clinical intervention.
The quantitative synthesis and systematic review, prospectively registered in PROSPERO under registration identification CRD420250655697 and prepared in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement, pooled evidence from randomized controlled trials comparing closed-loop vasopressor systems directly with standard manual titration strategies. Comprehensive literature searches were executed across MEDLINE via PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and the Cochrane Library for peer-reviewed studies published between January 1, 2000, and June 30, 2025. Three independent authors reviewed retrieved titles, abstracts, and full texts according to predefined eligibility criteria, resolving disputes through deliberation and formal adjudication by a designated senior author. To ensure data integrity, trials were restricted to adult populations eighteen years of age or older treated in perioperative or critical care environments requiring vasopressor support for blood pressure control, specifically evaluating autonomous titration systems paired with continuous arterial blood pressure monitoring against manual titration guided by clinician judgment.
Eligibility criteria strictly omitted observational cohorts, non-randomized designs, pediatric populations, simulation models, animal experiments, and studies published solely as conference abstracts. The authors deliberately excluded obstetric investigations examining spinal anesthesia-induced hypotension during cesarean delivery because those clinical settings represent a transient, distinct physiologic state driven by sympathectomy that is primarily managed using episodic boluses of phenylephrine or ephedrine rather than continuous infusion regimens, which would limit comparability to general surgical and critically ill medical patients. Database queries yielded an initial pool of 3,261 total citations across the six electronic indexes. Following the removal of 279 duplicate records, 2,982 records underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 2,973 citations. From nine full-text publications evaluated in detail, three were excluded for lacking the prespecified mean arterial pressure control endpoints, leaving six randomized controlled trials totaling 215 individual patients for inclusion in the formal systematic review.
The six analyzed randomized trials were published between 2008 and 2024, featuring individual trial sample sizes ranging from 18 to 53 patients. Clinical environments across the trials were predominantly perioperative, encompassing intermediate- to high-risk noncardiac operations as well as post-cardiac and major abdominal surgical procedures. Only a single trial was conducted within an intensive care unit, focusing on norepinephrine weaning in patients recovering from septic shock. The closed-loop control architectures varied considerably across the evaluated platforms, incorporating proportional-integral-derivative controllers, fuzzy logic algorithms, predictive rule-based programs, and specialized computer-assisted hemodynamic software systems. Despite architectural differences, all systems shared the core functional mechanism of automating infusion rate alterations in response to real-time arterial blood pressure measurements. Observation windows reflected their clinical contexts, ranging from short intraoperative sessions lasting two to four hours to extended postoperative assessments and weaning protocols concluding at drug cessation.
Methodological quality across individual trials was systematically evaluated using version 2.0 of the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials across five specific domains. Three of the included studies were judged to have an overall low risk of bias, while the remaining three trials were rated as presenting some concerns, with no trial classified as possessing a high overall risk of bias. Concerns identified by the evaluators were driven primarily by the inherent lack of provider blinding to the automation hardware, introducing potential deviations from intended interventions, alongside missing outcome data resulting from higher participant attrition in one trial. Domains evaluating randomization sequence generation, outcome measurement precision, and selective reporting were consistently judged as low risk across all six studies. Evaluating evidence certainty using the GRADE framework demonstrated moderate certainty for primary hemodynamic endpoints and major adverse events, low certainty for cumulative norepinephrine consumption, and high certainty for minor adverse event reporting.
Quantitative pooling of primary hemodynamic metrics drew upon five perioperative randomized trials comprising 176 total patients, as the single intensive care trial did not record comparable arterial pressure target metrics. Random-effects meta-analysis revealed that closed-loop vasopressor delivery significantly increased the percentage of time patients remained within their predefined mean arterial pressure target compared with manual administration, yielding a pooled mean difference of 33.94 percent (95% confidence interval 20.41 to 47.46 percent; I-squared = 77 percent). Concurrently, autonomous administration produced a statistically significant reduction in the duration of hypotensive exposure, achieving a mean difference of -18.24 percent (95% confidence interval -28.95 to -7.53 percent; I-squared = 73 percent). Although automated administration showed a numerical trend toward lowering the time spent in hypertension with a mean difference of -9.95 percent (95% confidence interval -21.95 to 2.05 percent; I-squared = 64 percent), this difference did not attain statistical significance.
Analysis of secondary clinical endpoints indicated that the heightened physiologic precision achieved by closed-loop titration did not translate into reductions in total vasopressor utilization or differences in patient safety profiles. Six trials reported cumulative norepinephrine requirements, and a pooled standardized mean difference showed no statistically significant difference between automated control and standard manual management (standardized mean difference -0.40, 95% confidence interval -1.07 to 0.27; I-squared = 64 percent), accompanied by a wide prediction interval spanning -1.92 to 1.12. Major adverse event frequencies evaluated across three reporting trials demonstrated comparable risks between intervention and control arms (risk ratio 0.73, 95% confidence interval 0.15 to 3.44; I-squared = 30 percent). Similarly, pooling minor adverse events across three trials revealed no significant divergence between autonomous delivery and manual titration (risk ratio 1.08, 95% confidence interval 0.46 to 2.53; I-squared = 10 percent), confirming that automated infusion rate adjustments maintained a comparable safety profile.
Healthcare utilization outcomes such as intensive care unit length of stay were examined across several trials but could not be combined into a quantitative meta-analysis because of substantial clinical and methodological diversity in outcome definitions and reporting metrics. Studies variously quantified resource use in hours, whole days, or composite variables such as intensive care unit-free days. Individual trial analyses consistently reported no statistically significant differences between trial arms: one trial recorded intensive care unit stays of 68 hours in the closed-loop group versus 86 hours in manual controls (P = 0.522), while two other trials observed identical medians of 9.0 hours and 19 hours between respective groups. In the intensive care septic shock weaning trial, investigators measured intensive care unit-free days at day 28 rather than absolute stay, identifying no significant difference between closed-loop and manual management (3 days versus 7 days; P = 0.74).
The authors contextualized their findings against existing medical literature, noting concordance with earlier reviews demonstrating that automated systems enhance physiological stability without functioning as drug-sparing technologies. An earlier umbrella review examining automated delivery of multiple pharmacological agents, including four norepinephrine trials, similarly found reductions in time spent outside target blood pressure parameters. Likewise, an obstetric meta-analysis showed lower incidence of hypotension without cumulative dose reductions, though restricted entirely to spinal anesthesia during cesarean procedures. A broader meta-analysis encompassing eight trials and 640 patients also identified improved hemodynamic stability without reductions in total vasopressor consumption; however, a large share of its analyzed cohort originated from obstetric populations. By deliberately excluding obstetric cohorts, the present systematic review provides a more clinically homogeneous evaluation focused strictly on non-obstetric adult surgical and intensive care environments.
The review authors highlighted critical methodological and practical limitations that temper the immediate generalizability of these findings to broader hospital populations. Substantial statistical heterogeneity was evident across primary analyses, reflecting marked differences in control algorithms, blood pressure targets, clinical case complexity, and observation timeframes. The total analyzed sample across the six included trials was relatively small at 215 patients, which restricts statistical power to detect rare adverse events or differences in meaningful patient-centered outcomes. Furthermore, because hemodynamic control benefits were demonstrated exclusively in short perioperative observation windows lasting two to four hours in highly controlled surgical suites, efficacy may not reflect performance in prolonged, dynamic critical care environments. The authors concluded that while closed-loop systems improve surrogate physiological control during surgery, larger pragmatic clinical trials are required to establish whether this algorithmic precision yields tangible improvements in major postoperative morbidity or mortality.
