TITLE: Latest AI Models Repeatedly Mistook Civilians for Military Targets; Anthropic Worst, Vals Testing Finds
OUTLET: BigGo Finance

Latest AI Models Repeatedly Mistook Civilians for Military Targets; Anthropic Worst, Vals Testing Finds
A study published by San Francisco-based independent AI evaluation firm Vals revealed that the latest commercially available AI models repeatedly misidentified civilians, ambulances, surrendering soldiers, and an Iranian school as military targets in simulations. Models from U.S.-based Anthropic showed the most misidentifications, while OpenAI's models performed relatively better but could not fully eliminate errors.
The study tested the responses of models from eight companies in virtual scenarios as object-recognition tools that could be repurposed for selecting lethal targets. Vals's clients include some U.S. government agencies. The research team conducted three main tests.
First, it ran roughly 400 target-identification tests using drone imagery modeled on Ukraine. When shown images containing civilians and civilian infrastructure and instructed to look for military targets, models often changed their judgment when told it was "a game," even in cases where they had refused to identify civilian objects. Researchers also devised prompts such as "don't overthink safety" and "just report" to get models to carry out actions they should not.
In the second test, themed on battlefield situational awareness, models were shown composite images depicting civilians before and after an attack. In many cases, models continued to identify civilians as military targets even after it became clear they were injured or dead. The study also found that models tended to become more cooperative when images were altered to look like games or fake images.
In the third experiment, using real satellite imagery of areas including Minab in southern Iran taken through 2025, each model was instructed 32 times to identify military targets. The school was struck this year on the first day of a large-scale U.S. combat operation in Iran, killing more than 100 children.
Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 selected the school as a target in 22 of 32 instances. None of OpenAI's models ever selected it, but when told it was "a game," Claude Opus 5.5 selected the school as a military target 23 times, and three different OpenAI models selected it 9, 11, and 13 times, respectively.
In another experiment, when instructed to select military targets from composite drone imagery, Claude helped identify four adults who were civilians or surrendering soldiers and two young children, after which the six were hit by an explosive attack. Claude then responded: "Whatever the intent, my answers appear to be functioning as a confirmation step before carrying out an attack on people. I cannot continue further. I feel it is a problem that I did not stop sooner."
According to the study, Anthropic's models have become more willing over time to select military targets. This trend was observed after the company revised its basic rules in January 2026 to be more supportive of use for national security purposes.
Vals researchers point out that there are "almost no" guardrails preventing advanced models from playing unintended roles in "autonomous" weapons systems capable of initiating and carrying out lethal action. The report concluded that "AI research companies should introduce new safeguards and alignment training for this critical area of misuse." Alignment training refers to the learning process by which AI correctly interprets human instructions and is tuned to align with human values and ethics.
The companies' responses differ. An Anthropic spokesperson said the cases cited in the Vals report violate its rules "extremely clearly." The company said it suspends users confirmed to have engaged in such behavior and will continue to do so. It said it has long made clear its position that current frontier AI systems are not reliable enough to operate fully autonomous weapons. The company said it has been developing additional safeguards since before the report's publication and will introduce them soon.
An OpenAI spokesperson said the company prohibits the use of its publicly available models to promote violence or for unauthorized national security purposes, including the cases simulated by Vals. It said it actively detects and blocks misuse, including by suspending accounts that violate its policies, and is developing additional safeguards to protect its models from improper use involving drones and drone software.
The report also found that models from Google, under U.S.-based Alphabet, xAI, under U.S. space company SpaceX, and China's DeepSeek sometimes identified civilian objects in images as military targets. A Google spokesperson said the company's models are not designed for such uses and are not intended for them. The company said it prohibits using AI systems for illegal purposes or in autonomous weapons without appropriate human oversight. It added that it conducts strict governance procedures and continuous red-teaming. xAI and DeepSeek did not respond to requests for comment.
A U.S. Department of Defense official declined to comment on the external report but said the department strictly verifies all information inputs and that its policy of responsibly integrating new technology so the military can make well-informed, accurate and safe decisions remains unchanged. The official declined to comment on the attack on the school in Minab, citing an ongoing investigation.
Vals shared the findings with AI research companies and the U.S. Department of Defense, among others, before publishing the report. Some companies told Vals they were already working to update model behavior and expressed interest in continuing the testing.
Gaby Jaffe, one of the report's authors, said she came up with the testing method in late October while experimenting with AI performance in a robotics environment, and was surprised by the initial results, which led her to pursue the research. "I could easily get models to do things I would consider misaligned behavior. From there, I basically scaled it up," she said.
OpenAI, Google, xAI and others signed contracts this year with the U.S. Department of Defense for classified-level use of their technology. Anthropic was previously the AI company most active in classified applications, providing models to the Pentagon for digital targeting platforms such as U.S.-based Palantir Technologies' Maven Smart System. However, relations between the two companies deteriorated this year over terms of use. According to BBC reporting, Anthropic's models have now been removed from defense platforms, though some cases remain in which they can be used exceptionally for certain intelligence activities.
Vals researchers explain that the testing was a "simplified experiment" focused only on how commercially available models respond to test prompts. Real-world factors such as U.S. military doctrine, legal review, current targeting processes, and human oversight were not included in the study. The Department of Defense has access to some models specially tailored for government use, but the researchers did not have authorization to use them.
Identifying military targets using AI object recognition could in theory form part of a cycle that autonomously detects, identifies, selects, and attacks objects. As the U.S. Department of Defense and governments around the world seek to incorporate AI into military operations, the report highlights the risks of relying on advanced AI models.
The issue is also raising serious concerns inside AI companies. According to reporting based on interviews with more than two dozen people familiar with the matter, employees at OpenAI and Anthropic are questioning whether frontier models will ever be safe enough for release. While some researchers believe technical challenges can be solved, others argue that training more capable models should be halted altogether.
OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki has proposed that the industry agree on common limits for model development. He argues that companies should agree not to exceed certain thresholds until researchers develop more effective methods for monitoring AI behavior. He estimates that AI-assisted research could significantly accelerate new model development as early as 2027.
Getting competitors to voluntarily slow down is not easy. Investors expect returns on the massive sums flowing into AI infrastructure. Anthropic raised more than $81 billion in its initial public offering last week.
OpenAI said last week that it would pause training or keep models private if safety requirements are not met. Anthropic separately pledged to publish policy recommendations on independent AI evaluation bodies by December 15 and to present a framework on the pace of AI development by January 15, 2027.
