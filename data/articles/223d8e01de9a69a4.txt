TITLE: Post-Mortem Microbiome Analysis Remains an Investigational Forensic Tool, Review Finds
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Post-mortem microbial analysis remains an investigational adjunct rather than a validated standalone forensic tool, according to a comprehensive scoping review evaluating human cadaveric biological samples. The human post-mortem microbiome is increasingly investigated as a potential source of complementary information in forensic practice, particularly for post-mortem interval estimation and decomposition assessment. However, its relevance to other medico-legal questions remains far less clearly established. Although post-mortem microbial communities provide informative complementary clues, researchers found that current evidence across forensic domains does not yet justify treating microbiome analysis as an independent, fully established forensic technique in death investigations.
To assess the forensic applicability of microbial communities identified in human cadaveric and post-mortem biological samples, researchers systematically searched major scientific databases. The authors searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for all relevant publications available up to 19 January 2026. Only original human post-mortem studies met the eligibility criteria for inclusion. Following the search, study data were extracted using a structured framework, and the methodological quality of each included study was systematically appraised using validated assessment tools selected according to study design.
A total of thirty-eight studies were included in the review, encompassing multiple external and internal biological matrices alongside predominantly bacterial profiling. Among the forensic questions explored, microbial succession related to decomposition and post-mortem interval estimation represented the most extensively investigated directly forensic domain. Researchers have repeatedly tracked changes in microbial communities across decomposing human tissues, examining bacterial succession as a possible biological indicator of post-mortem interval. Nevertheless, substantial methodological differences across studies continue to challenge the comparability of these post-mortem interval models.
Evidence concerning applications beyond post-mortem interval estimation proved considerably more limited across the identified literature. The scoping review found sparse data regarding the determination of cause of death or manner of death, infection-related forensic investigations, disease-associated or tissue-associated microbial signatures, and sample preservation. In addition, the forensic application of microbial profiling to suspected drowning cases was supported by only a single proof-of-concept study. These narrow findings demonstrate that microbial approaches to specific medico-legal questions remain in preliminary stages of development.
Across all evaluated forensic domains, the review identified several critical weaknesses that currently restrict forensic transferability. Prominent obstacles include widespread methodological heterogeneity, small human cohorts, contamination-related concerns, and limited external validation. Consequently, the authors emphasized that progress toward routine implementation in forensic practice requires the development of matrix-specific, contamination-controlled protocols. Furthermore, before post-mortem microbial profiling can serve as an accepted forensic method, it will require rigorous, independent validation across larger and geographically diverse human cohorts.
