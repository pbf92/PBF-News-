TITLE: Genetic study of endometriosis reveals new clues to disease biology
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

PHILADELPHIA—Dozens of previously unreported genetic risk variants for endometriosis have been identified, offering fresh insight into the biological mechanisms that drive the disease. The international research team—led in part by scientists at the Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania—analyzed genetic data from more than 1 million women across 14 biobanks worldwide. The findings are published today in Nature Genetics.
Endometriosis affects an estimated 1 in 10 women of reproductive age and occurs when tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus, often causing severe pelvic pain, infertility, and other chronic health issues. Despite its prevalence, diagnosis can take years and treatment options remain limited. The findings could guide future research on developing noninvasive methods for predicting endometriosis risk.
“This study significantly expands our understanding of the genetic architecture of endometriosis,” said Shefali Setia Verma, PhD, an associate professor of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine and principal investigator at Penn’s Center for Women’s Health and Reproductive Medicine. “By studying a large and diverse population, we were able to identify new genes and biological pathways that may help explain how the disease develops and why patients experience it differently.”
Identifying new risk regions and pathways
The researchers identified 58 genetic regions associated with endometriosis risk, including 27 not previously linked to the disease. Because roughly one-third of participants were from non-European ancestries, the team was able to uncover ancestry-specific genetic risk factors that earlier studies may have missed.
Advanced genetic and molecular analyses pointed to 314 genes that may contribute to endometriosis, many involved in immune function, inflammation, hormone regulation, tissue growth, and blood vessel formation. The findings reinforce the central roles of immune and estrogen‑related pathways in disease development.
The study also revealed genetic connections between endometriosis and several other health conditions and traits—including anxiety, insomnia, headaches, cardiovascular risk factors, and metabolic traits—highlighting the disease’s effects beyond the reproductive system.
Penn researchers contributed to the project through the Penn Medicine BioBank and the global biobank consortium that enabled the analysis. The findings lay the groundwork for research on earlier diagnostic tools and more targeted therapies. Investigators are now working to validate the most promising candidate genes and better understand how they influence disease progression.

Penn Medicine is one of the world’s leading academic medical centers, dedicated to the related missions of medical education, biomedical research, excellence in patient care, and community service.
The organization consists of the University of Pennsylvania Health System and Penn’s Raymond and Ruth Perelman School of Medicine, founded in 1765 as the nation’s first medical school.
The Perelman School of Medicine is consistently among the nation's top recipients of funding from the National Institutes of Health, with more than $588 million awarded in the 2024 fiscal year. Home to a proud history of “firsts,” Penn Medicine teams have pioneered discoveries that have shaped modern medicine, including CAR T cell therapy for cancer and the Nobel Prize-winning mRNA technology used in COVID-19 vaccines.
The University of Pennsylvania Health System cares for patients in facilities and their homes stretching from the Susquehanna River in Pennsylvania to the New Jersey shore. UPHS facilities include the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania, Penn Presbyterian Medical Center, Chester County Hospital, Doylestown Health, Lancaster General Health, Princeton Health, and Pennsylvania Hospital—the nation’s first hospital, chartered in 1751. Additional facilities and enterprises include Penn Medicine at Home, GSPP Rehabilitation, Lancaster Behavioral Health Hospital, and Princeton House Behavioral Health, among others.
Penn Medicine is a $13.7 billion enterprise powered by more than 50,000 talented faculty and staff.
