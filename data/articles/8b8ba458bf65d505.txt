TITLE: Novel hormone therapy significantly reduces risk of breast cancer recurrence
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

A new type of hormone therapy significantly reduced the risk of invasive breast cancer recurrence or death in patients with early-stage, hormone receptor (HR)-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer compared with standard treatments, according to findings from a large international phase 3 clinical trial co-led by investigators at the UCLA Health Jonsson Comprehensive Cancer Center.
The study, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, found that patients who received the oral drug giredestrant had a 30% lower relative risk of invasive breast cancer recurrence or death compared with those receiving standard hormone therapy. The findings suggest the drug could offer a more effective treatment option for patients at increased risk of recurrence after surgery.
“For patients with breast cancer, one of their biggest fears is having the cancer come back, even after surgery, radiation or chemotherapy,” said Dr. Aditya Bardia, professor of medicine at the David Geffen School of Medicine at UCLA, director of Translational Research Integration at the UCLA Health Jonsson Comprehensive Cancer Center and global principal investigator of the trial. “We need treatments that are not only more effective at preventing recurrence, but also easier for patients to tolerate so they can stay on therapy long term.”
About 70% of breast cancers are hormone receptor-positive, meaning they rely on hormones such as estrogen to grow. After surgery and, in some cases, chemotherapy, patients typically receive hormone therapy for several years to help prevent the cancer from returning. These treatments work by lowering estrogen levels or blocking estrogen from stimulating cancer cells.
Although existing therapies, including tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors, have significantly improved outcomes, some patients still experience a recurrence despite treatment, and the risk can persist for decades. Some cancers also develop resistance to these treatments, and side effects can make it difficult for patients to stay on therapy long term.
Giredestrant is an investigational drug belonging to a newer class of hormone therapies called selective estrogen receptor degraders, or SERDs. The drug works by blocking estrogen from attaching to receptors on cancer cells and triggering the breakdown of those receptors. By both blocking and breaking down the receptors, giredestrant disrupts the signals that help cancer cells grow and may help overcome resistance to existing hormone therapies.
To evaluate whether giredestrant could improve outcomes, researchers conducted a phase 3 clinical trial called lidERA, which enrolled 4,170 patients with stage 1, 2 or 3 HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer who were at intermediate or high risk of recurrence. Participants had undergone surgery and some had also received chemotherapy.
Patients were randomly assigned to receive either giredestrant, a once-daily pill (2,084 patients), or standard hormone therapy, including tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors (2,086 patients). Both groups were assigned to receive treatment for five years.
After a median follow-up of approximately 32 months, researchers found that patients receiving giredestrant had a 30% lower relative risk of invasive breast cancer recurrence or death compared with those receiving standard hormone therapy.
The study also found that giredestrant reduced the relative risk of cancer spreading to distant parts of the body, such as the bones, liver or lungs, by 31%. This is particularly significant because breast cancer that spreads to other organs is more difficult to treat.
Side effects were generally similar between the two groups, with joint pain being the most commonly reported. Fewer patients taking giredestrant stopped treatment because of side effects compared with those receiving standard hormone therapy.
“This is an important advance in breast cancer treatment,” said Bardia. “For the first time in more than 20 years, we have a potential new hormone therapy option for patients with early-stage, estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer. By more effectively targeting the estrogen receptor, this approach could help reduce the risk of cancer returning and improve long-term outcomes for patients.”
The team will continue following patients to determine whether the benefits of giredestrant persist over time and whether the treatment improves overall survival. Additional studies are also exploring whether combining giredestrant with other targeted therapies could further reduce the risk of breast cancer recurrence.
The FDA is currently reviewing giredestrant for the treatment of early-stage, estrogen receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer, with a decision expected by Nov. 30, 2026.
