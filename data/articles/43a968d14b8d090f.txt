TITLE: Topical metronidazole improves anal fissure healing in clinical review of microbiome treatments
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Adjunctive topical metronidazole significantly improves healing rates and reduces pain in patients with anal fissures, according to a systematic review evaluating microbiome-related interventions in benign anorectal disease. The study, authored by Max G. Matta, Mohammed U. Memon, and James Kinross and published in the International Journal of Colorectal Disease, examined clinical evidence to assess whether targeting the local microbiome can improve outcomes in routine colorectal practice.
Benign anorectal diseases—encompassing haemorrhoids, anal fissures, anal fistulae, and pruritus ani—represent common causes of patient morbidity that frequently demonstrate variable outcomes under standard treatment regimens. Emerging scientific evidence has suggested that microbial factors may actively contribute to disease pathophysiology as well as the healing process. In response to these observations, the systematic review aimed to evaluate the clinical trial evidence for microbiome-related therapeutic interventions while summarising current mechanistic evidence supporting microbial involvement in these conditions.
To compile the available evidence, the investigators executed a PRISMA-compliant systematic review searching four major medical databases: PubMed, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and Ovid MEDLINE. The electronic search covered all records from database inception through October 2025. The authors restricted their primary clinical evaluation to randomised controlled trials investigating microbiome-related interventions, while mechanistic and observational microbiological studies were reviewed separately to establish the necessary biological context.
Methodological quality across the included trials was appraised using established evaluative standards. The authors assessed the risk of bias within each individual trial using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. Furthermore, the overall certainty of the compiled clinical evidence was systematically assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework, known as GRADE, ensuring a structured appraisal of trial validity.
Across the identified literature, six randomised controlled trials enrolling a cumulative total of 436 patients met the inclusion criteria, spanning haemorrhoids, anal fissure, and anal fistula. In patients with anal fissures, adjunctive topical metronidazole produced substantial clinical benefits compared with standard therapy alone. Fissure healing rates ranged from 67 percent to 83 percent in patients receiving topical metronidazole versus 33 percent to 47 percent in those receiving standard therapy alone. In addition, patients treated with topical metronidazole experienced lower pain levels, demonstrating a mean difference of negative 1.8 to negative 2.6 on a visual analogue scale.
Evidence regarding microbiome interventions in other benign anorectal conditions was substantially more restricted. Clinical data supporting the use of probiotics in haemorrhoids were limited to a single small randomised controlled trial conducted specifically in postpartum women. For anal fistulae, one small trial evaluating oral ciprofloxacin in patients with Crohn’s-related anal fistula demonstrated no significant effect on microbial diversity. Notably, the authors identified no eligible interventional studies evaluating microbiome-related therapies for pruritus ani.
Observational microbiological studies reviewed by the research team suggest that local anorectal microbial communities differ compositionally from faecal microbiota, although causal relationships between these differences and disease states remain uncertain. The authors concluded that while selected interventions—particularly adjunctive topical metronidazole for anal fissure—may improve clinical outcomes, current evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. Although existing microbiological studies offer biological plausibility for microbial involvement, further adequately powered mechanistic and clinical investigations are necessary before routine clinical implementation can be recommended in colorectal practice.
