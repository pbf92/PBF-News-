TITLE: Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program — Latest Stories
OUTLET: Pain News Network

By Crystal Lindell
When the federal government cut emergency funding for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) in 2023, people who lost food aid had higher rates of chronic pain and severe pain, according to a new study published in JAMA.
That’s especially relevant today, as the Trump Administration prepares new application rules for SNAP benefits – commonly known as food stamps – which could disqualify over six million Americans who are currently getting them.
The JAMA study looked at health data for over 9,300 low-income U.S. adults, about half of whom were enrolled in SNAP.
During the COVID-19 pandemic, the federal government temporarily increased emergency allotments for SNAP, which expired in 2023. When they ended, benefits were reduced an average of $166 per month per household.
Researchers found that the number of SNAP participants who reported chronic pain increased by 5.3% after their benefits were cut, while the number who reported having severe pain increased by 6.2%.
By comparison, low-income adults who were not enrolled in SNAP reported slightly higher rates of chronic pain (0.7%) in 2023, while the number reporting severe pain actually declined (-3.2%).
Researchers say the findings underscore the importance of food aid, and how food insecurity contributes to public health problems.
“Food insecurity, defined as limited or uncertain access to sufficient nutritious food, is a modifiable risk factor for chronic and severe pain. Food insecurity is associated with an increased risk of chronic pain diagnoses, symptoms, and health care visits. As the severity of food insecurity increases, the prevalence and severity of pain increases,” wrote lead author Anna Austin, PhD, an Associate Professor of Anesthesiology at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.
There are a few possible explanations for why rates of chronic pain and severe pain went up when food aid was reduced.
First, it’s possible people started spending money on food that would have gone to healthcare, housing, or other basic needs.
Second, food insecurity increases stress levels, which can lead to increases in physical pain and poor health in general.
And third, if people are forced to eat less food, skip meals, or eat cheaper, less nutritious food, that could worsen their pain and put additional strain on the healthcare system.
Researchers cited other studies that found an increased number of hospitalizations after Nebraska initially declined to increase emergency food stamp allotments, and again after the federal expiration of emergency SNAP benefits in the state.
They also pointed to studies that documented an increase in the number of poor physical health days and increases in poor health when emergency SNAP benefits expired.
Last year, I wrote about how cuts or disruptions to SNAP benefits can be especially harmful for people with chronic pain or disability, who often live in low-income households.
According to the CDC, over half of disabled Americans (52.4%) have chronic pain, while the most recent federal data show that nearly four in five (79%) SNAP households include a child or adult with a disability. Those households receive 83% of SNAP benefits.
Even those statistics don’t tell the full story. There are many people who are in too much pain to work full-time jobs that would pay enough to cover their household expenses — yet they are not disabled enough to qualify as fully “disabled” by government standards. Those people often rely on government programs like SNAP to make ends meet.
While a lot of people think that too many people get SNAP, the reality is, not enough people get it. What I wrote in 2025 about food being medicine still holds true:
“The number one thing I hear people say when they complain about programs like SNAP is essentially: ‘It’s not fair. I need it and don’t get it. Therefore fewer people should get it.’
But in reality, the conclusion should be the opposite. If you need a government aid program and can’t get it, the solution should be to expand access to that program – not to cut the program off for others.”
Millions of low-income Americans are about to get their SNAP benefits cut again, as the U.S. Department of Agriculture prepares to enact tougher rules for eligibility, which are expected to reduce the number of food stamp recipients by 17%.
SNAP enrollment has already fallen by more than 5 million people since the “Big Beautiful Bill” was passed in 2025, which added stricter work requirements and forced states to better monitor their spending on SNAP.
