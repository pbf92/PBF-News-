TITLE: Türkiye Orders Halt on Ads for Spellwork, Psychic Services, and Fortune-Telling
OUTLET: The Wild Hunt

ANKARA, Türkiye — Türkiye’s Advertising Board has ordered the company behind Turuncu Fal to stop advertising psychic services, spellwork, and fortune-telling on its website, Instagram, and Facebook. The board found the promotions misleading and deceptive, saying they exploited consumers’ fears and superstitions. The September decision, publicized by a Ministry of Trade adviser on November 14, applies an existing advertising restriction to one company’s online promotions.
Türkiye’s ancient sanctuaries also hold significance for modern Pagans. The Temple of Artemis at Ephesus is one of the best-known sites from the region’s spiritual past. Farther south, in Muğla province, devotees of Hecate are making pilgrimages to her sanctuary at Lagina.
In 2024, for example, The Wild Hunt reported that Türkiye had announced the restoration of the roughly 2,100-year-old Temple of Hecate in the Yatağan district of Muğla province as well as in Lagina. Other news sites such as CNN reported that Hecate’s modern devotees have made pilgrimages to the country and region.
What the Advertising Board decided
The Ministry of Trade recorded the ruling as Item 32, case 2026/1564, in the bulletin for its September 10 meeting. It identifies the advertiser as Turuncu Hizmet Gıda Limited Şirketi and lists February 10, 2026, as the publication date of the promotions under review. The board cited Türkiye’s Regulation on Commercial Advertising and Unfair Commercial Practices, which prohibits advertisements for services offered by “fortune-tellers, mediums, astrologers, and similar practitioners.” The same provision prohibits advertising for illegal betting and gambling.
The board described what it found advertised as medyum/büyü/fal, meaning “psychic services, magic, and fortune-telling.” In this context, büyü can also be translated as “spellwork.” The more specific Turkish term for Witchcraft, cadılık, does not appear in Item 32. Astrology appears in the regulation the board quoted. The findings identify psychic services, spellwork, and fortune-telling as the promotions at issue.
The board concluded that the ads violated several provisions of the advertising regulation and Article 61 of Türkiye’s Consumer Protection Law, Law No. 6502. It cited Articles 63 and 77(12) as the basis for ordering the company to stop the ads. The bulletin lists the stop order as the sole penalty in the case. Its findings describe the promotions by category and name the three channels where they appeared.
Bekir Kaplan, a communications adviser at the Ministry of Trade, publicized the ruling on X on November 14. 2026. “MINISTRY OF TRADE ADVERTISING BOARD ORDERS HALT TO ADVERTISING FOR FORTUNE-TELLING, MEDIUMSHIP AND SPELLS!”
In the post Kaplan added “We will not allow promotions that exploit our citizens’ fears, spirituality and superstitions for commercial gain.”
In response to Kaplan’s post one commenter on X questioned whether regulators enforce the rule consistently across media. The commenter pointed to television and radio segments featuring astrology, horoscopes, and tarot, arguing that guest appearances can serve a promotional purpose when practitioners publicize their brands or direct audiences to their social media accounts. They called on the Ministry of Trade and Türkiye’s broadcast regulator to explain where they draw the line between a program segment and an advertisement, and whether the same standard applies to a small social media account and a practitioner appearing on television.
The bulletin left open how regulators would assess advertising for other paid services presented as spiritual or devotional.
Several countries and U.S. jurisdictions enforce similar bans or strict restrictions on advertising and selling fortune-telling, psychic, and witchcraft services, typically citing consumer fraud protection or religious/public morality laws.
Kyrgyzstan amended its advertising law in 2024 and approved a list of prohibited categories that includes clairvoyance, divination, shamanism, and certain healing services. In Russia, lawmakers proposed restricting advertising for astrology, magic, and fortune-telling in 2025. The government recommended rejecting that proposal, citing a lack of legal clarity. Fraud and public harm.
States like New York and Pennsylvania have long-standing statutes against charging money for fortune-telling or occult predictions unless explicitly framed as entertainment. Dozens of municipalities, including over 120 cities in Tennessee, also maintain local ordinances banning or restricting paid psychic readings.
Independently, Federal Trade Commission rules separately require advertising claims to be truthful, substantiated where appropriate, and free of deception. The FTC evaluates the overall impression of an ad, so an entertainment disclaimer alone cannot cure a false claim. Many sites note the famous “for entertainment purposes only” disclaimer.
At corporate levels and even in regions where occult practices are completely legal, major social media platforms like Meta (which operates Facebook and Instagram) enforce strict global restrictions on occult advertising. Meta prohibits advertisements promoting magic spells, curses, and potions in all regions. While ads for tarot, astrology, and psychic readings are permitted in many areas, they must be framed strictly for entertainment purposes and cannot make scientific claims or guarantee specific life outcomes.
Furthermore, these permitted spiritual services are still automatically geo-blocked by social media platforms across more than a dozen countries in the Middle East, North Africa, and India to comply with regional laws and cultural sensitivities.
