TITLE: The Elvis Catalogue Could Enter Heartbreak Hotel
OUTLET: Vulture

On October 23, a group of rights holders to 274 Elvis Presley songs filed suit against Anthropic, alleging the artificial-intelligence giant illegally cribbed his songs from the internet to train AI models. Additionally, the rights holders claim that Anthropic stripped copyright-protection information from these songs and trained its models “to disregard the [protection] in both its training and output to conceal the infringement.” Some of the songs at issue include “All Shook Up,” “Can’t Help Falling in Love,” and “Return to Sender.” The lawsuit also accuses Anthropic of having “built its entire business model based on copyright infringement at a scale never seen before” — a claim in keeping with the lawsuits many creators and media companies have brought against Anthropic and other AI giants.
Lawyers for these Elvis rights holders claim that Claude has taken unlicensed material of “virtually every song from every catalog available on the internet” and created infringing material. “When recently asked to recreate specific songs ‘for a modern audience,’ Claude did so and admitted: ‘These renders keep landing very close to the originals: same structure, same hooks reused with light rewording, and full sets of verses/choruses/bridges,” the lawsuit claims. “That’s edging past ‘inspired by’ into reproducing the copyrighted song, even when the words are swapped out.” The Elvis rights holders are seeking more than $40 million in their suit. To better understand what’s going on with this lawsuit, intellectual-property experts explain what legal principles are at issue and what other artists could learn from it.
Other than involving some of the most famous rock songs in history, how is this lawsuit different from other AI lawsuits?
Ray Seilie, an attorney at Kinsella Holley Iser Kump Steinsapir, said there have been other lawsuits involving entertainment companies suing over alleged broad infringement of their catalogues. But the business-to-business vibe of these lawsuits has created “this image that this is sort of a faceless music rights holder versus a faceless AI company,” Seilie says. With this lawsuit, however, the attorneys made “the decision to focus their entire lawsuit on Elvis-based songs, which adds a level of optics that makes this case a little bit different and perhaps more likely to get public attention,” Seilie says. The other unique thing that might help Elvis rightsholder’s case? “These plaintiffs have made an interesting argument and focused on the outputs that Claude generates,” Seilie says, “and that’s because there is a lot of ambiguity over whether merely training an AI model on copyrighted works constitutes copyright infringement.”
So can AI legally ever produce anything from someone’s work?
AI companies could argue that if they obtained songs legally, teaching a model about that music isn’t all that different from teaching schoolkids about it in class. Those students might become artists who are inspired by these songs and “subconsciously or deliberately incorporate similarities to those songs when they create their own songs,” Seilie says as an example of the reasoning behind this contention. Companies have claimed that “what AI is doing is no different, except the process of learning and integration is much faster. So that’s the fair-use argument about the use of copyrighted material for training data,” Seilie says. Notably, this fair-use claim presupposes the material was obtained legally. Elvis’s team claimed Anthropic got the artist’s songs from illicit sources, including a torrent site.
Jacqueline Charlesworth, a partner at Bayla Law Group, has more to say on fair-use arguments when it comes to how AI companies get their material. “They ingest and they make copies of all this material. Sometimes they get it from the internet. Sometimes they take it from books. Sometimes it’s pirate libraries. But they’re making copies and feeding the copies into their systems, and the question then becomes whether that’s a fair use,” Charlesworth says. “Those copies that they’re making — no one’s really disputing that they’re making copies, and under ordinary rules of copyright, that wouldn’t be permitted.” While AI companies have insisted this is fair use, Charlesworth says she disagrees: “They’re copying the works of human authors owned by others without permission to then create competing works — works that compete with the works that are being copied — and that’s very problematic.”
“What else is important to know about the concept of fair use?”
One major aspect of fair use is the concept of derivative versus transformative works. If you’re creating something that is a duplicate or near-identical repackaging of copyrighted material, which is called derivative use, that’s not allowed without the rights holder’s consent. If you create something that adds enough of another creative layer, to the point of making a different product, which is known as transformative use, that’s generally allowed. So, for example, if you put a copyrighted painting on a T-shirt without a rights holder’s approval, that would be derivative and unlawful. If you take what’s likely a copyrighted soup label and create something dramatically different, à la Andy Warhol, that would be transformative and lawful, per Seilie. Elvis’s team alleges Claude is creating derivative work based on his songs.
Say Elvis’s team wins — how would that impact AI?
Daniel Cruz Hernandez, founder of the Royalty Law Firm, says he’s excited to see how compositions get litigated because music and lawsuits related to AI training have “largely focused on the sound recording so far.” “As somebody that’s in the stock market as well, I’m interested in how this could potentially affect the IPO of Anthropic,” Hernandez says, referring to its unprecedented (and highly criticized) valuation. “Even though this is like a tiny drop in the bucket — it’s like a cup of coffee of a litigation for Anthropic — there are a lot of implications that can come from the way AI models are built.”
Dale Nelson, a partner at Donaldson Callif Perez , says she thinks the Elvis rightsholders’ group is in a better position to win in the song output lawsuit than authors who sued over alleged AI training with their books. A judge found that AI companies can train their models on copyrighted books without authors’ approval while also holding that using pirated books for that purpose did not constitute fair use, according to NBC News. “If the plaintiffs get a favorable ruling that the copyrighted works can’t be used for training purposes without payment to the copyright owners, then Anthropic is looking at having to license every single copyright of work that is used for training data, or at least that it’s keeping in its central library or that could be generated using the system,” Nelson says. While the Elvis suit involves 274 songs, “if this becomes established precedent in a court ruling, then other lawsuits, other judges, and other pending cases and future cases may come to the same conclusion,” she says. Anthropic, Nelson said, “is a very wealthy company, but when you think about all the copyrighted works that it’s ingesting into its system and potentially generating, I don’t know if they’re going to be able to afford it.”
Have the Elvis rights holders or Anthropic said anything about the suit?
A lawyer for the Elvis rights holders previously said in a statement to NBC News, “The Elvis Plaintiffs bring this action to enforce these exclusive rights against the defendant that has built its entire business model based on copyright infringement at a scale never seen before.”Lead attorney for the plaintiffs Richard Busch also said, “The Elvis Plaintiffs are deeply protective of these iconic songs.” Anthropic previously said in a statement to NBC News, “We disagree with the claims and intend to defend this case fully.” Neither side responded to Vulture’s request for comment.
