TITLE: Early Enteral Nutrition in Severe Pancreatitis Lacks Standardized Clinical Protocols
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive scoping review evaluating seventeen clinical studies across seven nations has revealed that while clinicians widely initiate early enteral nutrition within seventy-two hours for patients suffering from severe or predicted severe acute pancreatitis, implementation protocols remain fragmented by marked variations in timing benchmarks, initial infusion rates, and tolerance monitoring. Published in Frontiers in Nutrition, the systematic synthesis demonstrates that although medical consensus has firmly transitioned away from complete pancreatic rest toward early gastrointestinal support, the practical execution of tube feeding lacks uniform operational standards. Across the investigated literature, feeding regimens displayed substantial heterogeneity in how clinicians defined the initiation window, selected formulas, titrated infusion speeds, and handled signs of gastrointestinal distress. The investigators noted that while initiating enteral nutrition within the first few days of acute illness is now a widely adopted clinical principle, existing published trials provide divergent guidance on how to safely advance and adjust nutritional intake based on real-time physiological tolerance, leaving critical gaps between broad guideline principles and bedside practice.
Acute pancreatitis represents a frequent abdominal emergency where approximately twenty-five percent of presenting individuals advance to severe disease marked by local tissue necrosis, persistent organ dysfunction, and potentially fatal infectious complications. However, administering liquid nutrients directly into the gastrointestinal tract during severe pancreatic inflammation introduces distinct operational difficulties. Patients frequently suffer from severe gastrointestinal dysmotility, increased gastric residual volumes, paralytic ileus, abdominal distension, vomiting, and elevated intra-abdominal pressures. These manifestations of feeding intolerance not only impede the timely achievement of caloric and protein targets but are also linked to heightened rates of adverse secondary outcomes in intensive care settings.
To rigorously map the clinical evidence surrounding this therapeutic window, the research team conducted a formal scoping review following Joanna Briggs Institute guidance and reported in conformity with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews checklist. The authors searched eight major international and regional biomedical databases from their respective inceptions through September 2026, including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, the China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, and the VIP database. Two independent reviewers performed three consecutive screening phases covering article titles, abstracts, and full texts, resolving discrepancies through structured discussion or consultation with a third arbiter. The search yielded seven thousand seven hundred thirteen primary records, which were condensed to four thousand five hundred fifty-five citations following automated duplicate removal. Subsequent screening excluded thousands of non-relevant publications, ultimately isolating seventeen original clinical investigations that met the rigorous Population, Concept, and Context criteria for tube-fed early enteral nutrition initiated within seventy-two hours in adult populations with confirmed or predicted severe disease.
The seventeen included studies, published between 2002 and 2026, captured a cumulative experience spanning randomized controlled trials, prospective controlled investigations, and retrospective cohort analyses with cohort sample sizes ranging from seventeen to two hundred eight patients. Geographically, ten of the investigations originated from China, two from the United Kingdom, and one each from Russia, Sweden, Hungary, the Netherlands, and the United States. Disease classification frameworks across the reviewed literature revealed notable baseline variability. Twelve studies enrolled cohorts described by the primary authors as severe acute pancreatitis, yet only six of these confirmed disease severity using established physiological consensus criteria such as the revised Atlanta classification or standardized Chinese management guidelines. Four studies assigned severe status based solely on admission predictive scoring systems such as APACHE-II or C-reactive protein thresholds, while five explicitly targeted predicted severe disease without requiring documented organ failure.
A critical divergence uncovered by the scoping review centers on the operational definition of early initiation and the prerequisites required before starting tube feeding. Although all seventeen studies delivered enteral nutrition within seventy-two hours of a stated reference point, the reference anchors varied substantially: eleven studies measured eligibility from hospital admission, three from initial symptom onset, two from clinical diagnosis or study enrollment, and one omitted a clear reference benchmark. Within these distinct temporal boundaries, eleven studies initiated nutrition within twenty-four hours, four began feeding between twenty-four and forty-eight hours, and one initiated between forty-eight and seventy-two hours. Furthermore, clinical prerequisites for commencing infusion differed widely. Five studies mandated hemodynamic stability and complete fluid resuscitation, two explicitly screened for and excluded severe contraindications such as intra-abdominal compartment syndrome with pressures exceeding twenty millimeters of mercury, four required radiological confirmation of feeding tube position, and eight commenced feeding immediately following diagnosis or randomization without documenting mandatory physiological prerequisites.
The physical delivery route and nutritional composition of enteral formulas presented further notable practice divisions among the evaluated clinical trials. Nutritional formulas similarly spanned distinct biochemical categories.
Nutritional dosing strategies and advancement patterns demonstrated considerable operational divergence across the reviewed literature, although a conservative baseline philosophy was evident in most centers. Twelve studies initiated feeding at infusion speeds between twenty and thirty milliliters per hour, one used ten to twenty milliliters per hour, one started between fifteen and twenty milliliters per hour, and one utilized forty milliliters per hour. In stark contrast, a single investigation reported an unusually aggressive starting speed of one hundred to one hundred fifty milliliters per hour. Target energy goals were generally aligned around conservative clinical targets, with ten studies establishing full nutritional benchmarks at twenty-five to thirty kilocalories per kilogram of body weight per day.
The assessment of feeding tolerance was identified by the review authors as the single most deficient component across modern enteral nutrition research in severe pancreatitis. Out of seventeen reviewed studies, seven failed to report any objective parameters or clinical criteria used to identify feeding intolerance. Seven studies relied entirely on routine recording of subjective gastrointestinal complaints, such as the emergence of nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, or diarrhea, without providing quantifiable thresholds for therapeutic intervention. Only one trial utilized formal European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism diagnostic criteria to define intolerance, while another developed an institutional scoring checklist based on returning bowel sounds and stool passage. A single contemporary clinical trial incorporated serum biomarkers reflecting intestinal barrier disruption, measuring diamine oxidase, D-lactate, and intestinal fatty acid-binding protein in conjunction with bedside gastric residual volumes. The investigators emphasized that universally accepted, standardized assessment frameworks combining clinical examination with validated intestinal biomarkers remain entirely absent from contemporary clinical literature.
When signs of gastrointestinal intolerance arose, clinical management strategies across the reviewed trials were heterogeneous, reactive, and rarely guided by closed-loop protocols. Documented corrective interventions included temporarily reducing the infusion rate, pausing the feeding pump for several hours, diluting the formula concentration, administering gastrointestinal prokinetic medications such as metoclopramide or erythromycin, adding oral probiotics, and prescribing antidiarrheal agents such as loperamide. In cases of persistent or severe intolerance, clinicians resorted to supplemental parenteral nutrition or converted completely to total parenteral nutrition. Notably, data from an included retrospective study highlighted the clinical gravity of this issue, demonstrating that feeding intolerance occurred in thirty-nine percent of patients and served as an independent predictor of twenty-eight-day mortality, conferring a more than twofold increase in death risk alongside significantly longer hospital and intensive care unit stays and higher rates of systemic infectious complications.
The review authors highlighted multiple methodological and structural limitations within the existing literature that restrict the generalizability of current evidence. Because scoping reviews do not perform formal methodological quality appraisals or risk of bias assessments, the overall synthesized findings may be shaped by underlying weaknesses in the primary reports. Nearly one-third of the included publications utilized retrospective cohort designs, and the preponderance of studies originated from single institutions within China, introducing potential geographic, institutional, and publication biases. Furthermore, small sample sizes, missing implementation variables, heterogeneous tolerance metrics, and the exclusion of non-English and non-Chinese publications limit definitive clinical directives. The researchers concluded that future research must move beyond simply asking whether early feeding is beneficial and instead conduct rigorous, multicenter randomized trials designed to standardize tolerance metrics, evaluate delivery routes, and establish validated, tolerance-guided adjustment pathways capable of delivering truly individualized nutritional therapy to critically ill pancreatitis patients.
