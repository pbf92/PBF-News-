TITLE: CT Scans And X-Rays May Raise Brain Tumour Risk In Children, JAMA Study Finds
OUTLET: Times Now

Medical scans are often among the first investigations doctors recommend when a child suffers a head injury, experiences unexplained symptoms or needs further evaluation. While CT scans and X-rays play an important role in diagnosing medical conditions, new research has raised questions about the potential long-term effects of radiation exposure during childhood.
What The Study Shows
A study published in JAMA on November 19, 2026, involving nearly 3.7 million children from the United States and Canada, found an association between cumulative radiation exposure from medical imaging and an increased risk of brain and central nervous system tumours. Researchers followed 3,704,860 children for an average of 12.3 years, during which 1,536 brain and central nervous system tumours were identified. The findings revealed a dose-response relationship, suggesting that the risk increased with higher cumulative radiation exposure.
Children exposed to cumulative brain radiation doses of 50–75 mGy had an estimated 7.9 additional brain tumours per 10,000 children by age 21, compared with children without measurable brain radiation exposure. The findings highlight why unnecessary or repeated radiation-based investigations in children deserve closer attention.
Explaining what makes children more vulnerable, Dr. Sunny Jain, Clinical Director, Medical Onco-Sciences and Head, Medical Oncology, Marengo Asia Hospital, Faridabad, says, "Children are not simply smaller versions of adults. Their organs and tissues are still developing, and many cells are undergoing active division." Ionising radiation can damage cellular DNA. Although the body has mechanisms to repair such damage, errors during the repair process can sometimes lead to mutations that may contribute to cancer development. Children also have more years ahead of them during which potential radiation-associated effects could emerge.

However, this does not mean parents should avoid medical imaging altogether. Dr. Jain cautions against interpreting the findings as a reason to refuse necessary investigations.
"CT scans remain extremely important in emergency medicine and oncology. In children with significant head injuries, suspected intracranial bleeding, serious infections or suspected malignancies, a CT scan may be necessary to establish the diagnosis quickly." It is also important to understand that CT scans and conventional X-rays do not expose children to the same radiation doses. CT scans generally involve considerably higher radiation exposure, while routine X-rays, such as chest X-rays, deliver much lower doses.
So, What Should Parents Do Before Agreeing To A Scan?
Dr. Jain recommends asking whether the investigation is medically necessary, whether previous imaging reports can be reviewed and whether alternatives such as MRI or ultrasound are suitable. "Every investigation should have a clear medical indication," he emphasises, adding that doctors should follow the ALARA principle, As Low As Reasonably Achievable, to minimise radiation exposure without compromising diagnostic quality.
The takeaway is not to fear medical scans, but to ensure they are prescribed judiciously. When a child's health depends on an accurate and timely diagnosis, the benefits of necessary imaging can outweigh its potential long-term risks.
