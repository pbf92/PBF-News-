TITLE: Can stories about animals and nature help rethink environmental education? Lessons from Japan
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Environmental education has traditionally emphasized scientific knowledge: understanding climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution, and the technologies that might help address these problems. Yet despite decades of scientific advances and growing environmental awareness, environmental challenges continue to intensify. What if addressing these challenges also requires changing how people understand their relationship with the natural world?
A new study published in ECNU Review of Education explores an alternative approach through an illustrative example: stories about animals and nature in Japan's elementary school language textbooks. The research team, comprising Hikaru Komatsu of Doshisha University and Jeremy Rappleye of the University of Hong Kong, suggests that these stories may offer an important educational pathway toward a worldview that recognizes humans as part of nature rather than separate from or superior to it.
The study examines five animistic stories that have gradually become established in Japanese elementary language education since the 1950s. Rather than treating nature simply as a resource for human use, these narratives encourage readers to recognize the dignity of nonhuman beings and the interconnectedness of life.
One example is Grandpa Daizo and a Goose, taught in Grade 5. The story follows an experienced hunter who repeatedly fails to catch a vigilant goose leader. Eventually, he devises a trap using his domesticated goose to lure the wild flock. However, when a falcon attacks, the wild goose leader risks his life to protect the domesticated bird. Moved by the goose leader's courage and compassion, Daizo abandons his hunting plan and takes the injured goose home to nurse him back to health.
The story transforms the hunter's perspective. The goose leader, initially viewed as an obstacle and hunting target, becomes a being worthy of respect. Through this change, students are encouraged to question the assumption that humans are inherently superior to other animals.
Another example, The Life of the Ocean, taught in Grade 6, explores a deeper sense of interdependence. A young fisherman, driven by grief over his father's death, seeks revenge against a giant fish. When he finally encounters the fish, however, he comes to recognize the fish as embodying the life of the ocean. This realization changes his understanding of what it means to be a true fisherman. Rather than killing the fish, he recognizes that his own life is inseparable from the ocean and its living beings.
The research team analyzed the stories, textbook teaching guides, classroom videos, and published accounts of students' reflections. They found that the educational emphasis extends beyond understanding the plot. Teachers encourage students to examine how characters' relationships with nature change, and students' written reflections demonstrate that they recognize these transformations. Some describe animals as possessing courage and dignity comparable to those of humans; others interpret the ocean as encompassing the lives of humans, fish, ancestors, and entire communities.
The research team also investigated how these stories entered Japanese textbooks. Their historical analysis suggests that the change was not initiated by a government policy or a coordinated educational reform. Instead, publishers and teachers gradually introduced and developed the stories from the late 1950s onward, against a backdrop of increasingly visible environmental problems, including Minamata disease. These developments challenged the prevailing belief that humans could independently control nature through science and technology.
The study further examined long-term public opinion surveys conducted in Japan since 1953. The surveys reveal a marked shift from the 1970s onward: increasing numbers of people endorsed the view that humans should be "attuned to nature," while support for the view that humans should dominate nature declined. By around 2000, nearly half of respondents favored the view that humans should be attuned to nature, while only approximately 5% favored the view that humans should dominate it.
The authors do not claim that textbook stories directly caused this societal change. Rather, they suggest that the curricular developments formed part of a broader cultural transformation involving environmental awareness, social movements, and changing public attitudes.
The findings highlight an often-overlooked possibility for environmental education. Cultural change may not require entirely new subjects or specialized environmental programs. Stories already embedded in everyday schooling may provide opportunities for students to reconsider their relationships with other living beings.
The Japanese experience also challenges the assumption that educational change must originate from national policies or academic experts. Publishers, teachers, and local professional communities played central roles in introducing, interpreting, and sustaining these stories.
As environmental challenges increasingly demand reconsideration of humanity's place in the world, this research invites educators internationally to look beyond scientific knowledge and technological solutions—and to explore how everyday educational practices might cultivate a deeper sense of interdependence with the natural world.
