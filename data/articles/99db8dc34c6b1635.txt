TITLE: SiriusXM agrees to human-edited transcripts for 30 of its top-earning shows
OUTLET: PPC Land

SiriusXM, Pandora and Stitcher have agreed to a court-supervised overhaul of their podcast transcripts to settle a disability rights lawsuit that the National Association of the Deaf and four deaf and hard-of-hearing plaintiffs filed in Manhattan federal court in December 2021. The proposed consent decree, filed on November 16, 2026 and announced by the plaintiffs' side on November 17, sets out machine transcripts that name speakers and describe sounds, human editing for 30 shows chosen from the companies' 50 highest-earning podcasts, and $1.5 million in payments, almost all of it legal fees. Nothing in it takes effect until Judge Jeannette A. Vargas signs it.
In Short
SiriusXM and its Pandora and Stitcher services have agreed to settle a five-year lawsuit by transcribing new podcast episodes with labeled speakers, described sounds and proper punctuation, and to have people edit the transcripts of 30 shows picked from their 50 biggest earners. The deal matters to deaf and hard-of-hearing listeners, to the creators whose shows SiriusXM distributes or sells ads in, and to rivals that the National Association of the Deaf hopes will treat the deal as a benchmark. Once a judge signs it, the companies have six months, on a best-efforts basis, to build a new transcript system, and the agreement runs for 36 months under court supervision.
What the settlement requires
The announcement went out on November 17, 2026 from New York as a joint release from Disability Rights Advocates, the nonprofit law firm that represented the plaintiffs, and the National Association of the Deaf. It lists press contacts at both organizations, and Podnews republished it on November 18. According to the release, the settlement takes "the form of a consent decree, which was filed yesterday in the Southern District of New York." The court docket lists the document as a proposed consent order, entry number 141, filed on November 16.
A consent decree is a settlement the court turns into an order, which matters here because the 14-page document is enforceable by the judge for its full term. Its own clock does not start on filing. The decree defines its Effective Date as "the date that this Consent Decree is so-ordered by Judge Jeannette A. Vargas," and every deadline in it runs from that day.
According to the release, transcripts of podcasts "owned or licensed" by SiriusXM and Pandora will include three things that machine transcripts often lack: speaker identification; atmospherics, described as "nondialogue audio sounds like sound effects, laughter, and music"; and "proper punctuation, paragraph breaks, and capitalization." The release adds human cleanup, "the use of a person to review and edit a transcript produced by automatic speech recognition," for "30 podcasts to be selected by Plaintiffs from the top 50 revenue-generating podcasts."
The rest of the package, according to the release, covers custom data dictionaries ("lists of words and phrases used in specific podcasts"), an annual review of transcript quality and of the best available transcription vendor, a style guide developed "with the Plaintiffs' feedback," and staff training "on Deaf culture and communication access."
The decree itself goes considerably further than the release. It names the vendors, sets accuracy floors tied to those vendors, splits podcasts into three commercial tiers and lists the timetable in days.
Obligations and deadlines at a glance
Every date in the table runs from the judge's signature, so the deadlines are scheduled relative to that event rather than fixed to calendar dates.
How the transcript system is meant to work
Read in order, the decree sets out seven stages.
- Machine transcription from day one. From the Effective Date, new episodes of Owned and Licensed podcasts are transcribed with ElevenLabs Scribe v2, which the decree identifies as ElevenLabs' automatic speech recognition model at version 2. Third-Party podcasts go through Rev, a transcription vendor.
- A layer on top of the machine. Within six months, the companies are to build the Transcript Enhancement Service, defined as the software and services that process and improve the raw transcripts, separate speakers, apply speaker labels and format text according to the Style Guide. The obligation is phrased as effort, not guarantee: "Defendants shall use reasonable best efforts to develop and implement the Transcript Enhancement Service within six (6) months of the Effective Date." Extensions are possible for unforeseen circumstances and "shall not be unreasonably withheld," but any extension lengthens the decree.
- A written rulebook. The Style Guide covers formatting, speaker identification, speaker turns, atmospherics, long single-speaker passages, and the handling of stuttering, filler words, curse words and censored words. The plaintiffs comment, the companies explain any suggestion they reject, and the companies keep the final say on wording.
- Humans on 30 shows. Once the enhancement service is running, the decree requires that "Human Cleanup shall be performed on thirty (30) Podcasts selected by Plaintiffs." The selection starts from a list: "Defendants shall provide Plaintiffs with a list of the top fifty (50) revenue-generating English-language Podcasts." The plaintiffs have 30 days to choose. It is a human in the loop arrangement applied to a fixed slice of the catalogue.
- Annual reshuffle. Each year the companies send a fresh top-50 list no later than 60 days before the anniversary of the Effective Date, and the plaintiffs choose 30 again.
- A way out for the humans. If speech recognition improves to the point where the companies conclude Human Cleanup is no longer needed, they may propose ending it, with supporting documentation. The plaintiffs have 30 days to respond, their consent "shall not be unreasonably withheld," and they get no discovery of internal company information for this purpose.
- Complaints and checks. Transcripts or the podcast pages that host them carry a link labeled "Report a problem with the transcript," which leads to a dedicated email address. The same link sits on the SiriusXM help page (siriusxm.com/help), the Pandora help page (help.pandora.com) and the Help pages in both apps. Each year the companies evaluate a sample of transcripts against "word error rate; grammar; Speaker Identification accuracy; the accuracy, labeling, and placement of Atmospherics," plus conformance with formatting rules, and report the results to the plaintiffs' lawyers.
Every transcript must also say how it was made. The decree gives two examples of the label, "by ASR technology with human edits" and "by ASR technology alone," and leaves the exact wording to the companies. Transcripts have to appear on the companies' apps and on siriusxm.com, pandora.com and stitcher.com "in reasonable proximity to the location at which the audio content" plays.
Three tiers of podcasts, three sets of rules
The most commercially revealing part of the decree is how it sorts podcasts. It does not divide shows by genre or popularity but by the contract SiriusXM holds with the creator.
Owned podcasts are those the companies create, produce, own, control and distribute. Licensed podcasts are shows on the services where SiriusXM holds the right to sell ad space under "individually negotiated 'ad rep' agreements with the podcasts' creators." Third-Party podcasts are "any Podcast which Defendants license from third parties pursuant only to a standard licensing agreement," one not individually negotiated with the creator.
The accuracy floors are pegged to each vendor's performance on the Effective Date. If the companies switch vendors, they must keep accuracy, speaker identification, atmospherics and formatting at least at the level ElevenLabs Scribe v2 offered at that point, find a replacement for Rev that is at least as accurate, and tell the plaintiffs about any change.
The release's summary, that "transcripts of podcasts that are owned or licensed" will include speaker identification and atmospherics, matches the decree. Shows that reach SiriusXM through standard licenses get a transcript and an accuracy benchmark, but the decree does not attach the speaker and sound requirements to them in the same way.
What is left out
The decree covers less than a reader of the release might assume. Its definition of a podcast is "prerecorded English-language audio content," limited to "audio on-demand content for which Defendants have the express consent of the content creators to create transcriptions." A show whose creator has not consented is outside the definition. So is live radio: the term "does not refer to audio content as it airs on live radio channels," until that content is made available on demand.
The obligation to transcribe applies to "New Episodes," defined as episodes produced and published on or after the Effective Date. Back catalogues are not covered. Non-English shows are not covered. Video podcasts are not addressed at all, even though SiriusXM's recent podcast deals cover video versions of shows. Mei Nishimoto, one of the plaintiffs, framed podcasts in the release as a source of learning "beyond reading materials and captioned videos," a reminder that closed captions on video and transcripts of audio are separate products governed by separate expectations.
Stitcher appears in the decree as a defendant and as a service, through stitcher.com, even though SiriusXM closed the standalone Stitcher app in August 2023. The defendant's corporate name also differs inside the document: the caption reads Stitcher Media Inc., while the signature block reads Stitcher Media, LLC.
The money
According to the decree, the payments break down as follows.
The decree describes the fee as "a lump sum payment for attorneys' fees, costs, and expenses" covering work through the Effective Date and the monitoring that follows. The four individual plaintiffs receive $20,000 between them, by PPC Land's arithmetic, so damages account for $35,000 of the $1.5 million, about 2.3%.
SiriusXM concedes nothing. The recitals state: "Defendants do not admit wrongdoing or concede liability, nor do they agree that Plaintiffs' allegations have merit." The plaintiffs, for their part, do not concede that their claims lacked merit. In return for the decree, the plaintiffs release claims "related to the accessibility of Defendants' products and services, including the provision of Transcripts," up to the date of the agreement. The release does not stop them from enforcing the decree itself.
Disputes follow a three-step path: a meeting within 30 days of written notice, then the court's own mediation program if the issue is not resolved within 30 days of that meeting, and then a ruling from Judge Vargas if mediation fails within 60 days. The parties will ask the court to keep jurisdiction for the full term.
What the plaintiffs and their advocates said
The release contains no statement from SiriusXM, Pandora or Stitcher. All five quotes come from the plaintiffs and their side.
Amber Martin, a plaintiff, said: "Podcasts are an indispensable media for entertainment and information sharing and it is critical that deaf people have equal access to them. I am glad that Sirius XM will provide high quality transcripts for their podcasts and I look forward to being able to enjoy and recommend them to friends and students."
Mei Nishimoto said: "I am pleased that a settlement has been reached. This expands accessibility for the Deaf community, allowing greater access to podcasts that were previously inaccessible and opening new opportunities for learning beyond reading materials and captioned videos."
James Munro dated the effort to before the filing: "This journey toward podcast accessibility began back in June 2021, and five years later, we're celebrating another important step forward."
