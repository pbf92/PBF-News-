TITLE: Looted Faith: The Church Under Megiddo Prison and the Museum That Won’t Say So
OUTLET: Washington Report on Middle East Affairs

Washington Report on Middle East Affairs, January/February 2027, pp. xx-xx
Special Report
By Susan Kerin
AROUND THE YEAR 230, a Christian woman named Akeptous paid for a table in a small prayer hall in the Jezreel Valley. Her gift was recorded in the floor in tiny colored stones: an inscription dedicating it “to the God Jesus Christ, as a memorial.”
It is one of the oldest declarations of faith in Christ ever found.
Nearly 1,800 years later, Akeptous’ words hang on a wall in Washington, DC, some 6,000 miles from home. The ground where she prayed is now Israel’s infamous Megiddo Prison, where Palestinians, including children, are held without charge and, according to human rights groups, tortured.
The Museum of the Bible calls its display preservation. A growing coalition of Christians calls it erasure.
A READING ON THE MUSEUM’S DOORSTEP
On Oct. 24, 2026, nearly a dozen activists gathered outside the Museum of the Bible to read aloud the part of the mosaic’s story missing from its wall labels.
They came with Heritage Without Erasure, a new interfaith coalition demanding that the museum end its partnership with the Israel Antiquities Authority (IAA), return the mosaic to the Palestinian Christian community and help restore the church from which it was removed.
“Jesus, who declared that he came ‘to proclaim freedom for the prisoners’ and ‘to set the oppressed free’...is the one to whom this church was dedicated,” said Badre Rachidi, one of the organizers of the coalition’s open letter. “This is not preservation. It is theft. It is colonialism.”
Museum President Carlos Campo and his staff came out to hear the activists’ demands. Campo said the mosaic would return to the IAA at the end of the year, when the exhibit closes.
But that answer did not resolve the central question: Who has the right to decide the future of a Christian artifact removed from a church on Palestinian land? The coalition says the museum acknowledged its blind spots but made no firm commitment to end its partnership with the IAA or stop funding its archaeological excavations. The museum’s stated intention to return the mosaic to the IAA, rather than to the Palestinian Christian community, underscores the dispute.
HOW A CHURCH ENDED UP UNDER A PRISON
The church ruins lay on the land of the Palestinian village of Lajjun. In May 1948, Israel’s Golani Brigade ethnically cleansed its roughly 1,280 residents, as part of an ongoing process Palestinians refer to as the Nakba. Today, that land is the location of Megiddo Prison, where Israel has jailed Palestinians since the late 1980s, many in “administrative detention” with no charge and no trial.
In 2005, a dig to clear ground for new cellblocks uncovered the church floor. Israel did not halt the prison’s growth. In 2024, the IAA shipped the mosaic to Washington.
The prison remains. So do the allegations of abuse against Palestinians held there. In its August 2024 report “Welcome to Hell,” the Israeli human rights organization B’Tselem describes a system of torture in Israeli prisons, including Megiddo. Among those held at Megiddo:
- Fouad Hassan, who says a guard broke his ribs after he refused an order to kiss an Israeli flag.
- Abd al-Rahman Mar’i, whom a fellow prisoner said was kicked down about 15 metal stairs while handcuffed. A lawyer in the next cell heard him cry for a doctor for hours, until he fell silent. He died on Nov. 13, 2023, with broken ribs and a broken breastbone.
- Journalist Sami al-Sa’i, who reported that guards laughed as they raped him with objects.
- Marwan Barghouti, the Palestinian leader imprisoned since 2002, whose lawyer reported that guards entered his cell with a dog, forced him to the floor and let it attack him repeatedly.
- Walid Ahmad, 17, who died in the prison in 2025 without ever having been charged. An Israeli doctor who observed his autopsy found extreme malnutrition.
ARCHAEOLOGY AS A WEAPON
The coalition’s Oct. 3 open letter, now endorsed by 34 organizations, lays out how the IAA, the museum’s partner, turns digging into dispossession. The IAA gets a Palestinian site declared an antiquity site, the letter says, then works with the Israeli military to expel the people who live there. Israeli settlers and the state take the land.
In Silwan, in occupied East Jerusalem, the settler group Elad leads the City of David settlement project, and the IAA digs alongside it. Their tunnel runs 3 to 4 meters beneath Palestinian homes in the Wadi Hilweh neighborhood. In 2017, damage from the digging drove 25 residents from their homes.
“Whose voices are being heard, and whose history is being preserved?” asked Mary Hershberger, another of the letter’s organizers. “Museums should stand with those communities, not help legitimize their displacement.”
The museum’s founders, the Green family, own Hobby Lobby, which in 2017 paid a $3 million settlement and forfeited some 5,500 artifacts smuggled from Iraq under false labels. Archaeologist Michael Press wrote in the online publication Hyperallergic that the undocumented antiquities the museum acquires “have often been looted from war zones, where their purchase funds continued violence.”
FOLLOW THE MONEY
Campo says the museum aims to be nonsectarian. Yet it helped launch Passages, a subsidized Israel trip for Christian college students. By the accounts of Steve Green and the program’s director, the idea came from figures tied to Prime Minister Binyamin Netanyahu. Tax filings show the museum gave Passages nearly $2 million in two reported fiscal years. Allen Quine, then a museum vice president on the Passages board and a member of the end-times Pre-Trib Research Center, said in 2017 that the goal was for students to become “supporters and advocates of present-day Israel.” Religious studies scholar Mark Chancey calls it a “Christian Zionist pilgrimage.” Campo noted, however, that the museum has ended its promotion of Passages tours.
The museum also has funded illegal excavations. Its 2016-17 tax filing reports $20,000 for excavating Qumran, in the occupied West Bank. Israel runs Qumran as a national park beside the settlement of Kalya.
THE CHRISTIANS THE MUSEUM FORGOT
Palestinian Christians in Bethlehem, Jerusalem, Nazareth and Gaza trace their faith to its beginnings. “The living Christian communities whose ancestors embraced Christianity in its earliest generations remain largely absent from [the museum’s] story,” said Philip Farah of the Palestinian Christian Alliance for Peace. “These Palestinian Christians are the living stones of the Church.”
Angie Alt, co-leader of Christians for a Free Palestine’s DMV (District, Maryland, Virginia) chapter, compared that absence to her own family’s history. “My great-great grandmother, a member of the Apsáalooke (Crow) tribe, was taken from her family as a child and sent to the country’s first Indian boarding school in Carlisle, Pennsylvania,” she said. “Boarding schools were part of a U.S. government policy of ethnocide. That very same process is being done now to our Palestinian Christian siblings, aided by the Museum of the Bible’s partnership with the Israel Antiquities Authority. We are standing with Palestinians to ensure that their attempt at erasure, of ethnocide, fails.”
WHAT COMES NEXT
In addition to returning the Megiddo stone to the indigenous Palestinian Christian community, the coalition demands that the museum end its IAA partnership and stop funding IAA digs. It also wants the museum to partner instead with Palestinian Christian churches and scholars, so that they more authentically tell the stories of those living communities, including St. Porphyrius Church in Gaza.
Akeptous dedicated her gift to a God who came to set captives free. Today her church lies under a prison full of captives. Until the Museum of the Bible faces that, the mosaic on its wall is not a witness to the faith. It is a trophy of the people who took the land. For more information visit <www.return-the-stones.org>.
