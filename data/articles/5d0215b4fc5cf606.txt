TITLE: OpenAI vs Anthropic AI Limits: 2 Approaches, 0 Verdict
OUTLET: shattered.io

OpenAI and Anthropic both want guardrails on frontier AI, but they do not want the same ones. Computerworld reports that the two companies diverge on who should pull the plug on risky AI, and the split has handed the industry a live test of whether one-size-fits-all limits can work at all.
That question sits underneath the story now circulating as “Why Generic AI Limits Don’t Work: OpenAI vs Anthropic Restrictions Explained.” Each lab publishes its own usage rules, its own high-risk carve-outs and its own view of who holds the off switch. Earlier, our coverage of Axios reporting on Anthropic’s unreleased models showed how little outsiders can verify about what sits behind those rules.
This analysis separates what the primary documents confirm from what only secondary reports claim. The headline’s broad assertion, that generic AI limits do not work, is unconfirmed as a general factual conclusion. So is any ranking of whose restrictions work better. What we can do is lay out the published rules, compare them, and explain why the debate over AI restrictions is moving from broad bans toward targeted controls.
What the Reports Say and What the Record Confirms
Several outlets have carried versions of this story, including 36Kr, The New Voice of Ukraine, Coinpaper, Nacionale, Digg and The Times of India. They agree on the broad outline. OpenAI and Anthropic approach the limits on risky AI differently, and the gap shows up in policy text, in legislative letters and in proposals for how to stop a system that turns unsafe.
The primary record is narrower than the coverage. OpenAI’s usage policies and Anthropic’s Usage Policy are public. Anthropic also publishes separate High-risk Use Case Requirements, and OpenAI submitted a letter dated October 20 that proposed an amendment to California’s SB 53. Those documents are the firm ground. Everything else in the debate, including any comparison of which lab’s limits perform better, rests on reporting and opinion.
We hold the line on that distinction throughout. When a claim comes from a named outlet rather than a company document, we say so. When neither source confirms a detail, we leave it out. The result is less dramatic than the headline, but it gives readers something they can check.
OpenAI’s Usage Policies: A Category List With an Exceptions Door
OpenAI’s published usage policies read as a list of prohibited categories. They cover threats, harassment, self-harm, terrorism, weapons, illicit activity, malicious cyber activity and real-money gambling. They also address certain regulated advice and some national-security, law-enforcement or intelligence uses.
The structure matters. A category list is easy to publish, easy to audit and easy to explain to a customer. It also treats every user in a category the same way, at least on paper. A request that touches malicious cyber activity falls under the same heading whether it comes from an attacker or from a defender testing their own network.
OpenAI acknowledges that tension. The company says it may grant case-by-case exceptions to certain restrictions for approved partners when appropriate safeguards are in place. That clause is the most telling line in the policy for this debate. It admits that a flat rule cannot serve every legitimate user, and it routes the hard cases through a vetting process instead of rewriting the rule.
The published policy does not say how many partners hold exceptions, which restrictions they lift or how safeguards get verified. We cannot fill those gaps without inventing details, so we treat the exception path as a confirmed mechanism with unconfirmed scale.
Anthropic’s Usage Policy and High-Risk Use Case Requirements
Anthropic takes a two-layer approach. Its Usage Policy, with older versions kept in Anthropic’s policy archive, sets the general rules. A separate set of High-risk Use Case Requirements applies to activities that pose elevated risks of harm. The second layer exists because the first one alone does not scale to every deployment.
Anthropic also states that its policies prohibit using Claude for censorship and disinformation. And it says Claude.ai is not offered to users under 18, backed by detection systems that identify and offboard potentially underage users. Those are specific, checkable commitments, though the policy sources we reviewed do not publish enforcement figures.
Then there is Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy. It is described as a voluntary framework that addresses catastrophic risks from advanced AI systems. The word voluntary carries weight here. Nothing in the retrieved sources says an outside body enforces the framework, which is part of why the shutdown-control argument has moved into legislative letters.
Put the two companies side by side and a pattern emerges. OpenAI leads with a category list and an exceptions door. Anthropic leads with a general policy and a tiered requirement set for the dangerous end. Both pieces of architecture concede the same point: a single flat limit cannot cover chat assistants, coding agents and security research tools at once.
Why Generic AI Limits Fall Short in Practice
The title claim, that generic AI limits do not work, remains unconfirmed. No source we reviewed measured the effectiveness of either company’s restrictions. What the documents do show is why a purely generic approach strains under real use, and that is an analytical argument, not a measured result.
Static categories meet dual-use requests
Malicious cyber activity is the clearest example. The same technical knowledge that helps an attacker also helps a defender. A static ban on the category blocks both, while a static allowance helps both. Neither company’s published text resolves that on its own, which explains OpenAI’s partner exceptions and Anthropic’s separate high-risk requirements.
Risk depends on the deployment, not just the model
A chat window and an autonomous agent carry different risks even when the same model sits underneath. Our look at a Black Friday simulation in which AI agents broke the rules illustrates the gap. Rules written for conversation do not automatically govern a system that takes actions, spends money or calls tools.
That is the strongest version of the generic-limits critique. It does not say restrictions fail. It says a restriction designed for one setting leaks or over-blocks in another, and that labs respond by layering exceptions, tiers and approvals on top.
Shutdown Controls and the SB 53 Letter
The sharpest public difference between the labs concerns who can stop a risky system. The statement attributed to OpenAI in its October 20 letter is the cleanest primary evidence. OpenAI wrote: “We support amending SB 53 to require frontier developers to maintain effective shutdown controls and an enforceable duty to use them when unsafe conditions arise, and no lesser intervention would adequately address the risk.”
Read closely, the sentence does three things. It asks for a legal requirement to build shutdown controls. It asks for a duty to use them, not merely to have them. And it argues that nothing weaker would be enough once unsafe conditions arise. That puts the responsibility on the developer.
Computerworld frames Anthropic’s position as a different answer to the same question. We will not attribute specific wording or legal mechanics to Anthropic beyond that framing, because the fact sheet behind this article does not confirm them. What the reporting establishes is that both companies back stronger oversight while disagreeing over who holds the authority to intervene.
For enterprises, the practical reading is straightforward. A shutdown duty placed on developers means contracts, incident plans and vendor reviews will need to name who can stop what. Teams building on third-party models should ask each vendor what controls exist, who can trigger them and what happens to downstream customers when they fire.
The Common Limits Proposal: What Secondary Reports Claim
A second thread in the story involves Jakub Pachocki and a proposed common upper limit. 36Kr’s account of the OpenAI and Anthropic restriction debate places that proposal at the center of the current argument. Claims about Pachocki, a shared cap, pre-training progress data, delayed model training and new OpenAI security requirements appear only in secondary reporting. The official policy sources we retrieved do not confirm them.
We therefore treat the details as reported, not established. Readers should note three gaps. No source we reviewed states what the first limit would be. None says when any limit would take effect. And none shows that a second frontier lab has agreed to it.
The concept still deserves attention because it points in the opposite direction from a single company’s usage policy. A usage policy governs what customers can ask for. A common limit would govern what developers agree not to build or train. Those are different levers, and confusing them muddies the whole debate over AI restrictions.
A measurable common limit would also face a comparability problem. A figure that one lab measures on its own systems does not automatically translate to another lab’s systems. Without agreed measurement, a shared cap risks becoming a statement of intent that no outsider can audit. That is our analysis, not a finding from the sources.
What the Executives Said, From the Verified Quote File
Four statements from named executives frame the debate. Each comes from a public source and appears here exactly as retrieved. None of them confirms the unverified claims above, and none addresses the headline claim directly.
Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI, said in remarks to the United Nations Security Council, published on OpenAI’s site, that the company does not want to build systems where it cannot make “alignment, monitorability, and safety guarantees that we must.” He added that everyone should avoid building such systems. The remark ties restrictions to a capability to monitor, not to a fixed list of banned topics.
Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, said in remarks carried by the Associated Press: “I believe that if slowing down bought us even an extra year or two before models reach critical levels of capability, and we used that time to advance alignment, we could greatly reduce the risk that something goes seriously wrong.” The statement is about pacing, which no usage policy can address.
Jakub Pachocki, Chief Scientist at OpenAI, is quoted by Insurance Journal: “I further propose common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements, that developers are committed to not crossing until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone.” Because this wording reached us through secondary reporting, we flag it as unconfirmed by OpenAI’s official policy pages.
Altman also warned, in comments reported by The Verge, about the opposite failure. Too much restriction, he argued, could leave a small number of companies, people or even one country holding too much power, which he said “also is a way that this can go horribly wrong.” The comment shows the cost side of limits, which the generic-limits debate often skips.
