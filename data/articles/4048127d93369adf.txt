TITLE: Early warning signs predict heart attack and stroke decades ahead
OUTLET: News-Medical

Early warning signs could help doctors predict those at risk of a heart attack or stroke up to 30 years before an event, research suggests.
Scientists identified early patterns in a range of risk factors for cardiovascular disease – conditions affecting the heart and blood vessels – including high blood pressure, diabetes and kidney function.
They also revealed differences in the blood of those who went on to have a cardiovascular event, such as a heart attack or stroke, more than a decade beforehand.
The findings highlight a large window of opportunity to intervene to reduce cardiovascular risk and prevent the development of serious disease, experts say.
Cardiovascular disease kills one in four people in the UK. Traditional risk factors, such as high blood pressure and cholesterol, have been widely linked to the risk of disease. However, previous studies have typically focused on a single risk factor and don't monitor long-term changes.
Other influences, such as kidney function and biomarkers in the blood, could also play an important role in establishing disease risk.
A research team, led by the University of Edinburgh, examined data from more than 100,000 people between 1985 and 2019 to explore the patterns of multiple risk factors in relation to cardiovascular events over time.
The research, funded by the British Heart Foundation, included data from four population studies across the United States, Norway and the United Kingdom.
Systolic blood pressure – the pressure in arteries when the heart beats or contracts – was consistently higher in those who went on to develop cardiovascular disease compared with those who were event-free, starting at 4 mmHg higher three decades before and increasing to 7 mmHg closer to the event.
Patterns were different between sexes throughout life. Younger women had lower blood pressure than younger men, but this switched around the age of 65, when female readings tended to be higher than males.
Researchers also identified important differences by age. Those who experienced a cardiovascular event at a younger age were more likely to have diabetes and a higher body mass index in the decades before the event.
Patterns in blood biomarkers also revealed early differences between those who went on to develop cardiovascular disease compared with those who didn't. Changes in the levels of a protein called NT-proBNP – used to monitor heart failure – could be seen up to 10 years beforehand.
Trajectories of other non-traditional cardiovascular risk factors, including physical activity and kidney function, also differed between individuals with and without cardiovascular events.
Tracking the course of risk factors over time may help to better predict serious cardiovascular events, experts say. Further research should also explore whether incorporating non-traditional risk factors could help to improve the estimation of cardiovascular disease risk, they add.
The study did not look at risk factors by ethnicity, and the team cautions that further research exploring potential differences in risk factor and biomarker trajectories across ethnic groups is needed.
The study is published in the journal JAMA Network Open: https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.45690?guestAccessKey=3b18e5dc-b122-4634-a5df-ada725268605 [URL will become active after embargo lifts].
I am excited about the findings of our study as they show that modifiable risk factors begin to diverge 30 years before a cardiovascular event occurs. This highlights a great opportunity to develop early prevention strategies throughout the life course, utilizing the patterns of established and emerging risk factors and biomarkers to reduce future cardiovascular events."
Dr Dorien Kimenai, BHF Research Fellow, University of Edinburgh's Usher Institute
Professor Bryan Williams, chief scientific and medical officer at the British Heart Foundation, said: "For the vast majority of people, heart attacks and strokes are completely preventable. But many of us don't realize we are at risk until it's too late.
"This study adds to growing evidence that red flags for cardiovascular disease appear decades before it arrives. That could lead to a simple blood pressure reading, or proteins in the blood, being used to forewarn people of their future cardiovascular risk.
"Knowledge like this could empower people to make lifestyle changes or start treatment while they are still relatively young, which might reduce their risk, prevent a heart attack or stroke, and rewrite their future.
"For now, it is worth remembering that we can all find out our risk of cardiovascular disease, for example by 'knowing your numbers' and getting your blood pressure and cholesterol checked, or having a free NHS health check with your GP in midlife for those living in England."
