TITLE: Novel platform reveals new metabolic targets for cancer treatment
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Cancer cells are hungry beasts. To fuel growth and division, tumors rewire a cell’s metabolism, diverting nutrients into the energy and raw materials they need to survive. But not all cancers have the same metabolic needs, and a new study reveals how different cancer-causing mutations can shape metabolism even within the same organ. The findings, published in Science, identify distinct metabolic vulnerabilities in liver tumors that could open new avenues for treatment.
Uncovering these metabolic vulnerabilities requires a new approach to studying the inner workings of tumors. "Technology is what made this study possible,” says Gökhan Ünlü, of Kıvanç Birsoy's Laboratory of Metabolic Regulation and Genetics at Rockefeller. "To get around a longstanding bottleneck in the field, we had to develop a platform that allows us to look directly inside cancer cell organelles in a living organism."
Early findings already suggest possible paths to more personalized medicine and strategies for improving existing therapeutics, such as immunotherapy.
Obstacles to studying cancer metabolism
Scientists have long known that cancer cells rewire their metabolism to survive, and that these changes largely depend on where a cancer develops. Two tumors driven by the same genetic mutation, one in the lung and one in the pancreas, can have very different metabolic needs. But even within the same organ, different cancer-causing mutations can shape how tumors obtain and use energy. For instance, in the liver, several genetically distinct and particularly difficult to treat cancers can take hold.
One obstacle to better understanding such distinctions has been the difficulty of homing in on metabolism in tumors, which contain a mixture of cancerous and healthy cells. Additionally, conventional methods for isolating mitochondria, the cell's central metabolic hubs, can take hours, during which the molecules that researchers are trying to investigate may change or degrade. Moreover, Birsoy’s group wanted to know not just what makes each tumor distinct, but which of those differences actually matter for the cancer cell's survival, a significantly more complex question.
"Most metabolic studies simply identify what goes up or down—they catalog biomarkers," Ünlü says. "Our goal was to develop a method to uncover truly actionable vulnerabilities."
New techniques
Birsoy, Ünlü, and colleagues overcame these hurdles by adapted a previously established technique to rapidly isolate mitochondria specifically from cancer cells. They used this system that introduced cancer-causing genes into mouse liver cells alongside a molecular tag that attached to their mitochondria. Because the tag was linked to the cancer genes, only the tumor cells produced tagged mitochondria, allowing researchers to separate them from healthy cells. And using magnetic beads that bind to the tag, the team could extract these mitochondria from tumor tissue in minutes, before their chemical contents could change or degrade.
With this new technique, the researchers then generated six genetically distinct types of liver tumors in mice and analyzed the proteins and metabolites within their mitochondria using mass spectrometry. Comparing these molecular profiles allowed them to pinpoint metabolic differences associated with specific cancer-causing mutations. They then tested whether these differences represented vulnerabilities that could be exploited to slow tumor growth.
The effort drew on collaborations across Rockefeller, particularly with the Proteomics Resource Center, and at other institutions across the United States and abroad. "Rockefeller has been an incredibly supportive, collaborative community," says Ünlü, who will start his own cancer biology lab at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center this December. "Kıvanç encouraged me from day one to try to turn a technical bottleneck into a powerful discovery platform."
New findings
Together, the team found that tumors driven by the MYC gene depended heavily on oxidative phosphorylation, the process by which cells use oxygen to generate energy, and that blocking this pathway indeed slowed their growth. Tumors driven by the KRAS gene, on the other hand, relied on creatine metabolism and, as predicted, disrupting this pathway slowed tumor growth, while supplementing mice with creatine accelerated tumor growth and could shorten survival.
Most importantly, they also uncovered an unexpected link between metabolism and the immune system. In MYC-driven tumors that had also lost the tumor suppressor gene PTEN, cancer cells produced large amounts of a particular membrane lipid. This altered lipid buildup essentially acted as an armor, reducing the availability of receptors that sense inflammatory death signals and shielding the cancer cells from being destroyed by immune cells. When the team blocked the production of this lipid armor, this restored the immune system's ability to attack the tumor.
Together, the findings point toward a more personalized approach to treating cancer, in which therapies target the particular metabolic vulnerabilities created by a tumor's genetic mutations. They also suggest that disrupting cancer metabolism could do more than slow tumor growth directly: it might make tumors more susceptible to existing treatments, including immunotherapy.
"There’s real power in trying to make an existing therapy more effective, or hitting the tumor directly through its metabolic dependencies,” says Ünlü. "In liver cancer, where patients have had few new options for decades, combining these approaches gives us a way forward."
Beyond these potential treatments, the study gives researchers a new technique that can be used to investigate cancer metabolism. This method could be adapted to study other cellular structures, including lysosomes, which break down and recycle cellular materials, and the endoplasmic reticulum, which helps produce and process proteins. It could also help determine whether the metabolic vulnerabilities identified in liver cancer extend to tumors in other organs.
“Among all the metabolic pathways within cellular compartments, we are particularly excited about lipids,” says Birsoy. “We have about 20,000 genes, but tens of thousands of different lipids, many of which are influenced by diet. Yet we still know surprisingly little about how these lipids affect cancer cells. This is now the second time we have uncovered a vulnerability linked to lipid metabolism, first in pancreatic cancer and now in liver cancer. We believe exploring this largely uncharted area of biology will reveal many more opportunities to target cancer, including ways to improve existing therapies.”
