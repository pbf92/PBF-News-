Study Finds Urticarial Lesions Dominate Yao Syndrome
A systematic review of 470 genetically confirmed patients maps the skin findings and treatment responses in this rare autoinflammatory condition.
Updated on Nov. 25, 2026 in Life Sciences
A systematic review published in JAMA Dermatology found urticarial-spectrum lesions accounted for 83.6% of classifiable skin findings in genetically confirmed Yao syndrome. The review pooled 32 observational studies from seven countries covering 470 patients.
Why it matters
Yao syndrome is rare and often takes years to diagnose, with the review reporting a weighted mean of 9.18 years between symptom onset and diagnosis. Recognizing the dominant skin pattern may help clinicians consider the condition sooner.
The review reports an 83.6% urticarial-spectrum share among 171 classifiable patients and a 9.18-year weighted mean diagnostic delay, with glucocorticoids showing an 83.9% overall response rate. Because every included study was observational and only cases with clinical images could be reclassified, these figures cannot yet confirm the best treatment choice.
The players
JAMA Dermatology
JAMA Dermatology is a peer-reviewed medical journal published by the American Medical Association that covers clinical and research topics in skin disease.
McGill University
McGill University is a public research university in Montreal, Canada, whose researchers contributed to the review as the lead author's institution.
The details
Researchers reviewed 32 studies covering 470 patients with genetically confirmed Yao syndrome and at least one skin or mucocutaneous manifestation. A board-certified dermatologist reclassified findings into predefined morphologic categories from published clinical images, and spongiotic dermatitis appeared in 32.7% of 52 biopsied patients.
Timeline
- Earliest included studies were published in 2011.
- Literature search cutoff was November 26, 2025.
- The review was published online in JAMA Dermatology on November 25, 2026.
The Big Picture
This pooled analysis consolidates years of scattered case reports on Yao syndrome, a rare autoinflammatory disorder, into a single morphologic and treatment-response profile. It gives dermatologists a clearer reference for a condition whose diagnosis has historically lagged by nearly a decade.
For people with long-standing unexplained rashes or hives, the review underlines how long diagnosis can take in this condition. Clinicians may be more likely to consider genetic testing when urticarial-type skin findings persist alongside other symptoms.
The takeaway
Persistent rashes that come and go can point to rare inflammatory conditions, so raising symptom history with a dermatologist may shorten the search. Keep a record of when skin changes appear and what treatments have helped.
