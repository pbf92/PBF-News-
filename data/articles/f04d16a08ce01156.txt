TITLE: Pew: Christians Will Be Minority in North America by 2070, Will Double in Africa
OUTLET: Christianity Today

Christianity in the West will continue to decline, a new Pew Research Center report projects, as disaffiliation from the faith outpaces new Christians.
Pew estimates that in the next 50 years, Christians will be a minority in North America, while the population of sub-Saharan African Christians more than doubles.
By 2070, the Democratic Republic of the Congo will have surpassed the US as the country with the largest population of Christians.
Christians will account for 46 percent of the North American population by 2070 and 52 percent of Europe’s population, Pew says. Europe’s slightly higher share of Christians in that time will be the result of migration, though some European countries will see a steeper decline in Christianity compared to the US.
Meanwhile, in sub-Saharan Africa, the Christian population will more than double, to a total of 1.6 billion, and will account for 54 percent of total Christians globally.
Pew’s latest report, “The Future of World Religions,” maps out the likeliest changes in global religious identity based on an analysis of recent surveys. Drivers of that change are fertility rates, global migration, and religious switching.
“In the United States, we have seen over the last few decades a decline in the Christian share,” Conrad Hackett, associate director of research at Pew, said in a news conference. “But we’re projecting that globally there will be an increase in the number of Christians … with growth in sub-Saharan Africa offsetting declines in Europe and in North America.”
The ascent of Christianity in sub-Saharan Africa is all but guaranteed, mostly due to fertility rates, according to Pew’s report. Christianity’s pronounced decline in the US and in other Western nations, particularly in Spain and Germany, are largely a result of religious switching.
“The global change in what one might think of as the center of gravity in Christianity is interesting,” Alan Cooperman, director of religion research at Pew, said in a news conference. “You take the trends we see now and take them all the way out to 2070, and the distribution of Christians around the world looks very different from the way it does today.”
Hints of this coming shift are already happening. The Pew report comes as Western churches’ relationships to their African counterparts are changing.
African churches historically tied to large Western denominations are now seeking more independence. The Catholic Church, Anglican Communion, and United Methodist Church have all recently wrestled with ideological and cultural disagreements—often over LGBTQ rights or women’s roles—between adherents in African countries versus the church leadership in the US or UK.
Within the Anglican Church, an alliance of churches in Africa took a major step this year toward independence from the Archbishop of Canterbury and are examining whether to fully sever those ties and appoint a rival leader. Some conservative Anglican churches in North America that broke away from the Episcopal Church in the last decade placed themselves under the leadership of African Anglicans.
The decline of Western missions to Africa—in terms of financial giving and missionaries—comes as church numbers shift to the Global South. Last year, the Presbyterian Church (USA)’s foreign mission board closed. In the face of the Western decline in missions budgets, the Southern Baptist Convention recently amended its yearly allocations to give the International Mission Board a higher percentage of the denomination’s collective budget.
But the center of missions is also now shifting instead of disappearing: an increasing number of missionaries now are from non-Western countries like Brazil and South Korea.
This research analysis is a relatively new endeavor for Pew. Four years ago, Pew released its first major projections for US religious identity through 2070. It said the share of US adults who are Christian would drop below 50 percent due to religious switching—when a person leaves the religious tradition in which they were born and joins a different religion or no religion at all.
Last year, Pew reported signs that the share of nonreligious Americans is plateauing.
But that slight, albeit significant, shift does little to derail the 50-year trajectory for Christianity’s drop in the US.
“If we were to (hypothetically) shut off the spigot of religious switching, which by and large has brought people from Christianity into the unaffiliated category … if we were to say, ‘No more of that’ all the way until [2070], we would still project a decline in the share of Christians,” Cooperman said.
