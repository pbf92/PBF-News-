TITLE: There Will Still Be Video Games After GTA 6
OUTLET: Polygon.com

Grand Theft Auto 6 is upon us: the biggest game launch of all time, the event that has been regarded for years by the rest of the game industry as a herd of diplodocuses might regard the asteroid that was about to kill them all — if the diplodocuses were also really looking forward to the asteroid, and some of them were even hoping the asteroid would double the crop of edible leaves in their area.
Look, the analogy doesn't really work, but that's kind of my point. A certain kind of doomsayer has presented GTA 6 as, metaphorically, the Last Video Game, an extinction-level event, a monumentally excessive fiddle solo performed by Nero amid a burning Rome. The business and culture of blockbuster AAA video games is unsustainable and visibly dying, goes the thinking, just as the ultimate exemplar of that kind of production is released. Beyond that, the entire industry is facing a series of existential threats, including both the generative AI boom and the shortage of affordable computer chips caused by the generative AI boom.
I'm sympathetic with this view, up to a point. I'm not about to deny the challenges facing the game industry right now, the AAA segment in particular. And the juxtaposition of GTA 6 against this landscape certainly looks like an appealing narrative to my journalist brain.
But I do think we've all got a bit carried away. GTA 6 is a gigantic cultural event, and it can feel like it's blotting out the sun. But I'm sure even its creators at Rockstar Games would admit that it is, in the end, just another video game. And once the dust has settled, we'll probably find a games industry and culture that are still there, still buzzing, and worse off in some ways but better off in others.
For starters, it's fair to ask how AAA can be unsustainable if GTA 6 — which has been in production for at least eight years at an estimated cost of a billion dollars — is clearly sustainable? Some calculations suggest it has broken even on pre-orders alone.
The counterargument is that GTA is a unicorn, the only franchise that can pull this off, but that strikes me as only half true. In the next two or three years we'll see a couple of similarly expensive and long-gestating projects that are also surefire winners: The Witcher 4 and The Elder Scrolls 6: Sentinel. Maybe they're one rung down the ladder from GTA, but only one.
You can call these AAAA games, if you must. They're massive releases in much-loved series from studios with enough power and independence to make us wait, preserving the sense of event. They are far too scarce to build an industry on, but they exist, and I believe GTA 6 will only increase gamers' appetite for these mega-games as the diet of "regular" AAA games supplied by the industry gets thinner and thinner.
Regarding the AAA drought of 2026, it's also worth asking if GTA 6 was the symptom or the cause. We all commented on the unseemly pileup of games in September as they tried to stay out of its blast radius. But the really big fish went to early 2027 instead: God of War Laufey, Fable, Final Fantasy 7 Revelation, Tomb Raider: Legacy of Atlantis, and Mass Effect spiritual successor Exodus will all be out within six months of GTA 6. If anything, the first half of 2027 looks unusually bustling.
This might just be a quirk of scheduling, or an aftershock of the GTA earthquake. It's pointless to pretend that AAA development hasn't drastically slowed down and got more risky. It's also reasonable to suggest that some of these games will do well, surfing on the wave of new and returning players attracted by GTA 6.
More importantly, if we're going to characterize GTA 6 as the end of an era, then we should note that eras end for a reason, and new things grow in their wake. AAA projects are struggling because publishers' calculus that big franchise plus big budget equals zero risk no longer holds true. In an intensely competitive media landscape that includes evergreen live-service games, social media, and streaming video, half of these conservative behemoths are simply not compelling enough to cut through. But other things are cutting through: "niche" AA productions like Clair Obscur: Expedition 33, weird social games like Meccha Chameleon and Tomodachi Life: Living the Dream, "triple-I" indies like Hollow Knight: Silksong.
People are not going to stop playing video games just because they've become expensive to make, or because it costs too much to upgrade the graphics card in their PCs. As a rule, artforms don't die when they're at their most popular. That kind of judgment is the result of capitalist logic that equates a lack of constant growth with death, when what it actually represents is a maturing.
Games will persist. They will change, and some of those changes will be healthy and necessary. I don't think GTA 6 will be the last of its kind, if only because Rockstar Games isn't going to disband as soon as it's finished. But even if it were, it would just signal the start of a new evolution. And we'd have plenty to play in the meantime.
