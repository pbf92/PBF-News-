TITLE: UK privacy watchdog keeps its Meta correspondence on Muse AI secret
OUTLET: PPC Land

The UK's data protection regulator is in ongoing contact with Meta about Muse, the company's AI agent, and holds correspondence between the two. It will not release any of it. That is the core of a freedom of information response dated October 23, 2026, sent by the regulator's Information Access Team to a requester whose name does not appear in the seven-page letter.
In Short
The UK's privacy regulator says it is in ongoing contact with Meta about Muse, Meta's AI agent, and holds correspondence between the two, but it refused to release that correspondence or any of its own internal analysis. The request behind the letter asked how AI companions and gadgets such as Meta's pocket device, Muse Charm, might affect children, including autistic children, under the UK's Children's Code. The answer confirms the work exists and is unfinished, so nothing about the regulator's thinking is public yet, and the person who asked has until mid-December to request a review.
What the regulator said
The response carries the reference IC-573504-M9R6. It answers a request that arrived on September 26, 2026, and it was handled under the Freedom of Information Act 2000, the law that lets anyone ask a UK public body for the information it holds.
The request was detailed. It asked for any correspondence, meetings or discussions with Meta about "Meta Muse Charm, Muse AI, or similar AI companion/wearable products." It asked for anything Meta had supplied on compliance with the Children's Code. It asked for impact assessments and risk assessments. And it asked, in four separate ways, whether anyone had considered how these products might affect autistic children, neurodivergent children or children with special educational needs and disabilities, a group usually shortened in Britain to SEND.
The regulator split its answer into three parts. It confirmed that it holds information for parts 1, 2, 5 and 7 of the request. It would "neither confirm nor deny" whether it holds anything for parts 3, 4 and 6. It released nothing at all.
One sentence carries most of the news value: "We are currently engaging with Meta regarding Muse AI, and we hold correspondence between Meta and the ICO on this topic." The letter does not say when that engagement began, which side started it or what it is about.
Part 2 deserves a closer look. It asked for "Any information provided by Meta concerning compliance with the Children's Code in relation to Muse Charm or Muse AI." By confirming it holds information within the scope of that part, the regulator has in effect confirmed that Meta has given it some material touching on Muse and the code. What that material says, and whether it concerns Muse Charm, the Muse agent or both, stays private.
The seven questions and the answers
The table records each part of the request, what the regulator said about it and the legal reason it gave.
Part 5 listed seven areas where the requester thought these products might affect children differently. They were social communication and interaction; understanding and interpreting social cues; emotional attachment or dependency; perceiving an AI as a friend or social being; trusting information or responses from an AI; substituting AI interaction for contact with other people; and understanding the difference between simulated empathy or friendship and a human relationship. The requester added a specific worry: whether any of these issues "may present different or heightened risks for autistic children or children with other communication and developmental needs."
The regulator narrowed one phrase before answering. It read "these types of products" in part 5 to mean "companion/wearable products where AI is the core feature." On that reading, it said it holds some relevant information.
Why nothing was released
Two separate legal routes kept every document back. Both are written in dense statutory language, so a plain explanation helps.
The confidentiality rule
The first route runs through section 44 of the Freedom of Information Act. It exempts information whose release is banned by another law. Here, the other law is section 132 of the Data Protection Act 2018, a confidentiality duty on the regulator's own staff. The letter quotes it in full. A person who is or has been the Commissioner, a member of staff or an agent "must not disclose information which-
(a) has been obtained by, or provided to, the Commissioner in the course of, or for the purposes of, the discharging of the Commissioner's functions,
(b) relates to an identified or identifiable individual or business, and
(c) is not available to the public from other sources at the time of the disclosure and has not previously been available to the public from other sources, unless the disclosure is made with lawful authority."
In practice, that means what a company tells the regulator during its work stays confidential unless one of a short list of legal exceptions in section 132(2) applies. According to the letter, none did. The regulator added that the protection "also extends to correspondence and discussions which would reveal information obtained by the ICO in the exercise of those functions."
Neither confirm nor deny
For parts 3, 4 and 6, the regulator went a step further and would not say whether the documents exist. Why keep even that quiet? Section 44(2) allows it when a simple yes or no would itself reveal protected information. Saying that Meta had sent a risk assessment, for example, would reveal something about what Meta had supplied. The letter is explicit that its silence proves nothing either way: "Please note, this response should not be taken as confirmation that the information you have requested does or does not exist."
Protecting unfinished work
The second route covers the regulator's own thinking, in parts 5 and 7. Section 31(1)(g) applies where release "would, or would be likely to, prejudice" a public body's functions. The letter names two purposes from section 31(2): "(a) the purpose of ascertaining whether any person has failed to comply with the law" and "(c) the purpose of ascertaining whether circumstances which would justify regulatory action in pursuance of any enactment exist or may arise."
Unlike section 44, this exemption is not absolute. The regulator had to weigh the public interest. It listed four arguments for release, including transparency about its work on emerging AI and "public understanding of the privacy risks and safeguards associated with AI products that may be accessible to children." It listed five arguments against, including "a strong public interest in protecting the integrity of ongoing regulatory and supervisory work."
The deciding passage is candid about the state of that work. "This is a newly emerging area of regulatory work. Some of the information remains in draft form and reflects developing analysis and assessment," the letter says. Release now "would reveal aspects of the ICO's emerging thinking before that work has concluded." The regulator concluded that keeping the material private outweighed release "at this time." Those three words leave room for a different answer later.
The letter also draws a line that matters for anyone reading it closely. The section 31 reasoning applies to the regulator's general work on AI companions and wearables. It does not say that an investigation into Meta is open. The confirmed fact about Meta is narrower: engagement and correspondence about Muse.
How the request was handled
The letter and its dates describe a standard process, in four steps.
- Request received. The request arrived on Saturday, September 26, 2026, and was handled under the Freedom of Information Act 2000.
- Answer issued. The reply is dated Friday, October 23, 2026. By PPC Land's count, that is the 20th working day after receipt, which is the time limit the Act sets for most replies.
- Review. The requester can ask the regulator to review its answer, in writing and "in a clear and concise manner," within 40 working days. The letter says legal terms and citations of legislation are not needed. By PPC Land's calculation, 40 working days from October 23 runs to Friday, December 18, 2026.
- Complaint. If a review does not satisfy the requester, the next step is a complaint to the same regulator, acting in its other role as the body that oversees freedom of information. According to the letter, such a complaint "will be handled just like a complaint made to the ICO about any other public authority."
That means the body that refused the request would also rule on any complaint about the refusal.
A regulator with a new name
The letter also captures a regulator in the middle of a change of identity. The request reached the Information Commissioner's Office on September 26. Four days later, on September 30, 2026, a new body called the Information Commission took over the ICO's functions, according to a UK government announcement that day. The government said the change did not alter the regulator's role, responsibilities or powers. The new body is led by interim chief executive Paul Arnold and seven non-executive members, with recruitment of a chair still under way.
The October 23 letter shows the halfway point. Its footer and sign-off say "Information Commission," while the body text refers to "the ICO" throughout and the letterhead keeps the line "The ICO exists to empower you through information." The head office address given is 4th Floor, No.3 Circle Square, 5 Hawkshaw Street, Manchester. This article uses "the regulator" and "the ICO" for the same organisation.
The AI toys project
One paragraph in the letter goes beyond Meta. "The ICO's Regulatory Sandbox currently has a project which involves conversational AI toys for children," it says. A regulatory sandbox is a programme in which companies work directly with a regulator on new products, so that data protection questions are settled before or during launch rather than after a complaint. Further information about the toy project was withheld under section 44.
The letter pointed to the regulator's "Current projects" page for a summary but did not name the company. That page, last updated on November 2, 2026, lists four projects: Truzy Limited, Tribela, IDUN Technologies and the Metropolitan Police Service. None of them is a toy maker. On November 6, MLex reported that the Information Commission had worked through its sandbox with Zebbingo, which makes a screen-free AI toy for children aged five to 11, on consent and on how the toy explains its data use to parents and children. The letter does not confirm that Zebbingo's is the project it describes, although the descriptions match.
What Muse and Muse Charm are
Muse is Meta's consumer AI agent, a system that can carry out tasks such as shopping on a user's behalf. Meta released it in the United States on September 8, 2026, through its own apps, muse.ai and WhatsApp. The request and the letter call it "Muse AI"; Meta calls it Muse.
Muse Charm is a separate piece of hardware. Meta unveiled it at its Connect event on September 23, 2026, describing it as a device that fits in a pocket and lets people talk to and interact with their Muse through a real-time voice model. Meta said it would have more to share later in the year. Technology outlets that saw it, including Tom's Guide, described a device roughly the size of an earbuds case, with a small touchscreen showing an animated character, cameras and a mobile data connection. Several compared it to the Tamagotchi, the 1990s digital pet. Press reports put shipping in December. None of them gave a price, and Meta's own announcement named neither a price nor the countries where the device will be sold.
On October 21, two days before the letter, Mark Zuckerberg told analysts that Muse Charm would move from idea to a device in people's pockets within a year. He gave no launch date.
