TITLE: Dining out would be the first cut for 29% of Americans, Edible poll finds
OUTLET: PPC Land

Restaurants, not birthday parties, sit first in line when American households go looking for savings, according to research Edible Arrangements published today. Asked which spending category they would cut first if they needed to reduce expenses, 29% of 2,003 US adults surveyed by The Harris Poll selected dining out or takeout. Just 5% chose celebrations.
The findings come from the first annual How America Celebrates Report, a 15-page document produced by The Harris Poll on behalf of Edible Brands for Edible Arrangements, the Atlanta company's fruit-arrangement and food-gifting business. The report was released today from Atlanta, as the holiday season gets under way. Looking 12 months ahead, 52% of respondents expect to reduce spending on dining out or takeout to ease financial pressure, compared with 30% who anticipate cutting back on celebrations. And 84% say it remains important to keep celebrating birthdays, milestones, holidays and personal achievements even when money is tight.
That is a tidy result for a company that sells chocolate-dipped strawberries, dessert boards and floral arrangements. The report is unusually direct about its own limits, though. According to Edible Arrangements, the findings "reflect stated attitudes, spending intentions and self-reported behavior," and the survey "did not track purchases or behavior over time, forecast sales or establish what caused reported changes."
In Short
A Harris Poll survey of 2,003 US adults, commissioned by Edible Arrangements and released today, found that 29% would cut dining out or takeout first if they had to spend less, against 5% who would cut celebrations first. For restaurants, gifting brands and the advertisers selling into the holiday season, it adds to a run of 2026 surveys showing households trimming everyday spending while trying to keep certain occasions intact, although 30% still expect to spend less on celebrations over the next year. The numbers describe what people say they would do, from a sponsor with a commercial stake in celebrations, not what they have actually bought.
The figures
All figures below are shares of US adults aged 18 and older, from the Harris Poll fieldwork of October 1-4, 2026, as published in the report and the accompanying release.
How the survey was run
The Harris Poll conducted the survey online within the United States from October 1 to October 4, 2026, among 2,003 adults aged 18 and older, according to the report's methodology page. The four-day field period closed more than seven weeks before publication.
The precision statement is given in Harris's standard form. "The sampling precision of Harris online polls is measured using a Bayesian credible interval. For this study, the sample data is accurate to within plus or minus 2.7 percentage points using a 95% confidence level," the methodology reads. It adds that "this credible interval will be wider among subsets of the surveyed population of interest." No subgroup results by age, income or region appear anywhere in the 15 pages.
Two of the report's footnotes matter for anyone reading the numbers side by side. The 56% who prefer celebrating at home and the 38% who say they actually do so more often "come from two separate survey questions," the report states. The giving and receiving preferences, both at 73%, "were measured separately." The question on the best ways to make a celebration special allowed respondents to "choose up to three" answers, which is why those shares sum to more than 100%.
Dining out takes the first hit
The headline comparison is lopsided. By PPC Land's calculation, respondents were 5.8 times as likely to name dining out or takeout as their first cut as they were to name celebrations. Over the 12-month horizon the gap is 22 percentage points, 52% against 30%.
What the report does not show is the rest of that first-cut question. Dining out and celebrations together account for 34% of answers, according to PPC Land's arithmetic on the published figures, which leaves roughly two thirds of respondents choosing some other category, or none. The report does not list those other categories, so its statement on page 12 that dining out or takeout "was the most selected first cut" cannot be checked against the full distribution of answers.
The dining category itself is broad. It combines eating out with takeout, so it spans everything from QSR drive-throughs and delivery apps to table-service restaurants, and the survey does not separate them. Restaurant advertisers therefore cannot read from it which part of the trade is most exposed.
Nor are celebrations exempt. "They also underscore that celebrations are not immune to budget pressures, with nearly one-third anticipating reduced spending in the category," the release states. The report's own wording is careful on the point: "Rather than demonstrating that celebration spending will remain unchanged, the research offers insight into how consumers say they are balancing financial pressures with the importance of recognizing meaningful moments."
The importance question tells a similar story once it is unpacked. The 84% who call celebrating important when money is tight splits into 49% who say very important and 35% who say somewhat important. Some 16% say it is not very or not at all important.
At home, shared, and at short notice
Beyond spending, the report sketches where and how people say they celebrate. Nearly three in four, 72%, report at least one change in how they celebrate compared with a few years ago. The report lists two of those changes: 38% celebrate special occasions at home more often, and 30% focus more often on creating experiences or memories. Separately, 56% say they are more likely to prefer celebrating at home than they were a few years ago.
Shared experiences dominate the answers on what makes an occasion special. Spending quality time with family and friends was selected by 59%, creating a memorable experience by 38%, and sharing food, treats or a meal by 35%. On gifts, 73% prefer giving something that can be shared or experienced together over a traditional gift, and the same proportion express that preference when receiving.
Everyday occasions get their own chapter. About two in three respondents, 67%, agree that they often celebrate personal achievements, good news and informal gatherings, and 42% agree that last-minute celebrations seem more frequent than a few years ago. The release itself frames that second figure as "reflecting how respondents perceive changing celebration habits" - a perception, not a count.
The final set of questions turns operational. When arranging food for a last-minute celebration, 94% rate quality as very or somewhat important, 89% say the same of reliable fulfillment and 86% of convenience, including ease of ordering. Those three attributes map closely onto Edible Arrangements' own model, which according to the company combines digital ordering with a network of local stores.
The sponsor and its framing
Edible Brands is based in Atlanta and describes itself as a company that "acquires, develops and manages a world-class portfolio of consumer brands renowned in the hospitality and foodservice sectors." Its portfolio includes Edible Arrangements and Rōti Modern Mediterranean, a restaurant brand, according to the company's boilerplate. The report does not discuss what its dining-out finding might mean for that restaurant business.
Somia Farid Silber, chief executive of Edible Brands, explained the reason for the study in the report's opening letter. "At Edible Arrangements, we wanted to better understand how people are approaching these decisions and what makes celebrations meaningful today. That's why we commissioned The Harris Poll to conduct our inaugural How America Celebrates Report. One finding stood out," the letter reads, before citing the 29% and 5% figures.
In the release, Farid Silber put the result in commercial terms. "When people are watching their spending, they make choices about where to save, and celebrations usually aren't the first place they look," Farid Silber said. "From a gathering at home to a small personal win, the occasions may look different, but the desire to connect comes through clearly. For Edible Arrangements, that's a valuable perspective as we continue thinking about how gifting fits into people's lives, beyond traditional holidays and major milestones."
The report's letter closes on a pull quote attributed to Farid Silber: "The way people celebrate may change, but the importance they place on celebrating remains."
Page 14, headed "What the findings mean for businesses," groups the data under five labels: celebrations matter, even on a tighter budget; celebration habits are evolving; shared experiences add value; everyday moments matter; and quality and convenience are key. The page notes that these are "implications informed by The Harris Poll research on behalf of Edible Brands."
What the report says it does not show
The caveats run through the document rather than sitting in a footnote. On the spending questions, page 12 states: "These findings reflect future spending intentions and hypothetical choices rather than verified purchasing behavior." The executive summary adds that "the research reflects self-reported behaviors, preferences and spending intentions rather than actual purchasing activity or measured changes in consumer behavior over time."
The methodology page is the most specific. "Comparisons with a few years ago rely on respondents' recollections," it states. The release repeats the point: the research "does not establish the causes of those changes or measure year-over-year consumer behavior."
Those limits matter because the gap between stated purchase intent and checkout behavior is well documented. A respondent who names dining out as a hypothetical first cut in October has not necessarily cooked more dinners at home by December. As the inaugural edition, the report also has no prior year to compare against.
A season of trimmed budgets
The Edible data lands in a crowded field of holiday-season surveys, most of which point in the same direction on budgets while differing sharply in method.
Simon-Kucher's eighth holiday shopping study, fielded August 6 to 20, 2026 among 1,501 consumers, found that US households plan to cut holiday budgets 5.5% to an average of $1,018. In the same study, only 22% said advertised discounts fully reflect true savings, down from 46% in 2025.
Walmart's own panel reached a similar place. In its 2026 Holiday Trend Report, built from the retailer's Customer Spark Community, affording the holidays was the top stressor for 43.8% of Walmart shoppers, while 33.1% planned to spend more on food and holiday meals. That last figure sits comfortably beside Edible's finding that 35% count shared food among the best ways to make a celebration special.
Eating in has surfaced before as a saving. An Adtaxi poll of more than 1,100 US adults, fielded from May 2026, found that 42% were eating at home as a cost-saving behavior and that 40% of Americans switch to lower-cost brands. Earlier in the year, an InMarket survey of 4,844 US adults for Valentine's Day found that most shoppers kept budgets under $100, with 39% of those affected by price inflation planning to dine at home rather than at a restaurant.
Gifting itself has been reshaped in other surveys. SHEIN's in-app poll of 24,863 US customers, fielded September 6 to 14, found that 61% of its app shoppers put themselves first on holiday lists, a measure of self-gifting that sits awkwardly against Edible's 73% preference for shared gifts. The two samples are not comparable: one is a retailer's own app users, the other a national online panel. Optimove's 2026 Mother's Day report found that 49% of shoppers use AI for gift ideas, with 51% naming quality as the single most important factor in a gift.
