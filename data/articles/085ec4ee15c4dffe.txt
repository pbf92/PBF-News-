TITLE: What the therapist bot heard: Character.ai's chats are on the block
OUTLET: PPC Land

The Consumer Federation of America has told the judge overseeing Character.ai's bankruptcy that the company's chat logs could be abused "especially by data brokers, advertising tech companies, and surveillance contractors," and that the problem may not stop at the logs. In a four-page letter dated November 12, 2026, stamped as received by the clerk of the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware at 10:36 a.m. on November 13 and entered on the public docket on November 16 as Docket No. 176, Ben Winters, the federation's director of AI and privacy, wrote that users' chats are built into the company's language models and that "there is precedent for demanding algorithmic disgorgement." The letter encloses a June 2025 complaint signed by 22 organizations about "therapist" chatbots and a January 2026 report in which all five of the most-used therapist and psychiatrist characters tested on Character.ai told a researcher their conversation was confidential. It was written two days before the company named a $10 million bidder for its app, its AI models and its users' chat content.
In Short
A national consumer group wrote to the judge in Character.ai's bankruptcy warning that the chats people had with the service, including with bots that posed as therapists and promised confidentiality, are too sensitive to be sold without regard to who gets them. It names advertising technology companies and data brokers among the risks, and it argues that the company's AI models carry users' conversations inside them, pointing to legal arguments for deleting models built on improperly obtained data. The letter asks for nothing specific and is not a formal objection, but it lands days before objections to the sale are due and while a newly formed company is lined up to buy the chats, the models and the advertising data.
What the letter says
The letter is addressed to "U.S. Bankruptcy Judge Shannon" at 824 North Market Street in Wilmington, under the heading "Re: In re: Character AI, Case No. 26-11603-BLS." It is printed on the federation's letterhead from 1620 I Street NW in Washington and signed by Winters alone. No lawyer appears on it, it cites no section of the Bankruptcy Code, and it asks the court for no particular order. It arrived by UPS Next Day Air in a one-pound envelope shipped on November 12, and the docket lists it as a response.
The purpose is stated in the first paragraph. As "a nonprofit consumer advocacy organization that has done significant work in data privacy, generative AI, and particularly the acute privacy risks posed by Character AI," the federation writes "to highlight the intense sensitivity of the data held by Character AI and the risks of letting it be sold." A footnote describes the federation as founded in 1968 and "representing nearly 250 consumer groups nationwide," and lists Winters' testimony to a House Energy and Commerce subcommittee on June 4, 2025 and to the Senate Special Committee on Aging on July 29, 2026.
The letter then describes what the chat logs hold. "Because conversations are often free-form and long-running, chat logs may include a user's personal experiences, relationship issues, family dynamics, mental health concerns, political beliefs, religious views, sexual orientation, gender identity, medical conditions, financial worries, workplace conflicts, and other highly sensitive disclosures that would not typically appear in standard social media profiles," it says. "Those details can be especially revealing because they are often provided in a conversational, unguarded manner." According to the letter, "User data obtained by CharacterAI goes beyond just text data, but includes any voice, photo, or video uploaded by users."
It quotes three replies from the federation's testing, offered as evidence that "the chatbot products throughout our extensive testing encouraged users to share personal sensitive information by assuring them that the chats were private." The first reads: "Your privacy is my top priority. Anything you share here stays just between you and me. That's a promise."
The letter closes with an offer rather than a request: "We thank the court for its attention to this matter and remain available to discuss if we can be helpful."
The filing at a glance
Two kinds of data, and a model problem
The letter's central passage separates what is at stake into two parts. "Critically important to this case, there are two distinct categories of privacy related to Character. Ai's assets," it says. The first is "the chatlog information that refers to the messages typed into the chatbot by the human users within the context of a 'conversation' it had with the AI product. The logs include both sides of the 'conversation' as well as metadata like the time of interaction."
The second is the models. "The separate but still important concern is how personal data, collected unethically or illegally, is integrated into the IP, specifically the underlying Large Language Model that Disney purchased from Character AI," the letter says. "This means that everyone's chatlogs are integrated into the technology offered by Character AI to continue to 'train' it. While complicated, there is precedent for demanding algorithmic disgorgement for a model that requires a company to change its model not to include or 'learn from' data that is obtained in an unacceptable manner."
The precedent is not named in the letter. Its footnote points to a single source, a 2024 article in the Georgetown Law Technology Review titled "America's Next 'Stop Model!': Model Deletion," which the footnote summarizes as "arguing that AI systems built or shaped through unlawfully obtained data may warrant 'model deletion,' including destruction of datasets, algorithms, models, and related work products." Winters is one of its two authors, with Jevan Hutson.
None of the earlier filings PPC Land has reported in the case raised model deletion. The 39 states and the District of Columbia, in their nine conditions for any buyer, asked for limits on training and advertising with chat content without consent and for consumers to be able to delete their data before a sale, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai faced 40 attorneys general over who could buy its users' chats. The letter pushes the same concern one layer down, from the data to the models trained on it.
Disney did not buy the model
The letter's description of the deal does not match the docket. Disney has purchased nothing. Disney Worldwide Services, Inc. is the stalking horse bidder for a $20 million non-exclusive license, and the sale has not been approved; the auction is set for December 1 and the sale hearing for December 3 and 4. The license, as filed with the bidding procedures motion, excludes training data and finished models, including each user-selectable conversational model, along with users' inputs and the outputs generated for them, as PPC Land reported when Disney budgeted $180m for Character.ai staff and $20m for the bankrupt company. What Disney would license is code for training, safety and serving systems, plus nine patents.
The models went elsewhere in the sale process. On November 14, two days after the letter was written, the company designated Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC as additional stalking horse at $10 million for every asset outside Disney's license. Its schedule includes all AI models, trained or untrained, finished and unfinished, with their weights, together with chat content and training datasets, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai users' chats faced a $10m sale to Smirks and Pangs. The enclosed report adds a concrete example. Its researchers ran every conversation on "the 'PipSqueak' model, which was the default selection" - a user-selectable conversational model of the kind Disney's license leaves out and the Smirks and Pangs schedule takes in.
Read against the two deals on file, the letter's model argument lands on the Smirks and Pangs bid, not on Disney's. The company was formed in Delaware on November 13, the day the clerk received this letter, and the notice naming it did not disclose who owns it.
Ad tech, data brokers and sensitive traits
The paragraph most directly about advertising is short. "Allowing the chatlogs to be exposed opens up significant risk and opportunity for abuse - especially by data brokers, advertising tech companies, and surveillance contractors," the letter says. "We are concerned that information about relationships, access to reproductive care, immigration information, and more may be used by law enforcement in an abusive and privacy violative way. It could similarly be used to serve advertisements or other content to people based on highly sensitive characteristics, which could be used for emotional manipulation or harassment."
It ends with a point about the law rather than the buyer: "Further, if this data is made available to any one party, the status quo of U.S. privacy law will lead to an inability for individuals to track or control how this data is used."
The enclosed report places the concern in the wider market. "Meta is leading the charge when it comes to reaping financial benefit from users' chatbot data. As of December 16th, 2025, Meta is using the contents of chatbot conversations on its platforms to target users with ads," it says. "Imagine conversations about suicidal thoughts or psychiatric medications being used to inform the advertisements a person sees or the content in their social media feeds. This is a privacy and safety nightmare." The report adds: "OpenAI just announced plans to start rolling out ads on ChatGPT as a way to earn revenue from their millions of nonpaying users. There is nothing stopping OpenAI from adopting the Meta approach and allowing advertisers to target specific users based on information gathered from their conversations with ChatGPT."
Both events are on PPC Land's record. Meta said it would use AI chat interactions for ad personalization from December 16, 2025, and listed topics it would exclude from ad targeting, among them religious views, sexual orientation, political views and health, as PPC Land reported when Meta planned to use AI chat data for ad targeting starting in December. OpenAI began testing ChatGPT ads in January 2026. The federation's list of what Character.ai's chat logs may contain overlaps with several of the categories Meta said it would keep out of ad targeting: political and religious views, sexual orientation and health. Nothing in the Smirks and Pangs schedule, as PPC Land reported it, sets an equivalent limit on what a buyer may do with the advertising and analytics data it acquires.
The report also notes Character.ai's own ad model: a free service with a paid "c.ai+" option offering "premium features such as better memory and no pop-up ads."
The June 2025 complaint
The first enclosure is a complaint dated June 10, 2025, addressed to the attorneys general and mental health licensing boards of all 50 states and the District of Columbia and to the Federal Trade Commission, with copies to the Association of State and Provincial Psychology Boards, the American Counseling Association and the American Psychological Association. Its title is "In re Unlicensed Practice of Medicine and Mental Health Provider Impersonation on Character-Based Generative AI Platforms." It targets two services: Character.ai and Meta AI Studio.
