TITLE: Anthropic’s Claude claims a proof of VP ≠ VNP
OUTLET: Kingy AI

On November 23, Anthropic released a paper claiming that VP ≠ VNP over every field, a separation that would resolve Leslie Valiant’s 1979 conjecture in algebraic complexity. The paper credits an internal Claude research model with the discovery. Its central claim is that even unrestricted arithmetic circuits need superpolynomially many gates to compute the permanent of a matrix, except in characteristic two, where the proof uses the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial. The same lower bound applies to circuits that compute these polynomials as formal limits. Read the paper.
Anthropic released an AI-written Lean 4 formalization, and the repository’s automated check passed. Anthropic says no human mathematician has checked the proof. The most important open verification step is to establish that the formal definitions and theorems capture the mathematical claims the paper makes. The Clay Millennium Problem P versus NP concerns a different model of computation and remains open. Anthropic’s release notes · GitHub verification run · Clay Mathematics Institute.
What VP and VNP mean
Start with a circuit whose inputs are variables and constants from a field, such as the rational numbers or the integers modulo a prime. Each gate adds or multiplies two earlier results. The circuit can reuse its intermediate results. What matters is the number of gates needed to produce a formal polynomial, with all of its coefficients and monomials. Each arithmetic operation counts as one gate regardless of the bit length of its operands. The paper also counts variables and constants as input gates. Paper, section 1.3.
VP contains families of polynomials whose degree and number of variables grow at most polynomially and that can be computed by arithmetic circuits of polynomial size. Think of a family as one polynomial for each input size. VNP allows one additional operation at the level of the definition. Take a polynomial from VP with extra variables, evaluate those variables at every possible string of zeroes and ones, then sum the results. Each summand has a compact circuit, even though a sum over r extra bits has 2r terms. VP sits inside VNP by taking a sum over zero extra bits. Valiant’s question was whether every family defined by such a sum still has polynomial-size circuits. Valiant’s 1979 paper · Modern definitions in the Anthropic paper.
Why one sign matters
For a two-by-two matrix with rows (a, b) and (c, d), the determinant is ad − bc and the permanent is ad + bc. In an m-by-m matrix, each term selects one entry in every row and every column. The determinant gives each permutation its sign. The permanent gives every term a plus sign. On a zero-one matrix, evaluated over the integers, the permanent counts perfect matchings in the corresponding bipartite graph. Valiant’s permanent paper · Paper, section 1.2.
Both expressions have m! terms when fully expanded. That term count gives no gate lower bound. For example, multiplying m factors of the form (xᵢ + yᵢ) produces 2m distinct terms with just m additions and m − 1 multiplications, before counting inputs. The determinant likewise has polynomial-size circuits. The paper reports that the best previous lower bound for unrestricted, division-free circuits for the permanent was proportional to m² log m. Stronger lower bounds for restricted models did not settle this question. The hard part is ruling out every comparable shortcut for the permanent, including circuits that reuse subexpressions and cancel intermediate terms. Paper, section 1.2.
In characteristic two, +1 and −1 are the same field element, so the permanent becomes the determinant. The paper handles that case with the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial. It uses the product of edge variables for each directed tour that visits every vertex once and returns to its start, then adds the products. The permanent sums over all permutations, including disjoint cycles. The Hamiltonian cycle polynomial keeps just the permutations that form one cycle. It is VNP-complete over every field. Hamiltonian cycle polynomial and its completeness · Theorem 1.1.
The claimed lower bound
For sufficiently large m, the paper says the circuit needs more than mc(log₂ m)1/3 gates. The constant c is positive and can be chosen independently of the field. The threshold for “sufficiently large” can depend on the field’s characteristic, and the proof does not give a usable general threshold. The exponent grows without bound, so it eventually beats any fixed polynomial mk. Equivalently, the bound is of the form 2c(log₂ m)4/3. That is a quasipolynomial lower bound, with a large gap to the known exponential-size constructions. Theorems 1.1 and 1.4 · Appendix L on the threshold and exponent.
The separation follows because these polynomial families are in VNP and the bound would put them outside VP. Completeness gives the reduction in the other direction: if the permanent had polynomial-size circuits in characteristic other than two, every VNP family in that setting would have polynomial-size circuits too. The Hamiltonian cycle polynomial plays the same role without the characteristic restriction. Here a projection means substituting variables and constants into a suitably sized instance of the complete polynomial. Valiant’s completeness framework · VNP-completeness under projections.
How Claude’s proof works
The paper first builds two candidate polynomials designed to expose limits of small circuits. One comes from a network with a wire between every pair of nodes. The other encodes the sum of the weights of all accepting runs of short nondeterministic programs. Reductions let the authors use a hypothetical small permanent or Hamiltonian cycle circuit to compute the relevant candidate. They can then exploit the candidate’s structure. Paper, section 2.1.
Both branches use tensors, which here are arrays of coefficients indexed by several groups of variables. The proof uses spectral points to assign values to tensors. These values have rules for how they behave under combination and restriction. Taking logarithms turns the value of a network of wires into a sum of lengths. The geometry induced by those lengths yields two hypotheses, called flat and contracting. At least one holds over each field. The proof needs no decision about which holds over the reals, and the paper leaves open whether both can hold at once. Definition 2.1 and Theorem 2.2.
The flat case squeezes the circuit through a cut
Under the flat hypothesis, Euclidean distances are available to measure the complete network. A small circuit would express the network’s coefficient tensor as a sum of pieces, each of low rank across a balanced split of the variables. Low rank means that across the split the piece can be assembled from relatively few products of contributions from either side. The proof compares the original network’s length with how much length those pieces can account for using two hubs joined by a wire. The geometry forces the joining wire to have more capacity than a small circuit can supply. This case sets the weaker of the two rates and therefore determines the headline exponent. Paper, sections 2.2 and 2.3.
The contracting case makes a hypothetical accelerator contradict itself
Under the contracting hypothesis, the geometry yields identities that rewrite tensor networks as sums of tree networks. The trees allow weighted computations to be cut into shorter pieces. A small circuit for the complete polynomial would supply an accelerator for summing the weighted runs of those pieces. It would also supply a large stock of constants that the accelerator can name, which is why the argument has to move up to larger scales before diagonalizing. By carrying that same stock of constants forward while speeding up larger computations, the proof obtains enough room to build a weighted program that adds one to the accelerator’s answer on its own input. If the accelerator were correct, the result would have to equal itself plus one. Paper, section 2.4.
Why the border result is stronger
Allow the constants inside a circuit to depend on a parameter ε, including negative powers of ε. The output is required to have the target polynomial as its constant term and only positive powers of ε beyond that. Over the reals, in simple cases this models a family of circuits of the same size whose output polynomials approach the target coefficient by coefficient as ε goes to zero. The intermediate constants can diverge in that limit. The formal-series definition lets mathematicians express the same algebraic idea over finite fields. The paper claims that this extra freedom still requires superpolynomial size. Definition 1.3 and Theorem 1.4.
That would also give a superpolynomial lower bound on the size of determinants needed to represent the permanent, including through the corresponding formal limits, outside characteristic two. A small determinant representation would yield a small arithmetic circuit because determinants themselves have polynomial-size circuits. Over the complex numbers this addresses a target of geometric complexity theory through a different proof technique. The paper identifies its determinantal-complexity consequence, Remark N.6, as the exception to its Lean coverage. Here “approximate” concerns the coefficients of the entire formal polynomial. A randomized numerical estimate on nonnegative input matrices is a different task, with an existing approximation algorithm. Appendix N · Jerrum, Sinclair and Vigoda’s approximation algorithm.
Why P versus NP stays open
P versus NP asks about decision problems on finite input strings, such as whether a Boolean formula has a satisfying assignment. A Boolean circuit can agree with a polynomial on every zero-one input while computing a different formal polynomial. For instance, x² − x vanishes at both zero and one. Adding a multiple of it changes the formal coefficients without changing the answers on those inputs. Both branches of Claude’s argument inspect formal coefficients or degree components, precisely the information that such a change can alter. The paper’s section 2.5 identifies where the proof loses its grip on Boolean circuits. Paper, section 2.5 · Clay’s P versus NP problem statement.
The practical consequence is narrow. The claimed bound excludes an entire kind of exact symbolic shortcut, even with arbitrary field constants and unlimited circuit depth. It gives no lower bound on general-purpose SAT solvers, no runtime prediction for a particular matrix size, and no new attack or security guarantee for encryption. Boolean counting complexity and algebraic gate complexity ask different questions even when both use the word “permanent.” Paper, sections 1.3 and 2.5.
What Lean has checked and what reviewers need to check
Anthropic provides three pieces of evidence: the paper, a statement file that imports Mathlib and defines the objects and theorems, and a Lean development proving those statements. The continuous-integration workflow also uses Comparator to check the stated targets and recheck the proof with nanoda, an independently written Lean kernel. The paper reports only Lean’s standard axioms for the proofs. The paper directs readers to Lean for several machine-correctness steps on which the contracting argument relies.
