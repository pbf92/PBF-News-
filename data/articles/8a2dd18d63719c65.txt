TITLE: Clinical Review Synthesizes Patient Blood Management Strategies for Placenta Accreta Spectrum
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive clinical review published in Frontiers in Medicine synthesizes the evidence across the continuum of patient blood management for placenta accreta spectrum, mapping clinical interventions from antenatal diagnosis through delivery to postpartum recovery. Authored by Andrzej Jaworowski, Hubert Huras, and Magdalena Kołak from the Jagiellonian University Medical College in Krakow, Poland, the narrative review emphasizes that placenta accreta spectrum carries an inherent risk of sudden, life-threatening obstetric haemorrhage that requires coordinated multidisciplinary care. Rather than treating individual interventions as isolated therapies, the authors construct a longitudinal framework structured around five connected stages: antenatal anaemia optimization, referral and preparation, surgical bleeding control, haemorrhage management, and postpartum recovery alongside outcome review. Across these clinical domains, the investigators distinguish core care processes supported by established professional guidelines from selective, technology-dependent interventions that require specific clinical criteria and currently possess unproven comparative superiority in controlled placenta accreta spectrum trials.
Placenta accreta spectrum presents a formidable surgical challenge because abnormal uteroplacental vascularity, extensive pelvic dissection, bladder or parametrial involvement, emergency presentations, and unintended placental disruption can trigger rapid, catastrophic blood loss within minutes. The authors note that the physiological consequences of bleeding depend not only on the absolute volume of blood lost but also on the patient’s pre-existing haematological reserve, antenatal bleeding episodes, and cardiopulmonary reserve. Drawing on definitions from the World Health Organization and the Network for the Advancement of Patient Blood Management, Haemostasis and Thrombosis, patient blood management is framed as a patient-centred approach designed to optimize red-cell mass and physiological haemostasis, limit avoidable surgical blood loss, and ensure appropriate, timely transfusion support. The review highlights a cited meta-analysis of 20 studies encompassing 1,091 cases of placenta accreta spectrum that calculated a weighted mean requirement of 5.19 red blood cell units per patient, accompanied by substantial statistical heterogeneity with an I-squared value of 91 percent, emphasizing that blood bank readiness must be calibrated to individual patient anatomy, diagnostic certainty, and local resources rather than a fixed cross-match standard.
Antenatal haematological optimization represents the initial phase of the proposed pathway, resting on observational data showing that antepartum anaemia substantially impairs a patient’s capacity to tolerate intraoperative bleeding. In a massive German administrative dataset comprising 6,493,606 hospital deliveries, 38,060 cases were coded for placenta accreta spectrum; among these patients, anaemia was documented in 60.4 percent compared to 23.3 percent in other deliveries, and red blood cell transfusion was administered in 14.7 percent versus 0.7 percent. In a more granular retrospective investigation of 984 women with placenta accreta spectrum managed across three tertiary referral centres, propensity-score matching revealed that antepartum anaemia was significantly associated with higher transfusion requirements and elevated adjusted odds of a composite adverse maternal outcome, yielding an odds ratio of 1.84 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning 1.22 to 2.78. Although anaemia coexisted frequently with placenta praevia, antepartum bleeding, and deeper placental invasion, the authors emphasize that baseline testing must begin early enough to identify reversible causes, such as iron deficiency, before delivery. While a small retrospective study of 72 women receiving preoperative intravenous iron reported fewer transfusions and hysterectomies compared to 71 historical controls, the lack of randomization and secular changes in clinical practice make independent treatment effects uncertain. A cited 2024 Cochrane review of 13 trials in general obstetric populations indicated that intravenous iron achieved a slight increase in peripartum haemoglobin compared to oral administration, with a mean difference of 0.55 grams per decilitre, but demonstrated no distinct reduction in postpartum haemorrhage or transfusion requirements.
The review underscores that accurate prenatal diagnosis provides the cornerstone for institutional referral and timely multidisciplinary preparation. A cited meta-analysis of 31 observational studies established that prenatal diagnosis was associated with fewer emergency caesarean births, reduced estimated blood loss, and a significant reduction in transfused red blood cell volume, with a mean difference of negative 1.96 units and a 95 percent confidence interval from negative 3.25 to negative 0.68 units. Although hysterectomy occurred more frequently among prenatally diagnosed patients, this finding was confounded by greater disease severity, including higher proportions of placenta increta and percreta, alongside intentional selection of planned caesarean hysterectomy. Receiving referral centres must offer specialized imaging review, obstetric and anaesthetic leadership, agreed emergency mobilization pathways, blood-bank capabilities, and critical care access. In an observational cohort of 417 patients, implementation of a multidisciplinary protocol—combining standardized imaging, preoperative planning, routine bilateral ureteric catheterisation, a dedicated surgical team, and on-site urology support—was associated with lower adjusted odds of transfusion, urological trauma, and hysterectomy, alongside an adjusted reduction in blood loss of 260 millilitres with a 95 percent confidence interval from 480 to 70 millilitres. Furthermore, multicentre data indicated that surgery performed without placenta-specific surgical expertise was associated with an approximately threefold elevation in the adjusted odds of massive haemorrhage exceeding 3,500 millilitres.
Surgical management demands careful differentiation between anatomical preservation strategies and definitive extirpation, as clinical comparisons are deeply influenced by baseline disease severity and patient selection. Primary operative strategies include caesarean hysterectomy, which generally avoids disturbing the placenta prior to uterine removal; uterus-preserving resective surgery, which excises the invaded myometrium and reconstructs the uterine wall via techniques such as one-step conservative surgery; and expectant management, which leaves the placenta in situ without immediate resection. A 2025 meta-analysis pooling 16 comparative studies and 2,300 patients reported that local resection and expectant management were both associated with lower estimated blood loss than caesarean hysterectomy, with local resection also reporting fewer transfused intraoperative red blood cell units. However, prospective cohort data from the PACCRETA study showed that while leaving the placenta in situ was associated with a lower probability of receiving more than four red blood cell units within six months, it resulted in significantly increased rates of uterine artery embolisation, endometritis, and hospital readmissions. In a recent multicentre feasibility trial that successfully randomized 60 of 64 eligible women to one-step conservative surgery or hysterectomy, intention-to-treat analyses revealed no overall significant differences in blood loss, transfusion volume, operative time, or adverse events, although conservative surgery achieved shorter operative times and fewer transfusions among the subgroup with topographic type 1 lesions. Across another multicentre cohort of 101 attempted uterine preservation procedures, 29 cases required intraoperative conversion to hysterectomy for uncontrolled haemorrhage, an outcome strongly associated with antenatal bleeding requiring hospitalisation, yielding an adjusted odds ratio of 2.85.
Intraoperative autologous cell salvage is increasingly considered during planned placenta accreta spectrum surgery, yet its precise capacity to reduce allogeneic donor transfusion in this specific pathology remains unproven. The review notes that in the large SALVO trial, which randomized 3,028 women undergoing caesarean delivery at elevated haemorrhage risk, allogeneic red blood cell transfusion occurred in 2.5 percent of patients allocated to routine cell salvage compared to 3.5 percent with standard care, an effect that did not achieve statistical significance with an adjusted odds ratio of 0.65 and a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.42 to 1.01. A subsequent 2024 Cochrane review evaluating three obstetric trials including 3,115 participants concluded that cell salvage provided a low-certainty, statistically imprecise reduction in donor blood exposure with a risk ratio of 0.45 and a confidence interval spanning 0.15 to 1.33. Observational studies evaluating cell salvage specifically in placenta accreta spectrum are confounded by treatment selection and concurrent practice changes over time. In one retrospective cohort of 136 women evaluating care before and after cell salvage implementation, an unadjusted comparison showed no significant difference in the composite primary outcome of major blood component use, although post-hoc adjustment for blood loss and clinical factors yielded a significant odds ratio of 0.21. The authors caution that adjusting for blood loss may artificially obscure the true mechanism of intervention, concluding that while cell salvage is a valuable conditional option when major loss is anticipated or rare blood types are involved, proven donor exposure reduction in placenta accreta spectrum is not yet confirmed by robust trial data.
During active, catastrophic haemorrhage, physiological resuscitation must proceed in close coordination with surgical haemostatic manoeuvres. Dilutional coagulopathy, hypothermia, hypocalcaemia, acidosis, and progressive consumption of platelets and fibrinogen can rapidly destabilize the patient. While initial resuscitation in early haemorrhage avoids routine plasma or platelet administration without documented coagulopathy, established massive bleeding protocols frequently implement empirical component ratios, such as the World Health Organization example of a 1:2 plasma-to-red blood cell ratio during uncrossmatched release. The authors emphasize that fixed ratios remain unvalidated specifically in placenta accreta spectrum and that ongoing therapy must be guided by serial laboratory assessments. In the retrospective analysis of placenta accreta spectrum cases, laboratory coagulopathy was strongly correlated with extreme blood loss and transfusion demands, whereas the anatomical depth of placental invasion showed no direct association. Viscoelastic testing and conventional Clauss fibrinogen assays provide essential guidance for targeted replacement; World Health Organization guidance recommends cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate when fibrinogen falls below 2 grams per litre, targeting concentrations at or above this threshold. Randomised postpartum haemorrhage trials assessing empirical fixed-dose fibrinogen administration in unselected patients failed to demonstrate clinical benefit, reinforcing that replacement should be targeted rather than universally administered.
