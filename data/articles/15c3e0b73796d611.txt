TITLE: Systematic Review Identifies Compounding Inequities Restricting Mesothelioma Cancer Treatment Access
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

An integrative systematic review published in Supportive Care in Cancer has synthesized international evidence on the factors influencing patient access to and uptake of systemic anti-cancer therapy for mesothelioma. Conducted by researcher Bethany Taylor and colleagues Jenna Drury, Kate Montague-Hellen, Angela Tod, and Clare Gardiner, the investigation addressed documented disparities in cancer care and clinical decision-making. Registered on the PROSPERO database under identifier CRD42024603309, the review followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and incorporated ongoing input from the Mesothelioma UK Research Centre Patient and Public Involvement panel. The team executed structured searches across seven databases: APA PsycInfo, ASSIA, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, PubMed Central, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search covered English-language articles published from January 1, 2008, to October 18, 2024, focusing on mesothelioma, systemic anti-cancer therapy, and care access. Of 716 initial bibliographic records, 117 duplicates were removed, leaving 599 citations for title and abstract screening. Following the exclusion of 383 records, full-text reviews were performed on 215 articles. Ultimately, 17 database articles and one study identified via reference lists met inclusion criteria, establishing a synthesis of 18 studies.
Mesothelioma is a rare, aggressive, and incurable malignancy that predominantly arises in the pleural lining of the lungs, but can also develop in the peritoneum or other sites. Caused almost exclusively by asbestos exposure, it has an extended latency period of 30 to 50 years. The United Kingdom exhibits the highest recorded incidence worldwide, diagnosing approximately 2,700 cases each year. International forecasts project incidence will rise by 30.4 percent in Europe, up to 46.9 percent in North America, 57.1 percent in Oceania, and 72.0 percent in Africa by 2040. The disease primarily affects older adults and men, reflecting historical occupational exposure across mining, construction, and shipbuilding industries alongside subsequent asbestos bans. Mesothelioma carries poor clinical outcomes, with roughly 60 percent of patients dying within one year of diagnosis. Rapid disease progression, severe symptom burdens including intractable pain, substantial emotional distress, and complex legal and financial challenges demand coordinated multidisciplinary supportive care.
Because no curative treatments exist, systemic anti-cancer therapy is delivered with palliative intent to slow disease progression, reduce symptoms, improve quality of life, and extend survival. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence recommends systemic anti-cancer therapy as first-line treatment in the United Kingdom. Current options include chemotherapy and immunotherapy, with the phase III CheckMate 743 trial establishing nivolumab plus ipilimumab as a first-line option by demonstrating improved overall survival over platinum-based chemotherapy. In contrast, surgical intervention for disease control is recommended against by the British Thoracic Society. Furthermore, the phase III MARS2 trial reported worse survival and quality of life outcomes with extended pleurectomy decortication plus chemotherapy compared to chemotherapy alone. Despite these therapeutic advances, National Mesothelioma Audit data indicate that only 40 percent of patients receive chemotherapy. The 18 included studies encompassed 15 quantitative, one qualitative, and two mixed-methods investigations from the United States, Australia, the United Kingdom, Italy, Canada, the Netherlands, and Belgium, including one multi-country dataset. Most publications examined malignant pleural mesothelioma exclusively, while two incorporated other anatomical subtypes. Cohorts were predominantly male, with median or mean participant ages ranging from 68 to 78 years. Notably, 17 of the 18 studies examined chemotherapy, with only one study evaluating both chemotherapy and immunotherapy, meaning findings primarily reflect chemotherapy access. Methodological quality was evaluated using Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklists and judged moderate overall. Most studies demonstrated alignment between aims and design, suitable recruitment, and consideration of confounding factors. Common methodological limitations included insufficient statistical precision, unclear generalizability, limited discussion of clinical implications, and incomplete reporting on recruitment procedures, which presented risks of selection bias in several cohorts. Reviewers retained all eligible studies regardless of quality to provide a comprehensive narrative synthesis, noting that findings should be interpreted cautiously.
Demographic factors showed strong associations with treatment receipt, particularly patient age. Chemotherapy uptake consistently declined as age increased, with older age largely defined as exceeding 75 or 80 years. Patients aged 75 and older were underrepresented among treatment recipients, and those selecting active symptom control were significantly older than individuals receiving systemic therapy, with mean ages of 74.4 versus 68.4 years. Advanced age also affected treatment completion; patients older than 80 were less likely to complete three chemotherapy cycles, and another study observed decreasing average age across successive chemotherapy lines. Evidence regarding patient sex remained mixed; one study identified no significant link with chemotherapy uptake, whereas other analyses found female patients were less likely to receive chemotherapy and more frequently chose active symptom control. Additionally, body mass index influenced treatment patterns in one study, where individuals with higher body mass index were significantly more likely to receive systemic therapy than those with normal weight.
Patient attitudes and treatment preferences played a substantial role in treatment uptake, even among clinically eligible individuals. One study by Schmid and colleagues found that 44 percent of patients with favorable performance status declined chemotherapy due to personal preference. Another survey of 78 patients revealed that while 65 percent favored chemotherapy, 17 percent opted against it and 12 percent remained uncertain. Qualitative data indicated that treatment reluctance stemmed from desires to preserve quality of life, fears of side effects, prior negative experiences, and perceived limited clinical benefits, with patients feeling therapy burdens outweighed prospective gains. Quality and availability of information were infrequently investigated, though approximately 80 percent of participants in one study felt adequately informed to decide about chemotherapy. Nurses emphasized clear education regarding feasibility and acceptability, while seven percent of surveyed patients reported wishing they had spoken with peer survivors with lived experience to support their choices.
Clinician perspectives profoundly influenced treatment pathways. While many patients viewed treatment decision-making as patient-led, shared decision-making and clinician influence were common. Around 90 percent of doctors and over half of nurses reported moderate-to-high involvement in guiding choices. However, 70 percent of doctors cited therapeutic nihilism—defined as skepticism regarding clinical benefit—as a key barrier to recommending chemotherapy. This clinical skepticism contributed to lower systemic treatment uptake. Furthermore, clinicians reported delaying chemotherapy in asymptomatic patients with good performance status until symptoms emerged. Because mesothelioma progresses unpredictably and rapidly, this observational strategy sometimes resulted in performance status deterioration before therapy could begin, eliminating treatment opportunities. Nurses emphasized that patient education and managing symptoms like pain and breathlessness enhanced treatment feasibility. The review also highlighted family involvement as an important influence, showing that clinical recommendations intersect with personal and familial factors to govern access.
Clinical and disease-related factors demonstrated mixed associations with treatment access, with functional performance status emerging as the most consistent predictor. Patients exhibiting favorable Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group scores of zero or one were significantly more likely to receive systemic therapy than those with scores of two or greater. In contrast, evidence regarding tumor histology remained inconclusive; multiple studies found patients with epithelioid histology were more likely to receive therapy than those with biphasic or sarcomatoid subtypes, with chemotherapy favored in epithelioid cases and immunotherapy in non-epithelioid cases, though one study identified no histological association. Findings concerning disease stage were similarly contradictory; some studies reported lower treatment uptake in advanced stages due to rapid progression, whereas others observed higher treatment administration in advanced stages to relieve pain, and two studies found no link. The impact of comorbidities also varied, facilitating treatment initiation and continuation in some cohorts but showing no significant association in a large-scale evaluation.
Healthcare systems and geographical location imposed substantial structural barriers to systemic therapy access. Treatment rates varied widely across cancer networks in England and Wales, ranging from 47 to 78 percent overall and 46 to 71 percent among patients with good performance status. Similar regional variations occurred in Italy, with chemotherapy uptake ranging from 28 to 71 percent, while cross-national differences showed uptake rates of 60 percent in Belgium, 41 percent in the Netherlands, and 37 percent in England. In Australia and New Zealand, 44 percent of doctors identified lack of rural specialist services as a barrier, while 58 percent of nurses cited limited clinician knowledge. Delayed diagnosis represented another major structural obstacle, with 74 percent of surveyed nurses in Australia and New Zealand identifying prolonged intervals between symptom onset and diagnosis as limiting treatment access. Furthermore, failure to refer patients to medical oncology directly restricted care; in the United Kingdom, 47 percent of patients who declined systemic therapy had never been reviewed by an oncologist.
An overarching theme of intersectionality demonstrated how demographic, clinical, informational, and organizational influences interact to shape access to systemic anti-cancer therapy. Rather than operating as isolated obstacles, co-occurring factors compound disadvantage along the care pathway. These findings align with priorities highlighted in the 2026 National Cancer Plan for England, Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan, and the World Health Organization’s Global Status Report on Cancer, all emphasizing equitable access to specialist services for rare cancers. The review highlighted that wider social determinants of health, including socioeconomic status, ethnicity, and health literacy, remain unaddressed in the mesothelioma literature. To mitigate these disparities, the authors recommended establishing earlier oncology referral pathways, enhancing access to specialist multidisciplinary teams, investing in clinical nurse specialists, and addressing therapeutic nihilism through professional education. Because 17 of 18 included studies focused on chemotherapy, prospective research evaluating access to immunotherapy is urgently required to assess whether contemporary regimens introduce new treatment inequities.
