Recent studies examine JAK inhibitor safety, psoriasis risk, chronic hand eczema prevalence, pediatric scabies treatment, and diagnostic innovation.
Recent dermatology research continues to highlight opportunities to refine treatment decisions, improve disease recognition, and address persistent gaps in clinical care. From predicting thrombotic risk among patients receiving Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors to investigating molecular approaches for diagnosing complex inflammatory skin conditions, newly published studies offer insights into several areas of dermatologic practice.
This week's Dermatology Times Journal Digest examines 5 studies published in JAMA Dermatology and the British Journal of Dermatology, exploring treatment safety, chronic disease burden, pediatric management, and emerging diagnostic strategies.
A research letter published in the British Journal of Dermatology introduced the Thrombo-JAK score, a proposed risk prediction tool designed to identify patients with atopic dermatitis (AD) who may be more susceptible to thrombotic events while receiving JAK inhibitor therapy.¹
JAK inhibitors have expanded treatment options for patients with moderate to severe AD, particularly those requiring systemic therapy. However, concerns regarding venous thromboembolism and other cardiovascular safety outcomes have reinforced the importance of individualized risk assessment when selecting treatment.
The investigators evaluated clinical information from 305 patients to develop a model intended to stratify thrombosis risk. The reported model demonstrated an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of approximately 0.72, suggesting moderate discriminatory performance. Estimated thrombotic risk varied across the identified risk categories, ranging from approximately 3.4% to 31%.
The findings suggest that structured risk assessment may eventually support more individualized prescribing decisions. However, the relatively small development cohort and need for external validation limit immediate clinical application. The score should be considered investigational rather than a replacement for established prescribing precautions or comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment.
A cohort study published in JAMA Dermatology examined the association between changes in smoking intensity and subsequent psoriasis development, addressing an important question regarding modifiable risk factors and disease prevention.²
Smoking has previously been associated with psoriasis onset and severity, including psoriasis vulgaris and palmoplantar pustulosis. Although complete smoking cessation remains an important preventive health recommendation, investigators sought to explore whether reducing smoking intensity without achieving complete cessation might also be relevant to psoriasis risk.
The study evaluated changes in smoking behavior in relation to subsequent disease development, with particular attention to the potential implications of smoking reduction as an intermediate behavioral goal.
The research adds to ongoing discussions about the relationship between environmental exposures, inflammatory pathways, and psoriasis susceptibility. From a clinical perspective, the study highlights the importance of addressing tobacco use during dermatologic consultations, while recognizing that observational associations cannot independently establish a causal protective effect from smoking reduction.
The direction and magnitude of the study's associations should be confirmed from the full results before making specific recommendations about smoking reduction and psoriasis prevention.
A large cross-sectional study published in JAMA Dermatology found that approximately 9.6% of US adults reported health care professional–diagnosed chronic hand eczema (CHE), highlighting the substantial prevalence of a condition associated with persistent symptoms, occupational challenges, and impaired quality of life.³
Led by Raj Chovatiya, MD, PhD, MSCI, and colleagues, the investigation included 10,636 adults in the United States. Among these participants, 1,019 reported a diagnosis of CHE, corresponding to a prevalence of 9.6% (95% CI, 9.0%-10.2%).
Of those reporting CHE, 75.8% experienced hand eczema continuously for at least 3 months and reported at least 2 flare episodes within the preceding year.
Subgroup analyses revealed differences across demographic and occupational characteristics. Prevalence was higher among men than women (10.5% vs 8.7%), employed than unemployed participants (10.3% vs 7.8%), and urban than rural residents (10.4% vs 6.3%).
The investigators emphasized the need for additional epidemiologic research to better characterize CHE burden and inform prevention and management strategies.
Although the findings suggest that CHE may affect a substantial proportion of US adults, the reliance on self-reported clinician diagnoses introduces the possibility of recall and classification bias. Nevertheless, the results reinforce the importance of recognizing persistent hand dermatitis, evaluating potential occupational exposures, and addressing disease burden beyond visible skin symptoms.
A comparative effectiveness research study published in JAMA Dermatology examined topical treatment strategies for scabies among infants and toddlers aged 24 months or younger who weighed less than 15 kg.⁴
Scabies, a contagious parasitic skin infestation caused by Sarcoptes scabiei, presents particular treatment challenges in young children because of age- and weight-related restrictions, differences in medication tolerability, and concerns regarding emerging treatment resistance.
Led by Edoardo Cammarata, MD, and colleagues, the study evaluated 3 topical therapies: permethrin, sulfur, and benzyl benzoate. These treatments represent established options for scabies management, although their use in very young patients requires careful consideration of efficacy, safety, and applicable treatment guidelines.
Current European guidelines recommend topical permethrin and benzyl benzoate among first-line therapies, while sulfur preparations are generally considered alternatives. Oral ivermectin is subject to regulatory restrictions in children weighing less than 15 kg, further emphasizing the importance of effective topical treatment strategies in this population.
The investigation contributes to ongoing efforts to optimize pediatric scabies management, particularly as reports of reduced permethrin susceptibility raise questions about treatment selection. The comparative efficacy and tolerability results require confirmation from the complete research letter before identifying a preferred regimen.
For clinicians, the study highlights the importance of age-appropriate treatment selection, adherence to application instructions, and consideration of household transmission when managing scabies in infants and toddlers.
A study published in the British Journal of Dermatology investigated the potential role of multi-omic profiling in improving the molecular diagnosis of erythrodermic skin diseases, a group of conditions that can present substantial diagnostic challenges.⁵
Erythroderma is characterized by widespread skin erythema, frequently accompanied by scaling, pruritus, and systemic complications. It may arise from inflammatory dermatoses, drug reactions, or underlying malignancies, including cutaneous T-cell lymphoma.
Distinguishing between these etiologies can be difficult because different erythrodermic conditions may share overlapping clinical and histopathologic features. Consequently, patients may require extensive diagnostic evaluation, repeated biopsies, and longitudinal monitoring before a definitive diagnosis can be established.
The investigators explored multi-omic profiling as an approach to molecular disease characterization. By examining multiple layers of biological information, these techniques may help identify disease-associated signatures that complement conventional clinical and histopathologic assessment.
Although molecular profiling holds promise for improving diagnostic precision, its clinical utility depends on reproducible findings, validation in independent patient populations, and demonstrated advantages over existing diagnostic approaches.
The research reflects a broader movement toward precision dermatology, in which molecular information may eventually support more accurate disease classification and individualized management.
