TITLE: Where Aristotle Taught Alexander
OUTLET: Biblical Archaeology Society

Where Aristotle Taught Alexander
The gymnasium, mosaics, and banquet halls of Mieza
The royal gymnasium at Mieza in northern Greece is, according to Greece’s Minister of Culture Lina Mendoni, “one of the most important monuments of Macedonian antiquity.” Excavations begun in 2024 have dated the structure to the mid-fourth century BCE—i.e., the reign of Alexander the Great’s father, Philip II. According to textual sources, this is the place where Alexander received his education from Aristotle, one of the most famous philosophers in history.
The royal gymnasium at Mieza covers almost 8 acres. The research team is currently focusing on the eastern side of the site, which contains a palaestra (courtyard for wrestling and other forms of physical exercise) and a didaskaleio (teaching area), where stone desks or benches were found. Previously, four bronze styluses were found within the larger gymnasium complex.
Within the didaskaleio, the team discovered a three-part structure that covers more than 1,700 square feet, consisting of a central vestibule and two banquet rooms. Both rooms feature mosaic floors and raised platforms for dining couches. The team estimates that each room accommodated ten such couches, allowing at least 20 diners per room. Other discoveries include an outdoor stadium, colonnaded walkways, water infrastructure, a covered running track stretching approximately 656 feet, and small finds such as coins and ceramics.
Mieza is identified as the place where Aristotle taught Alexander largely based on written sources, though these date from hundreds of years after Alexander’s time. Plutarch links the two figures at Mieza, saying Philip II gave Aristotle and his pupils a nymphaion there (a sanctuary dedicated to the nymphs, the female nature spirits tied to springs, caves, and groves). Other writers, such as Diogenes Laërtius, Aulus Gellius, and Pliny the Elder, also attest to Aristotle’s tutelage of Alexander, though they do not specifically mention Mieza. Aristotle had spent some 20 years studying under Plato in Athens, a worldclass education that he appears to have passed on to Alexander.
Alexander’s education under Aristotle belonged to a much broader Greek tradition in which intellectual development and physical training went hand in hand. The gymnasium was built for both, offering standard training in the body along with the mind, though philosophers did not agree on how the two were related. Plato leaned toward a dualism in which the soul was distinct and superior to the body (a view some scholars believe is echoed in the Gospel of John). Aristotle broke suit, arguing that the soul was not separate from the body. Alexander was therefore taught by a philosopher who had absorbed Platonic dualism and then reworked it, in a school that trained body and mind together.
While Aristotle was teaching young Macedonian aristocrats at Mieza, another chapter of history was unfolding in the Levant. In Jerusalem, Judeans were living in the shadow of the Persian Empire, the power that had once permitted their return from exile and the rebuilding of the Jerusalem Temple. Philip II and Aristotle lived during the final decades of the Persian Empire, recorded throughout the later books of the Hebrew Bible. Within a generation, Aristotle’s famous pupil would bring down the Persian Empire and usher in a new political era. Power over Judea passed first to Macedonia and then to the Greek-speaking kingdoms established by Alexander’s successors, the Ptolemies and the Seleucids.
