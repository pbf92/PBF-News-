TITLE: Clay's Jeff Barg says AI agents still can't fix themselves — and that's the next bottleneck for go-to-market
OUTLET: BigGo Finance

Clay's Jeff Barg says AI agents still can't fix themselves — and that's the next bottleneck for go-to-market
Clay's users hate choosing which AI model to use. That blunt admission from Jeff Barg, the company's head of AI, is the clearest sign yet of where the agent business is heading: away from pick-your-own-model menus and toward software that quietly decides for you, task by task.
Speaking on the Latent Space podcast, Barg described a company that has roughly 10x'd its business and grown from about 50 to around 600 people since he joined through Clay's late-2024 acquisition of his prior startup, Avenue. That vantage point — a founder brought in to build AI at a company that, by his own account, had "zero people working on AI" — makes his account of what actually breaks in production unusually concrete.
Go-to-market is becoming an engineering job, not a headcount job
Clay started seven years ago as a low-code spreadsheet tool and only narrowed onto go-to-market about three years ago, after briefly considering recruiting as an alternative. Its bet is that companies can build systems to find customers instead of hiring more salespeople — what Clay brands the "go-to-market engineer."
Barg describes the role as a hybrid: agent engineering (prompt and tool design for qualification), data engineering (building, transforming, enriching and filtering lists), and ordinary software engineering, such as researching a company when someone submits a website form and then routing it to a rep or an outbound campaign. Before the term existed, he says, this work sat inside revenue operations — Clay still sells into rev ops teams — but he considers the discipline genuinely new.
Clay's own internal version is a team of former engineers building tools on top of Clay for the sales team. The backgrounds of successful practitioners are "pretty across the board": a notable share are traditional software engineers approaching the problem from first principles, plus ambitious rev ops people who grew into the role.
The platform is deliberately generic — "an infrastructure provider, go-to-market flavored," as Barg puts it — integrating with hundreds of data providers while also shipping its own company and contact datasets. The trade-off he concedes: flexibility means many ways to build systems that don't work as expected.
Why Clay threw out its first agent and rebuilt it as a coding agent
The most substantive technical thread is the re-architecture of Sculptor, Clay's first-party agent. The original, launched in September 2025 and built on models around Sonnet 3.5, was a set of tool interfaces handed to a model in a loop, with heavy investment in prompts and tool definitions. Barg's own assessment: it was good at "add this column that does X," but not at "build me an outbound campaign that's going to deliver 10 qualified meetings on my calendar." It also locked out context a sales rep would have.
The rebuild rests on three lessons. First, coding agents are the right substrate — models are good at operating them, and coding agents are good at writing scripts in Bash or Python. Second, tool expressivity was too low; agents needed to chain tools and build five columns at once. Third, the primitives were too constrained — no CRM data, no campaign results.
So Clay built a platform-wide "audiences" layer that ingests CRM and Gong data and entity-resolves it into what Barg calls "the authoritative canonical spreadsheet of your customers and contacts." The new Sculptor is, in his words, "a proper coding agent." It runs in a cloud sandbox, uses Clay's publicly launched command-line interface, and orchestrates while the older Claygent agents fill in cells.
Barg says the rebuilt agent is "unbelievably more expressive and useful," is live in limited form, and will launch broadly across every surface soon. He cites it answering an internal question — how many YC companies use Clay — in about 60 seconds, versus a year earlier requiring scraping a YC batch to CSV, getting Snowflake access, and cross-referencing Salesforce.
Claygent itself predates all of this: Clay shipped an agent product in November 2023, a year after ChatGPT, that searched the web and did structured extraction into a column. It remains, in Barg's view, what most people mean when they say Clay. When he took over, the company had exactly one person working on agents — who then left to start his own company. "We were this really AI-forward company that had zero people working on AI," he said.
Frontier models matter for orchestration. For the plumbing, almost anything will do.
Barg is unusually direct about the model selector: "Our users hate our model selector. Like they hate it. I think most customers don't want to see it. They're upset when they see it, and we get a bunch of complaints about that." Clay will keep the selector for agency owners and power users who want control, but is launching a model router and an "Auto mode" — deliberately unbranded, "we're not trying to get fancy with it" — for everyone else. The pitch is that routing can operate at the task level: finding the CEO of Apple is a different problem from finding the CEO of an obscure company.
His view of where the most expensive models still earn their keep:
On post-training and open-weight models, Barg says the idea "hasn't been viable" for most of his tenure but became more plausible recently, mainly for cost. He describes two cost-reduction jumps: off-the-shelf open-weight models, which are good and much cheaper than the top tier but still expensive, and post-training a smaller model to perform at frontier level on a narrow tool set. Clay is "starting to explore" and will "launch something pretty soon in this area."
His warning to peers is specific: don't start until inference spend is material — he puts the threshold at roughly $10M–$100M. "Cost savings are great, but you have to do that on a substantial enough base where the cost savings are going to actually matter," he said. "A lot of companies are jumping into this just too early — they don't have enough inference spend where the savings are going to be material to the business." And don't expect post-training to beat the frontier: it buys cost, not capability.
He is skeptical that dedicated classifier models are additive yet — "we haven't done anything yet with Jev in production" — but offers a striking thought experiment on unit economics. Clay holds a dataset of 40 million companies. At roughly 1,000 LinkedIn tokens per company plus 500 tokens of ideal-customer-profile definition, asking "is this a good fit?" across every company in the world costs about $1,500 at $42 per billion tokens.
For context, he says $1–$10 per qualified lead is a good range, and $10–$100 per booked call "is not crazy," depending on the business.
The real eval — did the campaign land meetings? — is the one Clay can't own
Clay's eval practice started as LLM-as-judge over tool outputs: given "build a table to do X," did the agent add the right columns? Barg says the team was "writing evals, but I don't think we were measuring the right things." Launch exposed two blind spots: users immediately ran analysis on their tables, which nobody had planned for, and enterprise customers with millions of records broke things small-company evals never caught.
The current structure is use-case based. For each thing customers try, Clay describes the trajectory that should get there — if the question is about first-party data, the agent should query the audiences layer — and separately measures output quality. Barg's ambition is second-order measurement: if a user asks for an outbound campaign against a list, did those people actually respond? He acknowledges the flaw the hosts press on — that outcome depends on the customer's skill and company, not just the agent.
That leads to a structural tension the episode surfaces but doesn't resolve. Clay is a tool for volume, so it can't own outcomes the way an outcome-priced vendor can; Barg notes even Sierra, which does sell outcomes, built an eval platform in-house. His heuristic: "if something can't be expressed in our internal evals, it's probably like a product feature to be built around it." He floats building eval-like assurance into the product so customers can write their own tests.
On hiring, Clay's AI team is roughly evenly split between traditional machine-learning profiles, applied AI engineers who like eval work, and standard software engineers — all titled simply "software engineer." The hardest hire is people with hands-on production agent experience, which he says is scarcer in New York than the Bay Area, partly because the calendar is unforgiving: ChatGPT is only about four years old, and a year of agent engineering is a large share of the available pool.
The infrastructure is hand-rolled, and the boring problems are the ones that broke
Clay runs a JavaScript stack and, for agent durability, mostly BullMQ. "I don't know if we'll survive another 10x on BullMQ, but so far so good," Barg said. No Temporal, no LangGraph. Product data sits on Postgres and ClickHouse; internal analytics on ClickHouse and a flaky relationship with Fivetran; some internal datasets on Dagster, now being reworked. His summary of the modern data stack: "cooked."
What actually broke at scale was operational, not architectural. Clay lacked visibility into what agents were doing and onboarded LangSmith to see tool calls and failures. Inference workloads are spiky by nature — a customer researching a 50,000-company table fires an agent per company at once — so much of the work went into throttling, pausing and resuming execution when a provider rate-limits or goes down, and fairness so a large enterprise's million-row enrichment doesn't crowd out a new user's 10-row workflow. Clay does not currently dual-source inference, though Barg says it probably will.
On data tenancy, Clay's audiences layer is single-tenant per customer. Barg concedes downsides but argues it is simpler, and that there is less duplicative work than expected because customers' enrichment needs diverge quickly once past basics like employee count or phone number. On integrations, Clay launched 25–30 recently, and Barg says the bottleneck was contracting, not engineering.
Clay wants outside agents to work as well as its own — and the split is closer to 50/50 than expected
Clay's stated ethos is that the platform should be great for all agents, "whether it's our agent or external agents." Barg is explicit: "We explicitly don't want our agent to be markedly better than just using Clay and Claude Code." Clay ships both an MCP and a CLI; the MCP team was redirected to build out the CLI after the internal go-to-market team, who lived in Cursor, asked for it because they didn't want to leave it. Usage between Clay's own harness and external harnesses like Claude Code is "much closer to 50-50 than I would have thought."
The hosts push on why build a first-party harness if it isn't meant to win. Barg's answer is customer demand — people are already inside the Clay interface and want an agent there — and he allows that many such agents are effectively open-source coding agents in a container. Clay uses the Vercel AI SDK for its integration layer.
Institutional courage is the scarce resource, and Clay has a live example
Barg calls institutional courage "probably the most important thing right now at companies," and grounds it in Clay's growth from 50 to roughly 600 people, where "the momentum is just for things to calcify."
His case study: Clay was a launch partner for the first wave of ChatGPT apps and reprioritized about 25% of engineering for a month, mid-quarter, to hit the date. "Two days before we were supposed to launch, they were like, we're going to postpone this indefinitely," he recalled. Clay kept working on the assumption the decision would reverse.
