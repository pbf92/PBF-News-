TITLE: Systematic Review Finds Persistent Barriers in African Operating Room Safety Culture
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic review evaluating patient safety culture across African operating rooms has identified persistent systemic challenges alongside promising team-based interventions across twenty-two studies from twelve countries. The investigation, led by researchers Jacques Fadhili Bake, Naicen Ghanmi, Elena Guadagno, Kasereka Masumbuko Claude, Tsongo Kibendelwa Zacharie, and Dan Poenaru, synthesized findings from research published between 2014 and 2024 to examine how surgical safety measures operate in resource-constrained environments. Operating rooms represent high-risk settings where complex procedures and unpredictable physiological responses contribute to preventable clinical adverse events. Published data indicate that approximately ten percent of avoidable patient injuries occur within surgical contexts, with many incidents developing either before or after operative procedures. While patient safety interventions have progressed internationally following landmark initiatives, consolidated evidence regarding how surgical safety culture is understood and implemented across African healthcare facilities has historically remained limited.
To address this empirical gap, the investigators formulated a systematic review protocol registered with the PROSPERO database under identifier CRD42024627076, adhering to the updated PRISMA 2020 reporting standards alongside PRISMA-S guidelines for search strategy formulation. The research team hypothesized that systemic organizational obstacles continue to undermine perioperative safety culture despite the introduction of international surgical protocols. A medical librarian executed searches across eight major electronic databases from inception through December 5, 2024, including Medline, Embase, Cochrane Library, Africa-Wide, CINAHL, Global Health, Global Index Medicus, and Web of Science Core Collection. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary subject headings with variations of keywords related to patient safety culture and surgical care across African nations. Eligibility criteria restricted inclusion to quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods empirical studies involving operating room healthcare professionals, while excluding non-operating room settings, patient-centric investigations, reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, and inaccessible reports.
The comprehensive database search initially retrieved 9,875 records. Following the elimination of duplicates using EndNote duplicate checking and Covidence screening workflows, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts, followed by independent full-text assessments where discrepancies were resolved through consensus or third-party arbitration. Ultimately, twenty-two studies satisfied the predefined inclusion criteria. Methodological quality across the corpus was formally appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool, which evaluates empirical studies against design-specific criteria yielding quality scores from twenty percent to one hundred percent. All included qualitative studies and the majority of quantitative descriptive studies achieved perfect appraisal scores of one hundred percent. Among nine mixed-methods investigations, three met all appraisal criteria completely, while six received eighty percent quality ratings. Because the included studies demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in methodological designs, sample sizes, and evaluated endpoints, statistical meta-analysis was not possible, prompting the authors to employ narrative synthesis with subgroup analyses.
Geographically, the twenty-two studies spanned twelve African countries, with Ethiopia contributing the highest volume of literature with four publications, followed by Tunisia and Tanzania with three studies each. Nine of the twenty-two studies, representing forty-one percent of the analyzed corpus, were published between 2019 and 2024. Among the total included literature, eight studies specifically examined the existing baseline factors influencing patient safety culture, whereas fourteen studies evaluated targeted interventions or implementation strategies designed to improve surgical safety. The evaluation of baseline safety culture relied on several validated instruments, most notably the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture and the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire, alongside the National Surgical, Obstetric, and Anaesthesia Plans interview manual and non-validated contextual assessments.
Across the baseline assessments, multiple studies reported consistently low scores across core safety dimensions, reflecting widespread systemic difficulties in perioperative environments. Three investigations documented depressed ratings across all examined safety culture domains, while clinician assessments frequently revealed negative attitudes toward perioperative safety protocols. However, institutional variations were evident within the literature: one evaluated hospital demonstrated robust perioperative standards, and another localized study identified positive overall perceptions of surgical safety among operating room personnel. These divergent observations highlight that while broader institutional environments often struggle with safety governance, localized leadership and staff engagement can foster positive safety dynamics in specific facilities.
A critical recurrent vulnerability identified across the included literature was the absence of non-punitive responses to clinical error. Multiple evaluations using standardized surveys observed deeply ingrained blame cultures within surgical suites, where mistakes trigger punitive administrative or peer actions rather than constructive review. Investigators noted that blame-oriented workplace dynamics actively discourage healthcare personnel from reporting near misses or adverse events, creating cycles of concealment that prevent institutional learning and jeopardize clinical care. Furthermore, researchers noted that punitive work environments contribute to second-victim distress among staff members involved in inadvertent procedural errors, compounding workforce stress in already demanding clinical settings.
Communication openness represented another pervasive institutional barrier documented in the systematic review. Across several studies employing the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture, positive response rates for communication openness ranged between only 26.3 percent and 27.9 percent. This restricted communicative flow was strongly reinforced by rigid hierarchical structures within operating suites, where paternalistic management styles and traditional physician dominance over nursing staff inhibited collaborative dialogue. Subordinate staff members frequently expressed reluctance to voice safety concerns or question surgical decisions, fostering cultures of silence that heighten the risk of miscommunication and adverse surgical events.
Severe resource constraints further compromised surgical safety culture across multiple healthcare facilities. Positive perception scores regarding staffing adequacy were exceptionally low across studies, registering between 21.2 percent and 28.6 percent. Chronic shortages of trained surgical and nursing personnel, combined with pervasive financial limitations and inadequate physical infrastructure, elevated baseline workloads and reduced clinical vigilance. In conflict-affected regions, such as the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, severe deficits in human resources were further exacerbated by economic instability and armed conflict, imposing acute operational pressures that directly undermine safe surgical practices.
Despite these extensive systemic challenges, the systematic review identified four primary interventional strategies that demonstrated measurable positive impacts on operating room safety culture. Foremost among these was the implementation of the World Health Organization Surgical Safety Checklist, evaluated across eight studies. In Madagascar, a three-day multidisciplinary checklist training program implemented across twenty-one hospitals produced marked gains in checklist compliance, teamwork, communication, and protocol adherence, with follow-up studies confirming sustained improvements in hospital culture and job satisfaction. Similarly, nationwide implementation in Benin demonstrated durable gains in safety perceptions, while a four-day checklist training program tracked over fifteen months sustained adherence to six core safety processes in over half of participants despite ongoing hierarchical challenges.
Beyond checklist adoption, multi-component programs provided significant evidence of efficacy across diverse surgical contexts. In Tanzania, the Safe Surgery 2020 initiative implemented in ten facilities enhanced collaborative teamwork, safety protocol adherence, and checklist utilization, with institutional mentorship programs strengthening clinician confidence and standardizing operative routines. In Rwanda, the Non-Technical Skills for Surgeons curriculum improved participant comprehension of critical non-technical competencies from 55.6 percent pre-course to 80.9 percent post-course, yielding improvements in situational awareness, decision-making, and leadership. Additionally, evaluations of the Lifebox McCaskey Safe Surgery Fellowship demonstrated that structured multidisciplinary education fosters positive attitudes toward collaborative teamwork and improves compliance with surgical checklists, indicating that targeted educational initiatives can effectively counter systemic constraints in African operating suites.
The review also detailed specific regional adaptations and expanded applications of checklist interventions across African operating facilities. In Somalia, investigators documented a week-long training curriculum implemented in Mogadishu that improved procedural compliance, safety practices, team dynamics, and interpersonal communication. In another interventional study, researchers expanded the standard surgical checklist protocol to incorporate explicit infection control measures, resulting in documented improvements in staff knowledge and infection prevention practices. Furthermore, implementation research highlighted that successful checklist deployment in low-resource environments depends heavily on structured execution strategies, as demonstrated in a targeted study of twenty participants that achieved enhancements in staff communication and overall care quality.
Analysis of high-performing versus low-performing facilities within multicenter programs further clarified the organizational determinants of sustainable safety cultures. Comparisons among surgical centers participating in the Safe Surgery 2020 initiative showed that facilities achieving superior outcomes were distinguished by integrated clinical safety practices, active leadership engagement, and robust interprofessional collaboration. While lower-performing sites demonstrated measurable procedural improvements, they exhibited less cultural integration of safety behaviors. Similarly, investigations of non-technical skills training in Rwanda revealed that while high workloads, steep organizational hierarchies, and poor baseline communication posed substantial barriers to implementation, thorough institutional preparation and the presence of previously trained staff served as crucial facilitators for team empowerment and safer surgical conduct.
Reflecting on the broader implications of their synthesis, the authors underscored that addressing systemic operating room vulnerabilities requires multi-level policy and practice reforms. They emphasized the necessity of transitioning away from punitive environments toward non-punitive, just cultures that encourage transparent error reporting without fear of retribution.
