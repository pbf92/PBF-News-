TITLE: Google AI summary test says 4 Search advertisers are not browser tools
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google is testing AI-generated text inside the block of paid results on mobile search, with one summary placed above the entire "Sponsored Results" group and a second layer of generated text under each individual ad. Search Engine Roundtable reported the test on November 9, 2026, after SERP researcher Brodie Clark posted a video of it, and PPC News Feed relayed the find on November 18. Google has not confirmed the test, and neither report carries a comment from the company.
In Short
Google is trying out AI-written text in its mobile search ads: one line at the top that sums up every advertiser in the paid group, and another line under each ad. In the only published example, a search for "seo browser," that top line said the four advertisers offered marketing software or services, "not simple browser-based tools." The words introducing those paid placements were written by Google's AI rather than the advertisers, and Google has not said whether advertisers can see, edit or switch off that text.
What the published example shows
Clark logged the sighting in his notes on November 8, 2026, and shared a video through the SERP Alert account on X. Barry Schwartz of Search Engine Roundtable wrote it up the following morning, at 7:21 am. "This one shows AI-generated summaries above the grouping of the sponsored results, summarizing all the ads from all the advertisers, with also AI-generated summaries below each ad description," according to Search Engine Roundtable.
The example comes from a mobile results page for the query "seo browser." The page shows a signed-out user, with a tab row reading AI Mode, All, Images, Shopping, Videos and Forums. Two text ads fit on screen beneath the "Sponsored Results" header. The first is from Semrush, with the headline "SEO Page Tester" and a display path of semrush.com, seo, tools. The second is from Online Marketing Gurus, an agency with a .com.au domain, with the headline "Free Custom SEO Audit."
The generated text does not appear at once. In the still published by Search Engine Roundtable, the space under the header and the space under each ad description are filled with grey placeholder bars. "When the AI-generated summaries are loading, there is the loading animation, to keep us entertained," Schwartz wrote.
A later frame from the same video, reproduced by PPC News Feed, shows the loaded state. Directly under the header, one sentence reads: "Semrush, Online Marketing Gurus, Syntactics, Inc., and First Page Australia feature marketing software or services, not simple browser-based tools." Each brand name is underlined. Only two of the four ads named in that sentence fit within the frame; the Syntactics and First Page Australia ads are not visible.
Below each ad description sits a second generated line, marked by a small sparkle icon and followed by an expand arrow. Under the Semrush ad, it begins: "Semrush provides a platform for site audits, keyword research, and AI-enabled SEO data, but it is a marketing." The text is cut off there. Under the Online Marketing Gurus ad, the line begins: "Online Marketing Gurus provides SEO and GEO (Generative Engine Optimisation) campaigns, but this is a." Neither report shows how either sentence ends.
Clark's own description covered every element. "Google's sponsored results on mobile are increasingly becoming more generative," he wrote, according to Search Engine Roundtable. "Not only is there a loading animation for this example, but each individual ad has its own description for the business, and then there's an additional description at the top of the sponsored results that mentions the brand names that are listed with an additional link to their ads."
PPC News Feed's account is shorter. "These summaries appear above and below the sponsored results, providing an overview of all ads and additional descriptions for each ad. A loading animation is also part of this feature," according to PPC News Feed, which credited the find to Clark and the report to Schwartz. The frames place the per-ad text inside each ad unit, under the advertiser's description, rather than below the sponsored block as a whole.
The elements in the example
The country is not stated in either report. Two of the four advertisers, Online Marketing Gurus and First Page Australia, point to an Australian results page, and the Online Marketing Gurus ad pitched "SEO Marketing For Australian Companies." Clark's earlier sighting of an AI label on Search ads in April also came from Australia, as PPC Land reported in May.
How the observed layout loads
According to the two reports and the frames they published, the sequence runs in five steps.
- A query is entered on Google mobile search. The published example uses "seo browser."
- The "Sponsored Results" header appears, with grey placeholder bars below it and below each ad description while the generated text loads.
- A single summary sentence fills the space under the header. It names every advertiser in the group, including two whose ads sit below the visible frame. Each name is underlined and, according to Clark, links to that advertiser's ad.
- The advertisers' own names, display URLs, headlines and descriptions appear in the usual format.
- A generated line appears under each ad description, next to a sparkle icon and followed by an expand arrow.
Neither report describes what happens when a searcher taps an underlined name or the expand arrow.
A summary that qualifies the ads
What stands out is less the placement than the content. The group sentence did not describe the advertisers in promotional terms. It drew a line between what had been searched and what was on offer, telling the user that the four companies sell "marketing software or services, not simple browser-based tools." Both per-ad lines also turn on the word "but" before they are cut off.
The ads themselves read differently. Semrush's description promoted a newly live MCP library and claimed that 57% of consumers use AI to shop. The Online Marketing Gurus ad offered SEO for AI search and a complimentary audit. Google's line under the agency's ad described it as offering SEO and GEO campaigns, a reasonable paraphrase, before turning to a qualification that is not visible.
For an advertiser paying for the click, this is an unusual arrangement. The ad holds a paid position and carries the advertiser's headline, yet the first sentence a searcher reads in the block was generated by Google and, in this instance, questioned the fit between the ads and the query before any headline appeared. Would such a line cut wasted clicks, or simply cut clicks? Neither report measures it, and Google has published no data on the test.
Google has lately been putting its own assessments inside the ad unit. In June 2026, Ginny Marvin, Google Ads Product Liaison, announced a test of "Strongest match" and "Strong match" badges built on existing ad quality and relevance signals, shown to a small percentage of US users, PPC Land reported. Those badges signalled relevance. The November summary, by contrast, pointed to its absence.
There is also a query-matching backdrop. Google began automatically upgrading broad match Search campaigns to AI Max for Search on September 1, 2026, with search term matching switched on by default, as Google told advertisers in an email on August 5. Nothing in either report shows how Semrush, Online Marketing Gurus, Syntactics or First Page Australia built their campaigns, or which keywords triggered their ads on "seo browser." Looser matching, though, widens the set of queries an ad can appear on, and a generated sentence that names the gap sits at the end of that chain.
What Google has and has not said
Google has not commented on the November test in either source, and no Google help page found describes a summary placed above the whole sponsored group or a sentence that names several advertisers at once.
The nearest documentation covers review summaries. That help page, titled "About Review summaries," describes AI-generated summaries of online reviews that appear as part of individual Search ads. Google says they are positive only, eligible in all positions on Google.com, available in English in the US, UK, Australia and India, and excluded from sensitive categories such as pharmaceuticals, as PPC Land reported on October 25. Advertisers cannot edit that text. The November example does not fit that description neatly. The per-ad lines describe what each business does rather than what reviewers said, and the visible wording sets up a qualification rather than praise. Whether the two belong to the same system is not stated anywhere.
Google has spoken about two related experiments. When Darcy Burk spotted AI-generated summaries below Search ad descriptions in July 2026, a Google spokesperson told Search Engine Roundtable that it was a small experiment to see whether AI-generated context in Search ads helps people make more informed decisions. That version carried a disclaimer warning that Google's AI responses can make mistakes. In August 2026, after Sachin Patel spotted a loading animation for sponsored results that displayed the Gemini logo, Google told Search Engine Roundtable that it was not using Gemini to load sponsored results and that it was testing loading animations with and without the Gemini logo.
No disclaimer is visible in the November frames. Whether one appears after the per-ad text is expanded, or elsewhere on the page, is not shown. Generated text that describes a named business, inside a paid unit, carries the familiar risk of hallucination, which the July disclaimer acknowledged.
A year of generated text in the ad block
The November example is the latest in a run of sightings. Schwartz placed it in that line himself. "We've seen this before on sponsored ads and sponsored products but I am not sure if we've seen the summary at the top of the ads," according to Search Engine Roundtable. PPC News Feed listed four earlier posts of its own alongside the report, covering review summaries, summaries under ad descriptions, a repeat review summary test, and longer summaries for brand ads in AI Mode.
Semrush appears twice in that history. Its ad carried the "What customers love" block in September, and it heads the group sentence in November. Nothing in the sources suggests that Semrush took part in either test by arrangement.
Why it matters for advertisers
The test moves Google-generated copy from inside the ad to above it. Advertisers have, for some time, accepted generated components within their own ads. In February 2024, Google completed the rollout of generative AI in automatically created assets for English-language advertisers in the US and UK, writing headlines and descriptions for responsive search ads and Performance Max as an opt-in, campaign-level setting. In September 2025, Google introduced text guidelines allowing up to 25 term exclusions and 40 messaging restrictions per campaign, and in February 2026 that beta went global for AI Max and Performance Max.
Those controls govern text that Google writes on one advertiser's behalf, inside that advertiser's ad. A single sentence about four competitors belongs to none of them. Whose term exclusions would apply to it, if any? Google has not said.
The grouping itself has history. On October 13, 2025, Google placed text ads under a single "Sponsored results" label with a control to hide them, and said users would never see more than four text ads in a grouping, PPC Land reported. That design had first been spotted weeks earlier, on September 24, 2025, when a Vancouver SEO consultant documented the grouped header. The November group summary named four advertisers, the stated maximum. A label that once marked a container now, in this test, carries copy of its own.
