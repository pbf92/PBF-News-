TITLE: NAD supplement raises blood levels but fails to help Parkinson's
OUTLET: News-Medical

Once hailed as a promising way to slow Parkinson's disease, nicotinamide riboside failed its biggest clinical test yet, with patients taking the supplement experiencing slightly worse outcomes despite a measurable boost in blood NAD levels.
Study: Nicotinamide Riboside for Patients With Early-Stage Parkinson Disease. The NOPARK Randomized Clinical Trial. Image credit: mapo_japan/Shutterstock.com
A recent study published online in the journal JAMA explored the potential benefits of nicotinamide riboside as a disease-modifying agent in early-stage Parkinson Disease (PD), following promising findings in two early placebo-controlled clinical trials. However, the authors report no evidence to support its use in this phase 3 trial.
Could boosting NAD levels slow Parkinson's progression?
Parkinson disease is a leading cause of neurological disability worldwide, yet existing treatments can only manage symptoms rather than slow disease progression. This has prompted researchers to explore new approaches, including boosting cellular levels of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), a molecule involved in several processes essential for neuronal health.
The rationale comes from preclinical research showing that increasing NAD levels can influence mitochondrial function, protein handling, oxidative stress, inflammation, and DNA repair. Nicotinamide riboside, an oral precursor of NAD, has also demonstrated neuroprotective effects in several animal models of Parkinson disease, raising hopes that it could help protect neurons from degeneration.
Encouraging results from animal studies prompted researchers to investigate whether NAD supplementation could also benefit people with Parkinson disease. Initial uncontrolled trials of intravenous NAD reported possible improvements in Parkinson symptoms, prompting more rigorous investigations of nicotinamide riboside in two randomized, placebo-controlled trials.
In the NADPARK trial, patients receiving 500 mg of nicotinamide riboside twice daily for 30 days showed increased brain NAD levels, changes in brain metabolism, and reduced inflammatory markers in cerebrospinal fluid. The subsequent NR-SAFE trial found that a higher dose of 1500 mg twice daily for four weeks was well tolerated and increased systemic NAD metabolism. Both trials also reported preliminary signals of symptom improvement on the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS).
However, these short-term findings did not establish whether nicotinamide riboside could deliver lasting clinical benefits or slow Parkinson disease progression. With the compound already widely available as an over-the-counter supplement, the need for more definitive evidence was particularly relevant. The NOPARK trial was therefore designed to test whether a year of treatment could translate these early promising findings into meaningful clinical improvements.
Year-long trial compares NAD supplement against placebo
To determine whether nicotinamide riboside could improve outcomes in early Parkinson disease, researchers conducted the NOPARK trial across 11 sites in Norway. The year-long, phase 3 study enrolled 410 people aged 35 years or older who had been diagnosed with Parkinson disease within the previous two years. All had relatively early-stage disease, with a Hoehn and Yahr stage below 3, and brain scans confirming damage to the dopamine-producing nerve system.
Participants were already receiving treatment for Parkinson symptoms, with their medications standardized to levodopa-based regimens before the trial began. Researchers then randomly assigned 206 participants to receive 500 mg of nicotinamide riboside twice daily and 204 to receive a matching placebo. Neither the participants nor the researchers knew who was receiving the supplement.
Over the following 52 weeks, the researchers monitored changes in symptoms and treatment safety through regular assessments. Their main focus was whether nicotinamide riboside improved overall Parkinson disease severity, measured using the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS), which captures motor symptoms, daily functioning, and other aspects of the disease. Higher scores indicate greater impairment.
The team also examined whether treatment affected individual symptom measures, nonmotor problems such as sleep and mood disturbances, and dopamine transporter activity on brain scans, which can provide clues about disease progression.
Of the 410 participants randomized, 393 were included in the main analysis population, with 391 contributing to the primary outcome assessment. Their average age was 65.6 years, and 34% were women. Treatment adherence was high, with participants in both groups taking approximately 99% of their assigned doses.
Parkinson's symptoms worsened with nicotinamide riboside
After a year of treatment, patients taking nicotinamide riboside experienced slightly worse Parkinson symptoms than those receiving placebo. Their overall symptom scores increased by 2.45 points, compared with a slight improvement of 0.27 points in the placebo group. Although the difference of 2.72 points was statistically significant, it was smaller than the threshold generally considered clinically meaningful.
The differences between the groups became more apparent as the trial progressed. Both groups initially improved during the first three months, although the improvements were smaller among patients taking nicotinamide riboside. Symptoms then gradually worsened in the supplement group, while patients receiving placebo continued to improve until around six months, before their symptoms also began to worsen.
Importantly, the findings were consistent across the researchers' analyses, with no convincing evidence that any particular group of patients responded differently to treatment. This suggests that the lack of benefit was not confined to a specific patient population.
Nicotinamide riboside linked to greater nonmotor symptom burden
Nicotinamide riboside showed no significant benefit over placebo in measures of motor symptoms, daily functioning, or other aspects of the disease. Brain scans also showed no significant difference between groups in changes in dopamine transporter binding, a marker used to assess the loss of dopaminergic nerve terminals.
More concerningly, patients taking nicotinamide riboside had poorer outcomes on a separate scale measuring nonmotor symptoms, which include problems such as sleep disturbances, mood changes, and cognitive difficulties. The difference was statistically significant, adding to evidence that the supplement did not improve clinical outcomes.
Quality-of-life scores were also slightly lower among patients receiving nicotinamide riboside, although this was an exploratory finding. However, separate assessments of cognitive function and overall disease stage showed no significant differences between the groups.
Higher blood NAD failed to translate into symptom relief
Total blood NAD was about 30 μM higher with nicotinamide riboside, confirming its systemic effect. However, among 159 participants assessed using brain spectroscopy, the change in cerebral NAD/ATP-α ratio did not differ significantly at week 52 (difference 0.0057).
Overall adverse event rates remained similar between groups
The researchers found adverse events at similar rates between groups (54.6% vs 53.7%). Hypertension (9.8% vs 6.4%) and folate deficiency (4.9% vs 2.4%) were numerically more frequent with nicotinamide riboside.
Serious adverse events were less frequent with nicotinamide riboside (8.3% vs 14.1%), though the difference was not statistically significant. There were two deaths, one in each group.
Exploring why promising NAD therapy failed
The authors conclude that nicotinamide riboside did not improve outcomes and was associated with modestly worse outcomes. They defend the robustness of the result by pointing to its consistency across clinical endpoints and analyses.
They considered whether placebo-group improvement could be explained by dopaminergic therapy optimization during screening. They acknowledge that treatment optimization may partly explain this improvement, but found little evidence that differences in dopaminergic treatment explained the poorer outcomes with nicotinamide riboside, since medication exposure and later changes were balanced. The unexpectedly limited progression in the placebo group may also have reduced the trial's sensitivity to disease-modifying effects.
The absence of significant differences in DaT-SPECT binding between groups argues against accelerated neurodegeneration, though the authors caution that this method is not very sensitive over one year. The lack of a sustained rise in brain NAD, despite higher blood levels, may reflect adaptation over time or limited sensitivity of spectroscopy.
To explain the results, the authors offer two hypotheses, while stressing that both remain unproven. One, they speculate that sustained NAD augmentation has unfavorable effects in Parkinson disease. Alternatively, nicotinamide riboside may interact with dopaminergic therapy. However, the worsening of nonmotor symptoms makes a purely dopaminergic explanation less likely.
Ongoing Parkinson's medication complicated treatment effect assessment
The authors list several limitations. The assessments were made on medication, potentially reducing sensitivity to small differences in motor function, and there was no washout phase to separate symptomatic from disease-modifying effects. DaT-SPECT imaging is of limited sensitivity and may not detect subtle changes in neurodegeneration over one year.
Major trial challenges nicotinamide riboside use in Parkinson's
In this year-long, 410-person phase 3 trial, nicotinamide riboside 500 mg twice daily did not help people with early Parkinson disease. It was associated with slightly greater worsening on the primary measure and on nonmotor symptoms. The authors say the findings do not support its use as a disease-modifying or symptomatic treatment.
