TITLE: UK Survey Finds Sharp Drop in Youth Sex Amid Surge in Online Habits
OUTLET: Dosya Haber

LONDON — A landmark national health survey published in The Lancet reveals that one-third of Britons aged 16 to 24 have never had sex, underscoring a generational shift in intimacy that mirrors broader behavioral trends across high-income nations, including the United States.
Conducted between 2022 and 2024 among nearly 7,000 participants, the latest National Surveys of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal) found that adults aged 45 and under now have sex an average of twice a month. That figure marks a steady decline from four times a month in 1990–91 and three times a month in 2010–12. Among 16- to 24-year-olds, the share reporting no sexual experience climbed from 18 percent to 38 percent for women and from 18 percent to 28 percent for men compared with the previous Natsal study.
Digital Intimacy Takes Center Stage
For the first time, researchers tracked online sexual behavior alongside physical activity. Half of women and 86 percent of men aged 16 to 24 reported watching pornography within the past year. Meanwhile, half of respondents in that same age bracket said they had sent or received sexual images on their phones over the previous 12 months. The study authors noted that modern sexual lifestyles among young adults now routinely incorporate digital behaviors.
Multiple Factors Behind the Generational Shift
Despite the stark contrast between declining physical intimacy and rising screen-based activity, researchers caution against drawing a simple cause-and-effect conclusion. Prof. Wendy Macdowall of the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine noted that several converging factors are likely driving the change.
According to the study, similar declines in other high-income countries have coincided with rising rates of mental health conditions, lower alcohol consumption, delayed traditional milestones of adulthood, and increasingly online daily lives. Public health experts say further research is needed to understand how these overlapping shifts will shape long-term relationship patterns and reproductive health.
