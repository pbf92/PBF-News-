TITLE: Character.ai still faces Kentucky's chatbot lawsuit during bankruptcy
OUTLET: PPC Land

Character Technologies, the company behind the Character.ai chatbot app, is keeping a Kentucky law firm on its defense against the lawsuit brought by the state's attorney general, even as its remaining assets head toward a sale in bankruptcy. A declaration filed on November 24, 2026 in the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware shows that FBT Gibbons LLP, a Louisville firm, will act as local counsel in the case Attorney General Russell Coleman filed in Franklin Circuit Court in January. The firm is owed $11,180.90 for work done before the company filed for Chapter 11, holds no retainer and has no indemnification agreement with the company.
In Short
Character.ai's parent company filed papers on November 24 to keep a Louisville law firm, FBT Gibbons, working on its defense against the lawsuit Kentucky's attorney general brought in January 2026. The state case is still one the bankrupt company is paying lawyers to fight while its app and users' chats head toward a sale, and those legal bills come out of the same pot of money that creditors, including tech and marketing suppliers, hope to be paid from. Objections to the hiring are due by December 15, and the filing does not say whether the company treats Kentucky's case as paused by the bankruptcy.
What was filed
The document is Docket No. 243 in case 26-11603 (BLS), In re Character Technologies, Inc., before Judge Brendan L. Shannon. It is titled "Declaration in Support of Employment of FBT Gibbons LLP as Professional Utilized in Ordinary Course of Business." Theresa A. Canaday, a partner at the firm's office at 400 West Market Street in Louisville, signed it on November 20. It reached the docket four days later.
The court copy runs to eight pages: a three-page declaration and a five-page Exhibit 1, the list of parties in interest that the company's lawyers gave the firm to check for conflicts.
Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11 on October 12, 2026, and runs its business during the case as a debtor in possession, as the declaration's own wording confirms. PPC Land reported at the outset that the company had lined up Disney's $20 million bid for a license to its intellectual property, not the app.
The purpose fits in one sentence of paragraph 1: "The Debtor wishes to retain the Firm to provide legal defense against litigation pending in Franklin County, Kentucky during this chapter 11 case, and the Firm has consented to provide such services."
Paragraph 4 names the case. FBT Gibbons will "serve as local counsel in the case, Commonwealth of Kentucky, ex rel. Russell Coleman, Attorney General v. Character Technologies, Inc., et al., Case No. 26-CI-00029, Franklin Circuit Court, Kentucky."
Local counsel is a firm based where a case is heard. It works alongside lead lawyers from elsewhere and typically handles filings and court appearances under the local court's rules. Franklin County is home to Frankfort, the state capital.
Who leads? A second declaration filed the same day answers that. Docket No. 246, from Munger, Tolles & Olson LLP in Los Angeles, lists the Kentucky case, in Division 1 of the Franklin Circuit Court, first among three suits that firm will defend. The work it describes includes motions, discovery, document production and negotiations with the other side. The other two cases are the Pennsylvania State Board of Medicine's petition and a federal suit brought in California on behalf of a minor identified as A.D.
Two firms, one state case
Munger, Tolles & Olson figures are from Docket No. 246.
By PPC Land's calculation, the two firms are owed $157,562.89 between them for work done before the bankruptcy. That total overstates the cost of the Kentucky case alone, because Munger's figure covers all its work for the company and its declaration does not split it by suit. FBT Gibbons's bill is about 7.6% of Munger's, also by PPC Land's calculation.
The old bill is set out in paragraph 9: "The Debtor owes the Firm $11,180.90 for fees and expenses incurred before and unpaid as of Petition Date, the payment of which is subject to the limitations contained in the Bankruptcy Code."
In plain terms, money owed for work before the filing does not get paid simply because the firm is hired again. It becomes a claim against the company, handled under the bankruptcy rules alongside other unsecured debts, meaning debts with no collateral behind them. Paragraphs 10 and 11 close two other doors. "As of the Petition Date, the Firm holds no prepetition retainer," the declaration states, so there is no deposit the firm could draw on. And "the Firm was not party to an agreement for indemnification with the Debtor," so the company has not promised to cover the firm's own legal exposure.
How ordinary course hiring works
A company in Chapter 11 cannot simply keep paying outside advisers. Each one needs some form of court approval. For lawyers, accountants and consultants who do routine work unrelated to the bankruptcy itself, courts often approve a lighter procedure. According to the court docket and the declaration, the route that brings a firm like FBT Gibbons onto Character.ai's books runs in five steps.
- The motion. On November 3, 2026, the company asked the court for permission to retain and pay professionals used in the ordinary course of its business, as Docket No. 104. The docket set an objection deadline of November 10 and a hearing date of November 17.
- The order. Judge Shannon signed the order on November 13, as Docket No. 162, with a Schedule 1 and an Exhibit B attached, according to the docket. The declaration gives its full title: "Order (I) Authorizing the Debtor to Retain and Compensate Certain Professionals Utilized in Ordinary Course of Business; and (II) Granting Related Relief." The same day, the court also approved the hiring of four bankruptcy advisers, while the company's investment bank waited for a hearing.
- The sworn declaration. Each firm files a declaration under penalty of perjury. FBT Gibbons's covers the services it will provide, any fee sharing (none), any interest adverse to the company "with respect to the matter(s) upon which this Firm is to be employed" (none, "insofar as I have been able to ascertain"), what it is owed from before the filing, any retainer and any indemnity. Canaday also states: "I have reviewed the Ordinary Course Professionals Order, and I understand the limitations on compensation and reimbursement thereunder."
- The objection window. The caption sets an objection deadline of "December 15, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. (ET)," 21 days after the filing by PPC Land's calculation.
- Updates. The firm undertakes to supplement the declaration "if the Firm should discover any facts bearing on the matters described herein."
The declaration does not restate the limits it refers to, such as any monthly fee ceiling, and the order itself is not attached.
Why the order matters in practice is visible in the company's first monthly operating report, filed on November 23. It disclosed that on October 29 the company paid $19,920 to accounting firm Frank, Rimerman + Co. LLP for 2025 tax returns, before the ordinary course order existed. The report said the payment was made by mistake and that the company would ask for the money back pending the firm's retention under the order.
The Kentucky case
Kentucky's interest in the company predates the bankruptcy by more than 18 months. The attorney general's office issued a civil investigative demand, a formal demand for documents and answers before any lawsuit, on March 26, 2025 under the Kentucky Consumer Protection Act. It sued Character Technologies and its co-founders in Franklin Circuit Court on January 8, 2026. Both dates come from the chief executive's first-day declaration, which PPC Land covered in October. That declaration put the case at the motion-to-dismiss stage, with settlement talks continuing.
The complaint runs to 52 pages. A coalition of 39 states and the District of Columbia attached it to the notice of interest it filed on October 14, and quoted figures it drew from the company's own records: more than 21.6 million US accounts created by March 13, 2025, more than 2.1 million of them with a declared age under 18. The copy filed with that notice carried a Franklin Circuit Clerk stamp dated October 7, 2026. That notice also said that Kentucky was the only state to have sued both the company and its founders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas.
Kentucky's is the older of the two government lawsuits listed in the chief executive's declaration. Pennsylvania's Department of State, for the State Board of Medicine, sued in May 2026 over a chatbot that presented itself as a licensed psychiatrist. Texas, Florida and New Mexico have open investigations, according to the chief executive's declaration.
The Kentucky suit also sits at the center of the states' demands on the sale. When 42 states and the District of Columbia objected to the $10 million sale of the app and its data, they cited chat histories as evidence in Kentucky's enforcement action and asked that any order bar the destruction of data held under a litigation hold, as PPC Land reported.
Paused or not?
Filing for bankruptcy normally freezes lawsuits against a company. The automatic stay takes effect the moment a petition is filed and halts most attempts to collect debts or press claims. Section 362(b)(4) of the Bankruptcy Code carves out an exception for actions by a government to enforce its police and regulatory power.
The company's first-day papers touched on that exception. PPC Land reported that a proposed order on payments preserved government enforcement actions to the extent not otherwise stayed, and that the creditor list named the Commonwealth of Kentucky and the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania as litigation claimants. The motion did not say whether the company regarded either case as stayed.
The new declaration does not settle the point. It does, however, describe the Kentucky suit as "litigation pending" and the hiring as being "during this chapter 11 case." Read together with the Munger declaration, which lists discovery and document production among its tasks, the filings describe a case the company expects to need lawyers for in the coming weeks. Neither declaration gives a hearing date or a deadline in Frankfort.
The conflicts list
Exhibit 1 is the same list the company attached on November 18 to the declaration of Alex Feerst, Docket No. 189, the first ordinary course professional to file, as PPC Land noted in its report on the privacy ombudsman. Page headers in the court copy carry both numbers, 189-1 and 243-1.
It names six ordinary course professionals: Minami Tamaki LLP, Alex Jonathan Feerst, KPMG, Frank Rimerman, FBT Gibbons LLP and Jaymie Scotto & Associates. KPMG is also the subject of a separate retention application filed the same day, Docket No. 244.
Under "Litigation and Investigation Counterparties," the list names the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, the Pennsylvania Department of State, the Commonwealth of Kentucky, Italy's data protection authority (Garante per la Protezione dei Dati Personali), the US Department of Justice's Consumer Protection Branch and the Federal Trade Commission. Attorney general offices follow: 50 of them by PPC Land's count, covering 47 states, the District of Columbia, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands. Kentucky appears twice, as the Commonwealth and as its attorney general's office.
FBT Gibbons has not finished its own check.
