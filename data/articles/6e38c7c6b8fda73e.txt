TITLE: JudeLuxe lost a client two months in despite higher ROAS, Avery says
OUTLET: PPC Land

An ecommerce client fired JudeLuxe two months into the relationship even though return on ad spend and contribution had both risen and spend had fallen, according to the agency's co-founder, Chris Avery, who told the story in an episode of PPC Live The Podcast published on YouTube on November 19, 2026.
In Short
An agency improved a retailer's return on ad spend and margin within two months and was dismissed anyway, because what the retailer actually needed was to clear seasonal stock and get cash back into the bank. The story shows how far a platform target can drift from what a business needs, and it touches every agency, freelancer and in-house team that accepts a target without asking where it came from. At JudeLuxe, Avery says, stock, cash and trading priorities are now discussed before anyone talks about the Google Ads account.
The review that ended the account
The episode, hosted by PPC Live founder Anu Adegbola, belongs to a series built around a single premise: a paid search practitioner describes a serious mistake and what followed. Avery's choice took the conversation back "four or five years", to a period when JudeLuxe was young, run by two people and, in his description, eager to prove itself.
The agency had won the account from an incumbent that Avery estimated at 20 to 30 times its size, "with layers upon layers of people, account managers, account directors, account executives." There was, he said, "definitely some pride involved" in taking business from a competitor of that scale.
For two months, the metrics moved in the direction JudeLuxe wanted, ROAS among them. "When we looked at the numbers, [ROAS] was up, contribution percentage was up, spend was down," Avery said, describing the cuts as removing "what we would or most people would define as waste." The agency built "a full slide deck for the review" and went into the meeting expecting praise. On the call were the client's managing director, finance director and operations director.
"Like, these numbers, man. Like, look at them. Like, they're all green," Avery recalled thinking.
The verdict arrived afterwards, by email: "we're ending the relationship. We're going to settle your invoice prior." The notice period, Avery said, was zero. His first reaction was not reflective. "How on earth can they sack us with results like that?"
What the numbers missed
The client was, in Avery's words, "heavily weighted towards autumn and winter trading", and JudeLuxe had taken over in March. By then the retailer had "stock sitting in a warehouse costing them money" and needed to move it.
At the start of the engagement the agency had been given a ROAS target. Avery noted that such a target is naturally an average across the account, and said the agency treated it as "basically the brief." It improved the metrics it was working towards. What it missed, he said, was that "they had stock that they needed to clear and cash was short in the bank", and that "the commercial output that was needed from the Google ad account had changed and we hadn't been proactive enough to figure out why it had changed."
The list of unasked questions was long. Nobody at the agency had asked what stock was left, what needed clearing, how quickly, what holding it was costing the business, or which trade-offs the business would accept. Avery offered a hypothetical: against a 4.5 ROAS, "would you have taken a three [ROAS] if it meant that we could clear all the stock". He was even-handed about the blame. "They hadn't told us, but equally we hadn't asked."
That point about averages has surfaced in other forms on PPC Land this year. A measurement guide from Funnel, published this autumn, worked through an example in which $1,000 of spend returns $3,000, a ROAS of 3.0, while the next $500 adds only $200 - a marginal return of $0.40 per dollar. An account can look healthy on its average and be wasteful at the margin, or, as in Avery's case, look efficient while failing at a different job altogether.
Google has also built mechanisms aimed at the kind of trade Avery described. Promotion mode, announced on June 15, 2026, is a beta for Search and Performance Max that runs scheduled windows of 3 to 14 days, adjusting ROAS tolerance and adding daily budget, with a built-in end date. Separately, Smart Bidding Exploration lets Target ROAS advertisers accept a target 5% to 30% lower on Search, Performance Max and Shopping campaigns, according to a Google help page first reported in October. Neither tool answers the question Avery says his agency failed to ask, which is whether the business wants that trade in the first place.
The "button pushing" agency
Avery was blunt about JudeLuxe at the time. "I would define us as what I would now call a button pushing agency," he said. "You would give us a budget and a target and we'd get to work. You know, campaigns, bids, search terms, spend."
The problem, as he framed it, was a narrow idea of the job. "So when they gave us that [ROAS] target, we didn't see the start of a conversation, we thought we'd been given the answer." The same request now gets a different response. When a prospect says it needs "a [ROAS] of five", Avery said, the first reply is now "based on what?", followed by questions about categories and acceptable trade-offs.
He also questioned the assumption, learned early, that profit is always the goal. Cutting inefficient spend and improving ROAS look like sound decisions, but "most ecom businesses die because they run out of money." The agency had believed it was ahead of the field because it tracked contribution alongside ROAS. "We probably thought we were being a bit more commercially sophisticated because we were looking at contribution as well as [ROAS]," he said. Changing the metric did not change the thinking: "you can misunderstand a business while looking at a profit metric just as you can looking at a revenue metric".
The client, to its credit, explained. According to Avery, it "essentially gave us a business 101, which was nice of them", walking the agency through how an ecommerce business makes and loses money. Adegbola's response was dry. "They thought you'd know."
Avery accepted the point. "If I was in the brand shoes, I would expect an ecom PPC agency to understand that as well," he said. He estimated that about 80% of JudeLuxe's time on client calls is now spent on matters other than Google Ads, because competence inside the platform is expected - "table stakes", in his phrase. The same applies to hiring. He no longer asks candidates how to amend a target ROAS; he asks "how do you calculate a sell through rate for a client".
How JudeLuxe's onboarding changed
Avery described the lesson as more than adding a line about stock to an onboarding document. According to his account, the process now runs in six stages:
- Challenge the objective. "Now we believe part of our responsibility as an agency is actually to challenge the objective that we're given," Avery said, "not in a hostile way". He likened the approach to a three-year-old repeating "But why?": why this target, why these products, why now, what happens if it is achieved, and what has to be sacrificed along the way.
- Hold the commercial conversation first. The agency tells prospects that "we need to have a commercial conversation here first before we can even think about talking about your platform whatsoever". The right channel might be Google Ads, affiliates or Meta, which JudeLuxe does not manage.
- Look at the catalogue, not the change history. Avery said his old habit was to open the account's change history and judge the previous agency by its activity - "another story where you confuse activity with progress". The agency now asks how many SKUs a client carries, how long they have been in stock, what their sell-through rates are, what the priorities are, and whether merchandising has over-ordered a particular style.
- Widen the room. Five or six years ago, he said, an agency dealt only with the marketing manager or the ecommerce manager. JudeLuxe now has more conversations with finance, merchandising, operations and ecommerce as a whole.
- Set targets after stock and trading priorities. The agency now asks "about stock and trading priorities" before any discussion of targets in the Google Ads account, he said.
- Revisit the targets. On audits, Avery said, he asks when a ROAS target was set. One answer he quoted was "Two years ago." His follow-up: "Did someone forget to let Google Ads know?"
Change history is not a complete record in any case. In October 2024, changes made by a Google Ads representative to a UK client's account did not appear in it at all.
Avery's view of paid search as a magnifier ran underneath all six stages. Because the channel is predominantly direct response, it amplifies the current state of the business: put 10, 20, 30, 40 or 50 thousand pounds more behind a business that is a mess, he said, and the result is only more mess.
Stock is a diminishing asset
Avery's phrase for inventory that ties up money is cash in cardboard. His position on ROAS has softened since the days when, he said, many of his posts attacked the metric. "[ROAS] still has a place," he said. When the job is releasing cash into the business, he would run campaigns on ROAS or revenue. When the job is profit, a contribution-based model may fit better. Chasing profit while cash sits in stock, he argued, means optimising towards the wrong metric.
The episode's introduction set out JudeLuxe's framework for that choice. The agency has built an approach it calls BOI, short for bid on intent, which Adegbola described as starting from one question: what does the business need this product to do?
"We always say stock is a diminishing asset," Avery said, using UK Soccer Shop, a JudeLuxe client that sells football shirts, as the example. New shirts arrive in May. "They've probably got a window of 3 to four months where they can sell the stock at full price." After that come discounts, then clearance, "and then it's a different target and nobody's letting Google ads know".
He has a word for this, one he admitted might not exist: dynamicism, meaning how quickly an ecommerce business changes. "You can ask that question today but in two months time the answer might be completely different," he said. Google, in his view, will not notice on its own. "Because Google isn't sophisticated enough to do that."
Adegbola made a similar point about the machine's inputs. "It's going to do what you ask it to do on the level of knowledge that you give it. And if that's basic knowledge, it's going to give you basic performance." Avery added "outdated knowledge" to the list, and put the problem in currency terms: "Google can sit there and it can treat £100 in one product the same as £100 in another." The commercial reality behind two products can be completely different. "But to Google £100 is £100."
A stale target is arguably more consequential now than when JudeLuxe lost its client. Under Google's August 17, 2026 bidding change, budget-limited Target CPA and Target ROAS campaigns that beat their targets are pulled back towards those targets. One advertiser said a Shopping campaign set at a 300% target ROAS lost sales after the change, a single account with no data published. For customer acquisition, one of the four BOI jobs, Google has its own lever: percentage multipliers from 1.1x to 5x on new customer values left beta in October.
