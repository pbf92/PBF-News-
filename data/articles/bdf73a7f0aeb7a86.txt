TITLE: Systematic Review Finds High Neurodevelopmental Disorder Rates in Smith-Magenis Syndrome
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review investigating Smith-Magenis syndrome has revealed strikingly high proportions of autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder characteristics co-occurring alongside intellectual developmental disorders. Led by researcher Pauline Boiroux and colleagues, the investigation systematically synthesized international peer-reviewed literature to evaluate the prevalence, assessment methodology, and genotype-phenotype correlations of neurodevelopmental conditions in individuals affected by this rare genetic condition. Smith-Magenis syndrome arises either from an interstitial deletion within the chromosome 17p11.2 locus or from a pathogenic variant in the retinoic acid induced 1 gene, known as RAI1. While the syndrome has historically been recognized for moderate intellectual disability and distinct sleep disturbances tied to inverted circadian melatonin secretion, precise epidemiological estimates of specific psychiatric and neurodevelopmental diagnoses across affected populations have remained historically fragmented.
To rigorously quantify these diagnostic patterns, the investigators executed a pre-registered review adhering strictly to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, documented under PROSPERO registration number CRD42024512675. A specialized librarian conducted exhaustive searches across major scientific repositories on January 31, 2024, querying PubMed and MEDLINE, the Cochrane Library via Wiley, Web of Science via Clarivate Analytics, and Embase through Elsevier. The screening protocol evaluated original research articles published in peer-reviewed scientific journals that supplied empirical prevalence figures regarding neurodevelopmental disorders in confirmed Smith-Magenis syndrome cohorts. After removing non-eligible contributions, screening titles and abstracts independently across multiple evaluators, and examining full texts, 14 studies met all eligibility standards from an initial pool of 1,941 identified database records and seven review citations.
The collective evidence base comprised 451 individuals diagnosed with Smith-Magenis syndrome across nine countries, including the United States, France, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, Brazil, Norway, Sweden, Portugal, and Spain. The cumulative study population demonstrated an average chronological age of 13.9 years, spanning a broad demographic range between 1.5 and 51 years, with males constituting 51 percent of the total cohort. Within this aggregate clinical population, genetic etiologies were categorized, documenting 32 individuals carrying confirmed pathogenic variants in the RAI1 gene alongside larger cohorts presenting the classic 17p11.2 chromosomal deletion. Study authors systematically documented clinical characteristics, age distributions, diagnostic assessment instruments, organic and psychiatric comorbidities, and genetic variations to establish a clearer clinical picture.
Evaluations of autism spectrum disorder characteristics drew upon six studies examining cohorts totaling 220 patients, composed of 168 individuals with the 17p11.2 deletion, 10 with identified RAI1 gene mutations, and one study without specified genetic subgrouping. Across these investigations, the reported prevalence of autism spectrum disorder characteristics ranged between 14 percent and 95 percent, establishing an overall mean prevalence of 58 percent. When limiting analysis strictly to investigations utilizing standardized psychometric instruments, the minimum observed prevalence remained substantial at 52 percent. Notably, secondary stratification by underlying genotype revealed a marked divergence: individuals carrying the 17p11.2 deletion displayed an average autism spectrum trait prevalence of 43 percent, whereas patients carrying RAI1 pathogenic variants showed an average prevalence of 80 percent across reporting cohorts.
Assessment protocols for autistic traits across the identified literature displayed considerable methodological variation. Research teams utilized screening measures including the Child Behaviour CheckList, the Autism Screening Questionnaire where scores above 15 denote spectrum conditions and scores exceeding 22 signify autism, parental evaluation forms, and the Social Communication Questionnaire Lifetime form utilizing a cutoff score of 15 or greater. In addition, three studies evaluated the severity of social impairment, noting that 35 percent of individuals scored above pathological thresholds on current Social Communication Questionnaire scales, while two investigations utilizing the Social Responsiveness Scale identified profound social impairment affecting between 90 percent and 96 percent of participants.
Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder and related behavioral traits were similarly evaluated across six studies encompassing 174 patients from seven nations, representing 122 individuals with chromosomal deletions and 10 with RAI1 mutations. Within the reviewed literature, only a single study evaluated attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder through formal clinical diagnostic criteria using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, establishing a 100 percent diagnostic rate. Other cohorts measured individual symptomatic components using structured clinical interviews, behavioral checklists, clinical reports, or the Activity Questionnaire, documenting widespread impulsivity ranging from 49 percent to 86 percent with an overall mean of 78 percent, and hyperactivity ranging between 11.3 percent and 100 percent with an overall mean of 76 percent. Attention deficits were identified in 93 percent to 94.7 percent of individuals with deletions and 100 percent of individuals harboring RAI1 mutations, yielding an overall mean of 96 percent.
Intellectual developmental disorder was examined in nine included studies, showing prevalence rates spanning between 67 percent and 100 percent across affected subjects. Among individuals confirmed with the 17p11.2 chromosomal microdeletion, pooled results demonstrated an overall intellectual developmental disorder prevalence of 93 percent, with the greatest concentration manifesting as moderate cognitive impairment in 43 percent of patients. Conversely, among individuals with pathogenic RAI1 variants, intellectual developmental disorder was reported in 80 percent of individuals, presenting predominantly as mild intellectual disability in 69 percent of cases. Ten different psychometric instruments were utilized across studies to measure cognitive functioning, adapted according to participant age and impairment level, while specific learning disabilities were formally recorded in only one study, documenting a frequency of 9 percent among evaluated deletion carriers.
Methodological quality across the reviewed corpus was systematically appraised through an adapted four-domain framework addressing sample identification, syndromic confirmation, neurodevelopmental disorder evaluation, and data coverage. The assessment confirmed that data completeness was uniformly excellent and sample identification was adequate, while confirmation of the syndrome depended upon historical diagnostic tools available at the time of each study. Diagnostic evaluations of neurodevelopmental disorders exhibited considerable variability, ranking as poor in four studies, adequate in nine studies relying on standardized informant measures or established manualized criteria, and excellent in one study. The authors noted that studies with poorer evaluation quality, such as parental reports without standardized scales, introduced measurement noise, though overall reporting remained consistent with clinical expectations.
The review authors underscored that cognitive disparity between genetic subtypes highlights a noteworthy clinical paradox: individuals harboring RAI1 gene mutations frequently demonstrate higher overall cognitive performance and Full Scale Intelligence Quotient scores than deletion carriers, yet exhibit higher prevalence rates of autism and ADHD behavioral traits. This observation suggests that prominent neurodevelopmental symptoms in Smith-Magenis syndrome are not solely secondary byproducts of severe intellectual impairment, but rather represent primary clinical features of the underlying genetic alteration. Furthermore, structural neuroimaging literature cited in the review describes bilateral reductions in grey matter concentration within insulo-lenticular regions among individuals with Smith-Magenis syndrome, neuroanatomical regions similarly implicated in broader attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder neurobiology.
The authors emphasize substantial limitations within the existing literature, noting that the overall scarcity of studies and wide methodological heterogeneity precluded conducting a formal quantitative meta-analysis. Most available research relied heavily on general population screening instruments rather than tools validated specifically for Smith-Magenis syndrome, such as the Autism Diagnostic Interview or Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule. Furthermore, the confounding impact of characteristic circadian sleep disturbances and the clinical difficulty of disentangling behavioral manifestations from developmental delay complicate precise diagnostic attribution. The authors conclude that standardized, routine screening for autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder must be integrated alongside cognitive testing in clinical management to improve diagnostic precision, inform caregiver education, and guide targeted therapeutic interventions.
Clinical assessment in Smith-Magenis syndrome is further complicated by a distinct constellation of behavioral and sensory characteristics described across the broader scientific literature. Early development is frequently accompanied by specific maladaptive behaviors, including self-hugging, polyembolokoilamania, head banging, wrist biting, skin picking, onychotillomania, hand licking, and page flipping, alongside aggression, temper tantrums, and attention-seeking directed toward a preferential adult. However, earlier observational research indicates that positive behaviors tend to increase with age while negative behaviors decrease. Sensory processing difficulties have also been documented across all categories of the Sensory Profile in a cohort of 34 children with the syndrome, showing an association with impaired behavioral regulation and a trend toward extreme sensory processing behaviors in older females. In light of the pervasive attention-deficit and hyperactivity traits identified, the review authors noted that future clinical trials could assess the potential positive impact of pharmacological treatments such as methylphenidate.
Differentiating core neurodevelopmental conditions from underlying intellectual disability presents another complex challenge for clinical practice. To improve diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, the review outlines clinical guidance emphasizing that social skill development must be evaluated in relation to developmental age rather than chronological age alone. Evaluators are advised to examine whether social impairments and repetitive behaviors were present during infancy, how those features evolved over time, and whether the gap between social and other developmental abilities widened throughout maturation. This developmental perspective is critical because the intrinsic autism phenotype in Smith-Magenis syndrome may present atypically, marked by altered social communication despite a friendly phenotype alongside a reported reversed gender ratio.
