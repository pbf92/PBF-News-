TITLE: Direct Integration of GIS and AI in Tuberculosis Studies Remains Rare
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Direct integration of geographic information systems and artificial intelligence remains exceptionally rare in tuberculosis research, according to a systematic literature review published by Nur Adibah Mohidem and Sharifah Norkhadijah Syed Ismail in the International Journal of Health Geographics. Evaluating studies across medical and scientific databases, the authors determined that only two out of fourteen eligible investigations actually embedded spatial structures directly within their artificial intelligence modelling frameworks.
Tuberculosis continues to present a significant global health concern characterized by persistent spatial heterogeneity in incidence, patient mortality, and healthcare accessibility. Although geographic information systems and artificial intelligence tools have increasingly been deployed across epidemiology, their explicit integration within spatial epidemiological modelling has remained poorly defined. The systematic investigation aimed to clarify how these two methodologies currently converge in tuberculosis research.
To evaluate the scientific literature rigorously, the researchers conducted their review under the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. The comprehensive search captured literature published up to July 7, 2025, across Scopus, PubMed, the Cochrane Library, ScienceDirect, and Taylor & Francis. Following systematic screening, eligibility checks, and formal quality appraisal utilizing the MI-CLAIM framework, fourteen primary studies satisfied all criteria for final analysis.
The analyzed investigations addressed several distinct dimensions of tuberculosis epidemiology and disease management. Most included studies concentrated primarily on tuberculosis incidence and case counts. Other research teams investigated diagnostic procedures and medical treatment, patient mortality patterns, community-based public health applications, and the geographic distribution of Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains.
Across the corpus, researchers employed geographic information systems for varied analytical purposes. Spatial mapping and visual representation appeared frequently alongside spatial autocorrelation, hotspot analysis, and spatial clustering techniques. Additionally, investigators applied spatial regression, general spatial modelling, and spatial accessibility assessments, including specialized network analysis to evaluate access to healthcare services.
In parallel, the evaluated artificial intelligence methodologies spanned neural networks, deep learning architectures, tree-based and ensemble machine learning algorithms, and kernel-based machine learning. Models also featured Bayesian modelling, time-series or regression-based frameworks, and explainable artificial intelligence. However, rather than integrating these systems into unified structures, twelve of the fourteen studies applied geographic information systems and artificial intelligence independently, in parallel, or through sequential analytical pipelines.
The authors concluded that combining geographic information systems and artificial intelligence in tuberculosis investigations represents an emerging field marked by limited technical integration. To translate analytical outputs into operational real-time disease surveillance, future research must prioritize standardized spatially incorporated artificial intelligence frameworks. The review emphasizes the necessity of high-resolution geocoded data, spatially sensitive validation protocols, and emerging concepts such as GeoAI, digital twins, explainable artificial intelligence, and tailored implementation strategies.
