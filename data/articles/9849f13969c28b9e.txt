TITLE: Character.ai says blocking its $10m bidder a day before bids protects no one
OUTLET: PPC Land

Character Technologies, the bankrupt company behind Character.ai, today asked a Delaware bankruptcy judge to overrule objections to Smirks and Pangs Acquisition, the newly formed buyer it has lined up to pay $10 million for the Character.ai app, its AI models and its users' data, arguing that no rule of bankruptcy law requires it to publish the name of the investor financing the bid. In a nine-page reply and status report filed today as Docket No. 220 in case 26-11603 (BLS), the company says denying the designation "does not protect any party" and would instead "remove a $10,000,000 price floor" one business day before the bid deadline. The same filing sets out a proposed calendar of depositions, witness lists and exhibit exchanges running to December 5, around an auction that stays on December 1.
In Short
Character.ai wants Smirks and Pangs to remain its $10 million opening bidder even though the investor behind the buyer, a New York venture capital fund, has been named only in private to the US Trustee, the creditors' committee and state attorneys general. The US Trustee and Texas object, and the buyer would take the app, the AI models, users' chats and the platform's advertising accounts and data if no one outbids it. The hearing, scheduled for 3 p.m. Eastern today, decides whether bidding closes on November 24 with that $10 million floor in place and whether the public record will show who is paying for the data.
What the company told the court
The reply is short and its argument narrow. "The Objections raise issues of form over substance on one narrow issue, namely whether the sponsor of a bidder must be publicly disclosed," the company writes. It is filed by Character Technologies as debtor in possession, through Wilson Sonsini Goodrich & Rosati, and answers two objections: one from the US Trustee, Andrew R. Vara, and one from the State of Texas.
The disclosure requirement the objectors rely on is in the court's own bidding procedures order, entered on November 4 as Docket No. 106. That order let the company name additional stalking horse bidders for assets outside Disney's license, and required a newly formed buyer's parent company or sponsor to be identified, as PPC Land reported when the judge signalled he would overrule 40 states' objections to Character.ai's Disney sale rules. The company proposed the form of that order, and the reply concedes as much:
"While the Debtor's form of bidding procedures order contemplated such information would be included in a public stalking horse supplement, such order was proposed without the benefit of knowing the positions and issues an additional stalking horse would raise."
Once the buyer raised its concern, the company says, it discussed the issue with the parties and filed its motion to keep the sponsor confidential or to amend the order. "Under these circumstances and where there are no Bankruptcy Rules, Local Rules, or Bankruptcy Code provisions requiring disclosure of a bidder's sponsor, the circumstances do not justify denial of the Additional Stalking Horse Supplement."
The reply takes aim at the core of the Trustee's case. The Trustee had argued that leaving out the sponsor's identity prevents parties from evaluating the transaction, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai's $10m bidder faced objection over its unnamed backer. The company calls that "flatly incorrect." In its account, anyone who needed the name to decide whether to object "would have (i) objected to the Additional Stalking Horse Supplement or (ii) reached out to the Debtor to ask (any parties who asked were provided the information on a confidential basis)."
One detail in that passage does not match the source it answers. The reply quotes the Trustee as saying the omission prevents parties from deciding "if no objection is appropriate." The Trustee's objection, as PPC Land quoted it on November 20, speaks of deciding whether an objection is appropriate; the word "no" is not in that sentence.
Then comes the consequence the company wants the judge to weigh. "Denying the Debtor's ability to designate the Additional Stalking Horse Purchaser does not protect any party," the reply says. "Instead, it would remove a $10,000,000 price floor for the assets contemplated by the Additional Stalking Horse Agreement one business day before the Bid Deadline, disrupt a designation process that this Court expressly authorized in the Bidding Procedures Order, and significantly impede the Debtor's ability to maximize value for its stakeholders."
The relief requested is the same as before: overrule the objections and let the designation stand.
Who has the sponsor's name
The reply adds detail on how far the name has already travelled. According to the filing, the company consulted the creditors' committee before filing the November 14 designation and disclosed the sponsor's identity to it at that time. After the filing, lawyers for the company and for Smirks and Pangs held calls with the US Trustee and with representatives of the states, "where counsel for the Additional Stalking Horse Purchaser shared the identity of the sponsor and other key information related to the entity."
Texas is the change. When the company first asked for confidentiality on November 19, its proposed list of recipients covered the court, the committee, the US Trustee and the states' coalition, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai asked the court to keep its $10m buyer's backer secret. Texas was not on it. The reply now names "counsel to Texas" among the parties who will get the identity. The consumer privacy ombudsman, Lucy L. Thomson, is not mentioned anywhere in the filing.
The description of the sponsor is unchanged: "a New York venture capital fund that invests in technology and software companies." So is the footnote on the buyer's name, which "is a nod to the platform's user community" and refers to "the words 'smirk' and 'pang,' which both appeared frequently in responses generated by the Debtor's AI language models, a familiar pattern with AI language models, and are widely recognized among the platform's users."
Texas, the coalition and a disputed deadline
Texas filed its objection on November 20, as Docket No. 216. The reply calls it late. According to a footnote, it "was filed after the extended deadline to object to the Additional Stalking Horse Supplement," and the company is "unsure" whether Texas means to object to the designation itself or only to the substance of the sale. To the extent the court considers it, the company asks that its arguments apply to Texas as well.
The Texas filing tells a different story about time. It says the company had extended Texas's deadline by agreement to 11:59 p.m. Eastern on November 20, and the court's docket records it at 6:03 p.m. that day, as PPC Land reported when Texas said Character.ai's $10m sale can't move minors' data without parents. The reply does not say which extended deadline it has in mind. The Trustee's own objection had listed a separate 9 p.m. deadline on November 19.
A larger objection goes unmentioned. Late on November 20, a coalition of 42 states and the District of Columbia filed Docket No. 219, asking the court to keep users' chats and chat-trained models out of the sale and to decline the designation and its $350,000 termination payment on the current record, as covered when 42 states and DC asked the court to keep chats out of Character.ai's $10m sale. That filing also said the deadline to object to the designation had passed. The reply does not cite it. Instead it says that, aside from the Trustee and Texas, "the key parties in interest in this case (i.e., the Committee and the Multistate Group), have not taken issue with the Additional Stalking Horse Supplement nor the Debtor's business judgment in withholding certain confidential information from the public."
Two names for the states
The reply also renames the states. In the confidentiality motion, the company had called them the "NAAG Client States." After that filing, according to the reply, "the Debtor was contacted by a state listed in the following, clarifying that not all such states are 'NAAG Client States.'" The company now uses two labels, each defined in a footnote.
Neither list includes Florida, Missouri or Texas. The 42-state coalition behind Docket No. 219 is larger than both.
Contracts and the one counterparty objection
The Trustee's objection had a second strand: under the bidding procedures order, contract counterparties must raise any objection to a stalking horse's adequate assurance of future performance by the contract objection deadline, earlier than for other winning bidders. The company answers that this "is a sale hearing objection, and not an issue with the Stalking Horse Supplement."
It also says the concern is theoretical. "To date, the Debtor has only received one such objection, and it did not relate to the lack of disclosure related to the financial sponsor's identity." That objection is a limited objection and reservation of rights filed by DigitalOcean, LLC on November 18 (Docket No. 193), which the company "anticipates resolving" consensually. DigitalOcean holds the largest listed cure amount among the 55 contracts the buyer would take, $2,646,136, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai users' chats faced a $10m sale to Smirks and Pangs. No other counterparty served with the assumption notice (Docket No. 119), its supplement (Docket No. 178) or the designation has raised an issue, "either formally or informally," according to the reply.
The sale hearing status report
The second half of the filing is a status report on preparations for the hearing on the sales, and it shows how contested the process has become.
On November 9, the creditors' committee served its first set of document requests. After a series of meet and confers, the two sides agreed on a scope of production, "with both parties reserving all rights." The company says it has completed that production: "To date, the Debtor has made six productions totaling 6,238 pages and has also provided the Committee with access to various data rooms relevant to the Sales and Hearing."
Depositions followed. The committee filed six notices on November 18, and the NAAG Client States filed matching notices on November 19 and 20, naming the same six witnesses.
Roles are from earlier filings. Sontchi is a former Delaware bankruptcy judge who joined the board as an independent director on October 1, and Dunayer filed the declaration supporting the sale process, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai drew one unusable offer from 28 potential buyers. The states' corporate deposition had been noticed for November 30, with topics that include whether the models going to Smirks and Pangs can reveal users' personal information, as covered when Character.ai faced 35 states' questions under oath on users' data. The proposed protocol moves it to December 3.
The company says it is still negotiating "the appropriate topics for examination under the 30(b)(6) notices, the appropriate deponents in this matter, and an appropriate deposition schedule," and that it "anticipates reaching a global resolution on these issues in the near term." Marini, noticed by both groups, has no slot in the table.
The proposed protocol
The company proposed the following dates, which "appear acceptable to the parties, subject to certain caveats between the parties that remain under discussion." All times are Eastern, as listed in the filing.
Several entries differ from the calendar in the bidding procedures order, as PPC Land reported it on November 4. The company's sale replies were due on November 30 at 4 p.m.; the protocol puts them at 11:59 p.m. on December 2. The general deadline for objections to the sales passed on November 18; the committee, appointed by the US Trustee on November 4, now has until noon on November 25.
