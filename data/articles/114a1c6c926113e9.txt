Nvidia prepares for congressional hearings as tech industry braces for impact
The soap opera-like drama surrounding Nvidia NVDA and its CEO Jensen Huang for the past three weeks takes center stage again next week when he testifies in congressional hearings, which could certainly lead to further volatility in the tech and AI trade.
Huang resigned on November 11 amid scrutiny over reports of extramarital relationships with multiple women, including one involving a former Megaspeed executive suspected by U.S. authorities of links to Chinese intelligence. Subsequent reports have detailed how Megaspeed, a Singapore-based data center company, allegedly delivered some of Nvidia's advanced GPUs into China, in violation of U.S. export laws.
Nvidia CFO Colette Kress took over for a brief period as interim CEO. However, a vast collective of current and former Nvidia employees, along with high-profile executives from across the tech world petitioned for his return. U.S. President Donald Trump also got involved.
"They did ask for assurance on the national security threat," Trump said. "And we gave them assurance, at least with everything we know, and we know a lot."
On Monday, Nov. 23, Nvidia announced that it had reinstated Huang, who cofounded the company more than three decades ago, as CEO, President, and a member of its board of directors, effective immediately. The move prompted shares to surge 31.6%, adding more than $1T in market cap at the close of trading, more than doubling the largest single-day gain in stock market history. It uplifted the entire sector, as evidenced by the tech-heavy Nasdaq COMP jumping 6.5% by the closing bell.
Huang is slated to appear before the House Foreign Affairs, Intelligence and China Select committees for a closed-door hearing on December 1, followed up by a public hearing with the Senate Banking Committee on December 3. Nvidia's general counsel Tim Teter is scheduled to testify before the same House committees on December 2.
"The negative ripple effect from Jensen/Nvidia is around tightening export controls to China, closing any loopholes, and more scrutiny around Big Tech and the AI Revolution," said analyst Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives in an investor report. "The problem is that the vast majority of raw materials that make Nvidia and overall GPU chips come from China, which speaks to a circular situation between the US and China and Jensen and Nvidia right in the middle of the storm."
Huang has hired attorney Joshua A. Levy, who is highly experienced in congressional investigations, to help him prepare for the interrogations.
"We continue to expect a rollercoaster ride ahead, but our thesis in the AI Revolution buildout and the winners (including Nvidia) have not changed," Ives added. "Next week Jensen carries the weight of the entire tech sector and AI Revolution thesis when he marches into DC, as the politicians are eagerly awaiting these sessions with all the world watching."
To put financials into perspective, "China and the Asia loophole remain a containable 15% to 20% of revenue at most in our view; the 4Q27 guide already carries none of it, and the trajectory of the AI Revolution the next few years does not hinge on it," Ives noted.
"Big Tech capex in the $1.5 trillion range next year, a cloud industry backlog of $2.5 trillion per the 3Q27 call, 160 GW of demand through 2029 against ~80 GW to 100 GW of planned capacity, and a product cycle with Vera Rubin shipping nearly $20 billion in its first production quarter, Rubin Ultra on track for next year, and Feynman in 2028, with management holding a 72% to 73% gross margin for FY28 through the memory cost cycle," he said.
Still, additional investigations also loom, including one in California, where Attorney General Rob Bonta announced a probe into the allegations that Nvidia's advanced semiconductor chips found their way to China, breaching U.S. restrictions on chip exports.
