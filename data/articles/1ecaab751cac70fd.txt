TITLE: Character.ai drops request to hide who backs its $10m bidder
OUTLET: PPC Land

Character Technologies, the bankrupt company behind Character.ai, on November 24 dropped its request to keep secret who finances Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, the $10 million bidder for the Character.ai app, its AI models and its users' chats. The backer, disclosed in a court filing earlier in the day, is Octave Fund I LLC. At a 13-minute video hearing in the afternoon, Judge Brendan L. Shannon of the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware said he was ready to approve Smirks and Pangs as the opening bidder in the December 1 auction. He then declined to settle a new fight over which executives' files the company must hand to its creditors.
In Short
The company behind Character.ai on November 24 gave up its effort to keep secret who is paying for the $10 million bid on its app, AI models and users' chats, and named the backer as an investment fund called Octave Fund I LLC. That ended the objections of the government's bankruptcy watchdog and the court's privacy ombudsman, who had argued that users need to know who could end up with their conversations, and the judge said he would approve the bidder's protected place in the auction. A separate fight has just started: the creditors say the only company executive whose files were searched for them is a former chief executive who now works for Disney, and the judge left both sides to work it out, with a possible video hearing on Monday, November 30.
A name, after one night
The hearing picked up where the previous afternoon had stopped. On November 23, Judge Shannon told the parties he was struggling with the company's motion to seal the identity of the investor behind Smirks and Pangs, the stalking horse whose $10 million offer sets the minimum price rivals must beat. He gave the lawyers 10 minutes to confer and, when they could not agree, continued the matter to 3 p.m. Eastern on November 24, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai's banker won court approval for its $1.75m sale fee. The motion, filed on November 19, had described that investor only as a New York venture capital fund that invests in technology and software companies, as covered when Character.ai asked the court to keep its $10m buyer's backer secret.
By the time the judge opened the November 24 session, the dispute was over. He said he had received a notice withdrawing the confidentiality motion and a notice disclosing the stalking horse's backer. Both reached the docket around midday, about three hours before the hearing. The withdrawal, Docket No. 235, signed by Heather P. Lambert of Wilson Sonsini Goodrich & Rosati, runs to one operative sentence: "the Debtor hereby withdraws the Motion without prejudice." Without prejudice means the company gave up the request for now but did not give up the right to make it again.
Erin R. Fay, the company's lead lawyer at the same firm, did not hide her relief. "We made more progress overnight than we did in the 10 minutes that you gave us yesterday," she said.
The disclosure, according to Fay, was "filed this afternoon or this morning at docket 234, which discloses that the stalking horse, Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC's sponsor is Octave Fund I LLC, which is a Delaware limited liability corporation." Smirks and Pangs is itself a new company: it was formed in Delaware on November 13, the day before Character.ai named it as a bidder, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai's $10m bidder faced objection over its unnamed backer.
Fay attached one more assurance, which the US Trustee had requested. The company, she said, was prepared to confirm "that the stalking horse, Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, has no meaningful connection to either the debtor, Google, or Disney." A lawyer for Smirks and Pangs was on the call and could confirm it "if necessary," she added. Nobody asked. Both names carry weight in this case. Disney is the other stalking horse, offering $20 million for a license to Character.ai's technology while hiring most of its staff, and Google struck a $2.7 billion deal with the company in 2024.
Why the watchdogs stood down
Linda J. Casey, a trial attorney in the office of US Trustee Andrew R. Vara, the Justice Department official who oversees bankruptcy cases in Delaware, had led the opposition. She objected in writing on November 19 and argued against secrecy at length the next day. A name was enough to settle it. "Yes, for the purposes of the additional stalking horse designation, the disclosure of the fund behind the entity is sufficient for the U.S. Trustee," she said.
Her acceptance came with limits attached. "Of course, as Ms. Fay indicated, all parties' rights for any appropriate discovery in the case regarding anybody backing the fund, the CPO's rights to request additional notice to consumers in connection with the sale, are all reserved, but for the purpose today, we are resolved." CPO is the court's shorthand for the consumer privacy ombudsman.
The distinction matters. What was disclosed is the name of a fund, not the people or institutions that put money into it. On November 23, Casey had said the Trustee would not have objected at all if the original notice had named the sponsor or the equity holders. The November 24 disclosure answered the first half of that. The reservation keeps the second half, who stands behind the fund, available for the contested sale hearing.
Lucy L. Thomson, the consumer privacy ombudsman appointed on November 18 to review how users' personal data would be handled in any sale, had been the most forceful voice for disclosure a day earlier. She told the court then that the backer's identity and the people running the buyer had to be made public, because users needed that information to decide whether to object. Her appointment was covered when Character.ai's planned data sale got a $650-an-hour privacy ombudsman.
On November 24 she said she agreed with Casey's summary, and pointed to what the name now makes possible. "In fact, there's quite a bit of information that an interested party could find by Googling the Octave Fund I, particularly an SEC filing that gives people plenty of information to delve more deeply into the potential bidder if they want to." She did not say which filing she meant.
The designation, cleared
With the confidentiality motion gone, the judge returned to the question he had been ready to answer the day before. At that hearing, he said, the court had regarded the stalking horse protections and the designation "as non-controversial," while everyone's attention had gone to the request for secrecy. "That motion has now been withdrawn and resolved. So based upon the record before me, I would be prepared to enter an appropriate form of order that the debtors can upload approving the designation of the stalking horse." He asked whether anyone else wished to be heard. No one did. Fay said the company would submit an order after checking it with some of the parties.
The designation gives Smirks and Pangs two things. Its $10 million offer becomes the floor for the platform package, and if another bidder wins at the auction, it receives a $350,000 termination payment. The package covers the Character.ai app, all of its AI models, users' accounts and chats, and the platform's advertising and analytics data, ad monetization assets and ad and app store accounts, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai users' chats faced a $10m sale to Smirks and Pangs. The signed purchase agreement depends on moving those chats without asking users for fresh consent, as covered when Character.ai's $10m sale hinged on moving chats without further consent.
Bids were due at noon on November 24, three hours before the hearing. Nobody said in court whether any rival bids arrived.
A new dispute over whose files were searched
Julia M. Winters of McDermott Will & Schulte, counsel to the official committee of unsecured creditors, the group appointed to represent the company's ordinary creditors, had a different matter to raise. On November 23, she reminded the judge, she had agreed with Fay that discovery was going productively. "But last night we learned for the first time that the debtors' only company custodian of documents that they produced to us is Mr. Karandeep Anand, who is the former CEO, who is now an employee of Disney, and whom the debtors acknowledge is an insider as defined in the Bankruptcy Code."
Two terms in that sentence carry the argument. In eDiscovery, the collection and review of electronic records for a legal dispute, a custodian is a person whose email, chats and files are gathered and searched. And under the Bankruptcy Code, an insider of a company includes people such as its directors, officers and those in control of it. Anand ran Character.ai before joining Disney as its chief technology officer, as PPC Land reported when Disney paid $20m for Character.ai's IP and $180m for its staff. That hiring of staff is an acqui-hire, and it sits outside the auction.
"And this was surprising to us," Winters went on, "because the debtors' bid procedures motion represented that no insiders had meaningful input in substantial matters related to this Chapter 11 case or the debtors' sale process, including the proposed transaction with Disney." The company's October sale filings made a similar point in slightly different words. As PPC Land reported when Disney budgeted $180m for Character.ai staff and $20m for the bankrupt company, the motion said that none of the company's former insiders had had meaningful input in substantial matters related to the Chapter 11 case, and that committees made up solely of independents led the deal.
"So we didn't understand how the only company custodian of relevant responsive documents to our requests about the sale was the insider whom the debtors represented had no involvement in the sale," Winters said.
What the committee asked for
According to Winters, the committee sent its request earlier on November 24. It wants documents from the files of five people:
- Colin M. Adams, the chief executive, who has filed declarations in the case.
- Adam Dunayer of Houlihan Lokey, the banker whose declaration describes the sale process.
- The two members of the board's special committee who, according to the company, led the deal and recommended that the board approve the Disney transaction.
- The company's general counsel, "who is the signatory of the relevant pre-petition agreements with the debtor."
The urgency, she said, came from the calendar. "Mr. Dunayer's deposition is currently scheduled for tomorrow, and it is the committee's position that we can't possibly proceed with a deposition before we have his relevant custodial documents; otherwise we cannot test the various assertions of fact that he makes in his declaration." The company's proposed schedule put that deposition at 2 p.m. on November 25, by Zoom, as covered when Character.ai said blocking its $10m bidder a day before bids protects no one. The committee asked the judge to order the documents produced and to push any deposition back to 48 hours after it receives them.
The company's answer
Fay objected to the timing before she objected to the substance. The company, she said, had received the committee's points only about three hours before the hearing, and the judge would have no context. Then she gave the company's account. "So as to where we are today, the debtors' document production to the committee is substantially complete. We have produced or provided access to over 20,000 pages of materials, including opening several data rooms over an expedited two-week discovery sprint."
The page count is new. The company's written status report, filed on November 23, counted six productions totaling 6,238 pages, plus access to data rooms. Fay's figure on November 24 includes materials in those data rooms.
