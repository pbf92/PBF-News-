Study Links ICE Surge to Fewer Health Visits
Research finds Spanish-speaking patients in the Minneapolis-Saint Paul area sought care less often during a federal immigration enforcement operation.
Updated on Nov. 23, 2026 in Immigration

A JAMA Network Open analysis found that Spanish-speaking patients in the Minneapolis-Saint Paul metro area had fewer emergency, inpatient and outpatient visits during Operation Metro Surge, which deployed about 2,000 ICE agents from December 2025 through February 2026. The study, reported by CIDRAP, compared patient healthcare visits before and after the influx of agents.
Why it matters
The findings suggest enforcement activity may keep residents from routine and emergency medical care, with possible consequences for untreated conditions in Minnesota's immigrant communities. Researchers say many immigrants reported fear of leaving their homes during the operation.
The study compared visit counts before and after the deployment against a baseline of more than 1.3 million healthcare interactions for Spanish-speaking adults. Telehealth visits rose by 717 during the operation, but the increase only minimally offset the drop in in-person care.
The players
U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE)
ICE is the federal agency responsible for immigration enforcement and removal operations within the United States.
JAMA Network Open
JAMA Network Open is an open-access, peer-reviewed medical journal published by the American Medical Association.
The details
Researchers compared the number of patient healthcare visits before and after about 2,000 ICE agents arrived in the Minneapolis-Saint Paul metro area. Greater telehealth use minimally offset fewer in-person encounters, and the reduced visit rates were more pronounced in areas and periods with higher operation activity.
Timeline
- Prior to December 2025: baseline healthcare interactions were counted for Spanish-speaking adults.
- December 2025 through February 2026: about 2,000 ICE agents were deployed during Operation Metro Surge.
- November 23, 2026: the analysis was published in JAMA Network Open.
Big Picture
The findings extend a documented pattern in which immigrant families reduce healthcare use during enforcement surges, now measured in visit counts across a major Minnesota metro area. The results add quantitative evidence to a debate over how immigration policy affects public health access in the state.
Spanish-speaking Minnesotans may face delayed or skipped care, including emergency visits, during periods of heavy enforcement activity. Telehealth can partly fill gaps, but the study found it did not replace most in-person visits.
The takeaway
Health access can shift quickly when communities fear enforcement, so residents who need care should consider contacting clinics early and asking about telehealth or language services. Public health planners in Minnesota may need to track utilization alongside enforcement activity.
