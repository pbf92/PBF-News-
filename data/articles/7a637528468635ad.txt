TITLE: Buyers bid more on app ads with a device ID in 3 of 4 regions, Blasto finds
OUTLET: PPC Land

Advertising buyers placed more bids on in-app ad requests that carried a device identifier than on requests without one in three of the four regions where Blasto operates, according to The State of In-App Programmatic Advertising 2026, a report built on nine months of the company's own bid data.
The same report sets a ceiling on how often an app ad arrives with everything a buyer looks for. In Blasto's EU traffic, no more than 69% of requests carry all four of the signals Blasto counts: proof of who may sell the ad, a device identifier, a readable consent record and a measurement tag. Blasto chief executive Oleksii Sakhno summarized the findings in a guest member blog that IAB Europe published on its website on November 25, 2026. The 18-page report itself carries no publication date.
In Short
Blasto, a company that runs both ad-buying and ad-selling software, studied nine months of app ad requests and found that in Europe at most 69% arrive with all four pieces of information buyers use to judge an ad slot. Missing device IDs are the biggest gap in Europe, and buyers placed more bids on requests that carried a device ID or a measurement tag in three of four regions. Blasto argues that part of the Android gap, and the 5.7% of European requests whose consent records cannot be read, come from technical setup rather than user choice, which would mean they can be fixed without asking users anything new.
An ad slot with no page to look at
On a website, a buyer's system can at least check the page an ad will sit on. Inside a mobile app there is nothing comparable. The buyer's software receives a short machine-readable message, the bid request, and has a fraction of a second to decide whether the ad slot it describes is worth money.
Blasto's report reduces that decision to four questions: who is allowed to sell the slot, whether the phone can be recognized, what the user agreed to, and whether an independent company can check what happens once the ad is shown. The first answer comes from app-ads.txt, a public text file on the app developer's website listing the ad companies permitted to sell its ad space. The second comes from the phone's mobile advertising ID, called IDFA on Apple devices and GAID on Android. The third is the consent record that a European pop-up produces under IAB Europe's Transparency and Consent Framework (TCF). The fourth is the Open Measurement software kit (OM SDK), a piece of code from IAB Tech Lab that lets an outside verification company confirm whether an ad was actually on screen.
"In an app, there is no page for the buyer to see. A DSP judges an impression from the bid request and the app-ads.txt file published for the app," according to Sakhno, quoted in the report. A DSP, or demand-side platform, is the software advertisers and agencies use to bid.
According to Blasto, three of the four answers come from industry standards maintained by IAB Tech Lab and IAB Europe, the fourth comes from an identifier issued by Apple or Google, and "all four are free to implement."
The report gives the combination a name. In its own words: "A request backed by every signal that applies in its region is what we call the Fully-Signaled Impression: all four in the EU, and three in the US and APAC, where TCF is not counted." The IAB Europe version of the text uses the British spelling, Fully-Signalled.
How Blasto built the numbers
According to the report's methodology page, the figures come from six steps.
- Period and scope. The data covers the first three quarters of 2026 and in-app traffic only.
- Bid stream. Blasto analyzed in-app bid requests and the responses to them across four of its own traffic regions: EU, US East, US West and APAC.
- Supply crawl. The company downloaded the app-ads.txt files of 3,590 app bundles that entered its bid stream in the period. 75.5% were Google Play apps and 24.5% App Store apps. Seller domains were reduced to their registrable domain and merged into parent companies, and malformed lines were thrown out. Seller counts are per app; the RESELLER and DIRECT figures are shares of all lines.
- One signal at a time. Each of the four signals was measured on its own, as the share of requests that carried it.
- The ceiling. The maximum fully-signaled share is the smallest of those single-signal shares in each region, a statistical limit the report calls the "Fréchet upper bound." For the supply signal, Blasto used the 98% valid app-ads.txt share it measured in the bid stream.
- Shares only. All results are published as percentages or ranges. No request counts, spend figures or prices appear in the report.
That fifth step matters for reading the headline number. Blasto did not count how many requests actually carried all four signals at once. The 69% figure for Europe is the most that could be complete, because no combined share can be higher than the share of its weakest ingredient.
Supply: more than 200 sellers per app
Publishing an app-ads.txt file is close to standard practice. According to the report, 96% of the apps Blasto crawled publish one. The problem, as Blasto frames it, is length.
Each line in the file names an ad company, an account and a relationship. DIRECT means the account belongs to the app developer itself. RESELLER means it belongs to another company that is allowed to resell the app's ad space. At the median app in the crawl, more than 200 distinct companies are authorized, and nearly four lines in five are resellers.
"A buyer reading these files sees hundreds of permitted paths to the same impression, with little to show which of them is efficient," according to the report. A RESELLER line also says nothing about how long the chain behind it is. That information sits only in the request's SupplyChain object, a field that lists each company the request passed through on its way to the buyer.
The report cites Pixalate, a fraud-measurement company, which said that 35% of mobile app traffic carrying a SupplyChain object failed seller verification in the second quarter of 2025. According to Blasto, long seller lists give buyers more paths to check, "which can make that verification harder."
The pattern is familiar from web publishing. DataBeat's September 2026 Sellers Report, which tracks the ads.txt files of the top 50,000 US publishers, showed resellers adding 44,608 net lines in a month against 10,334 for direct sellers. In June, the same firm measured 46% domain duplication at top-tier SSPs, a sign of the same ad slot being offered to buyers more than once. And the chain itself is getting longer on paper: under the SupplyChain object version 1.1 that IAB Tech Lab published on November 18, the chain also covers companies that only route or rewrite a request without being paid.
Identity: an Apple gap and a European Android gap
Up to 60% of iOS requests carry an IDFA, depending on region, according to the report. The cause is Apple's App Tracking Transparency rule: an app can pass on the identifier only if it showed Apple's permission prompt and the user said yes. Apple announced the requirement in June 2020 for iOS 14.
Source: Blasto bid stream, first three quarters of 2026.
"Up to 60%" hides a wide spread. A PPC Land calculation from the rounded figures in that table, which Blasto does not publish itself, suggests that roughly 59% of iOS requests in US East carry an IDFA, the region that matches the report's upper figure. In the EU and US West the figure works out to roughly a third, and in APAC to about 46%. The same calculation implies that Android accounts for about 72% of Blasto's EU requests. These are estimates, sensitive to rounding.
According to the report, iOS accounts for 60% of the missing device IDs in the EU, "and engineering cannot close that part." Android is different. Outside the EU, 4% to 8% of Android requests arrive without a GAID; in the EU the figure is 17.3%.
Why would European Android phones lose their identifiers so much more often? Users rarely delete the advertising ID themselves, Blasto argues, so the gap must have other causes. The report lists three: software kits that drop the identifier when a user refuses one of the TCF consent purposes, apps that fall under Google Play's family policy, and devices without Google Play services. "Some of that is correct behavior. Some of it may be an identifier removed where the user's choices allowed it to stay, and that difference is worth auditing," according to the report.
Google itself blanks the identifier in one case. In 2021, the company said that when an Android user opts out of personalized ads, apps receive a string of zeros instead of the ID.
On iOS, the European picture is about to change for reasons outside the report. Germany's competition authority announced on August 17 that Apple's commitments on the tracking prompt were binding, and the commitments let apps ask users again even if they already said no. Apple separately told developers that apps distributed in Germany, France, Italy, Poland and Romania will get only a redesigned prompt from iOS 27.2. Blasto's data stops at the end of September, so it cannot show what either change does to opt-in rates.
Consent: present, but unreadable
Blasto calls the consent figures "the encouraging part" for a European audience. According to the report, 93.2% of requests in its EU traffic carry a valid TCF consent string, and only 1.1% carry none.
Source: Blasto bid stream, first three quarters of 2026.
The figure Blasto singles out is the middle one. In 5.7% of EU requests, a TC string, the coded record of what the user agreed to, was present but could not be used, "because it is malformed or cannot be decoded." The user made a choice; the record of it did not reach the buyer intact.
According to the report, the fault can sit in the setup of the consent management platform that runs the pop-up, in a software kit inside the app, or with a partner further along the chain. "Where the user's choice was captured correctly, fixing that problem needs no new prompt and no new user decision. It is an engineering task shared by publishers, SDK providers and SSPs," the report states. An SSP, or supply-side platform, is the software that sells ad space on the publisher's behalf.
Broken consent strings have become a recurring theme in Europe this year. Google made version 2.3 of the framework mandatory from March 1, 2026, with requests that fail its checks falling back to limited ads or being dropped. In October, AdSense moved its consent-string error reporting into the Policy center. IAB Europe's own compliance report showed that enforcement procedures against vendors rose 118.2% in 2025, from 269 to 587.
Measurement: the tag that lets outsiders check
Between 67% and 82% of requests declare an Open Measurement partner, depending on region, according to the report. The rest declare none, so the buyer has no sign that an independent firm can check viewability, the test of whether an ad was actually on screen long enough to count. Blasto says that gap "matters most for video and rewarded formats," ads that users choose to watch in exchange for an in-app reward.
Source: Blasto bid stream, first three quarters of 2026.
The kit is not new. IAB Tech Lab released it in April 2018, and by December of that year it reached 2 billion devices. Work on it continues: in October, IAB Tech Lab described an Android version that cut the time to start a measurement session for native ads from 268.51 milliseconds to 11.07 milliseconds on a low-end test phone.
The ceiling, region by region
Put the four layers together and each region's ceiling is set by its scarcest signal. In the EU and US West, that is the device ID. In US East and APAC, it is the measurement tag.
TCF is counted only in the EU. The maximum share and the scarcest signal come from the report.
