TITLE: Heineken campaign returned £2.92 per £1 invested, Mobsta says
OUTLET: PPC Land

Mobsta today introduced Adaptive Signal Weighting, a feature inside its proprietary Intelligence Engine that ranks real-world behavioral signals according to what an advertising campaign is meant to achieve. The company, which describes itself as a "geo-behavioural media and data intelligence technology company," said the capability is already running in client work, including a Heineken campaign that delivered £2.92 for every £1 invested. Mobsta put that result 40% above benchmark. It did not say which benchmark.
In Short
Mobsta, a data and media company, has added a feature that decides how much each piece of behavioral data, such as car ownership or shopping habits, should count when it builds an advertising audience. The company says it shows buyers which data was given priority and why, at a time when automated ad platforms make many of those choices out of sight. Its proof point is a Heineken campaign said to return £2.92 per £1 spent, but the release gives no benchmark, dates, market or measurement method behind the "40% above benchmark" claim.
What Mobsta introduced
According to Mobsta, Adaptive Signal Weighting is "available across the Engine's audience intelligence features" and lets brands and agencies "build more precise audiences by prioritising real-world behavioural signals according to their campaign objectives." The release, datelined London and titled "Mobsta expands Intelligence Engine with new Adaptive Signal Weighting capability," presents it as an addition to an existing product rather than a standalone tool. No pricing, client count or launch market was given.
The premise is simple enough. The release positions the feature against conventional audience segmentation, where a data point typically works as a filter: a household owns a car or it does not, a shopper visited a store or did not. Mobsta's argument is that the same set of signals should count for different amounts depending on the job. Rather than treating every indicator equally, according to the company, the system weights them by campaign objective to find "the audiences and geographical areas where opportunities for impact are likely to be strongest."
The signals named in the release are vehicle ownership, household characteristics, shopping habits, spending and visitation behavior. Mobsta says the engine analyzes and combines "billions" of such signals. The objectives the company associates with the weighting are awareness, store or dealership visits, and sales.
What the release sells hardest, though, is visibility. According to Mobsta, the feature gives advertisers "a clearer view of which behavioural signals have been prioritised, how strongly they have been weighted and why they matter to their campaign objectives." That is a pitch aimed less at the size of a data set than at the opacity of the systems that use one.
How the process works, according to Mobsta
The release does not set out a formal workflow, but read together, its description covers five parts.
- Signal collection. The Intelligence Engine analyzes and combines real-world signals, including vehicle ownership, household characteristics, shopping habits, spending and visitation behavior.
- Weighting by objective. Instead of treating each indicator equally, the system assigns weights according to the campaign objective, so the same underlying data produces different audiences for awareness, visit or sales goals.
- AI analysis and human interpretation. According to Mobsta, AI handles the analysis of complex signal combinations at scale, while the company's planning, strategy and insights teams supply client context and interpret the findings to build what it calls "tailored audience strategies."
- Geographic mapping. The resulting audiences are mapped geographically to identify the areas where impact is expected to be strongest.
- Activation. Mobsta says the mapped audiences support "privacy-first media activation" across connected TV, digital audio, digital out-of-home, display and social.
Step three matters for anyone assessing the product. The release describes a service as much as a piece of software: the weights are interpreted, and the strategies developed, by Mobsta staff. The company's boilerplate makes the same point, saying its Mobsta Intelligence offering "combines human expertise, judgement and creativity with the unique analytical capabilities" of the Intelligence Engine. Whether clients can set or adjust weights themselves in a self-serve interface is not stated.
The geographic step places the product within geomarketing, where spatial data is tied to areas on a map to plan and target campaigns. Two of the activation channels, connected TV and digital out-of-home, are ones where targeting usually works at the level of a household or a screen location rather than a single person, which suits audiences drawn on a map.
The worked example
Mobsta illustrates the mechanism with a hypothetical car launch rather than the Heineken campaign. The release offers no equivalent breakdown of which signals were weighted for the brewer.
"The underlying data remains similar, but the importance of each signal changes according to the campaign objective," according to the release. The example shows the logic. It does not show the weights themselves, the scale used to express them, or how much a change in weighting alters the size of the resulting audience.
The Heineken figures
The case study rests on two numbers. Mobsta says its work with Heineken "delivered £2.92 in return for every £1 invested, performing 40% above benchmark," and that the result shows "how a more sophisticated control and understanding of audience behaviour can translate into measurable commercial outcomes."
But 40% above what? The two calculated rows show why the definition matters. Depending on which figure the 40% describes, the benchmark sits somewhere between roughly £2.09 and £2.37 per pound, and the release does not say which reading is intended. Nor does it say whether "return" means revenue, gross profit or modeled sales, which would make the figure closer to a ROAS or to a return on investment.
Heineken made no statement in the release. Its role is described only by Mobsta, and only in passing: David Graham, Mobsta's chief executive, referred to the brewer as a client "we've been working with for years." The pound sterling figures and the London dateline suggest a UK campaign, but the market is not stated.
The release does not name the benchmark or who set it, give the campaign's dates, market, budget, channels or Heineken brand, say how the return was measured or by whom, or separate the effect of Adaptive Signal Weighting from the rest of the campaign. It also names no data suppliers and does not explain what "privacy-first" means for the signals used.
What Graham said
Graham framed the launch as a response to a surplus of data rather than a shortage. "Advertising doesn't have a shortage of data. What it's often short of is an understanding of which signals actually matter, to what extent, and how they work together," he said. "Someone's age, income or search behaviour may point to them being a potential customer, but that oversimplifies who we are as people because people are far more complex. Our behaviours, circumstances and motivations are constantly changing, and no single data point can capture this."
He tied the product to the debate over automated buying. "That's why Adaptive Signal Weighting is so important to us and our clients. It allows us to look at much richer and more complex combinations of signals, interrogate their relative importance and provide control over those priorities to build audiences around what a brand is actually trying to achieve," Graham said. "That visibility is now needed more than ever, as algorithms take on more of a role in campaign delivery, often at the expense of transparency for planning and buying teams."
On the brewer, he said: "We're putting Adaptive Signal Weighting into practice across our campaigns. It's great to see examples - such as Heineken, who we've been working with for years - of how a more intelligent understanding and ownership of audiences can translate into real business results." His closing line: "Ultimately, it's not about having more data. It's about making smarter strategic use of the data we have."
Why it matters for marketers
The transparency argument
Mobsta names no platform, but its target is plain. The release says automated systems "can improve campaign performance" while leaving audience decisions "often difficult for buying teams to understand." That tension has run through PPC Land's coverage of the large platforms for more than a year. Meta's AI automation drew skepticism from advertisers despite performance claims in November 2025, when one incrementality test found Advantage+ produced only 17% of the conversions Meta's own attribution reported. In June 2026, REI confirmed that Meta had auto-enrolled it into an AI personalization tool that distorted a product photo, a case that showed how far automated defaults can reach into an advertiser's own creative.
Independent audience vendors have been pitching some version of the glass box for a while. In July, GumGum's Mindset Agent began turning an uploaded brief into a targeting segment in The Trade Desk, with The HEINEKEN Company among the early adopters named. The brewer has also appeared in measurement work before: in 2025, Heineken raised digital to more than 42% of its media budget from 30% after a cross-media study with AudienceProject and Meta. With the brewer's name attached to more than one vendor's work, no single case study can be read as Heineken's verdict on the market.
A crowded market for location signals
Location and visitation data, the core of Mobsta's "geo-behavioural" description, have become a standard input in programmatic buying this year. In June, The Trade Desk added Adsquare's real-world location data to Audience Unlimited. On July 23, Adsquare's location segments became available in Amazon DSP in six markets, including the UK. In September, Drako began bundling foot traffic reports at no extra charge, a measure often sold at about $0.50 CPM, while Drako's own chief executive called footfall attribution a partial signal.
What separates Mobsta's pitch from a segment marketplace is the weighting layer and the consulting wrapped around it. Whether that layer is worth paying for is a question the release leaves to the client case study, which is where the evidence is thinnest.
The regulatory backdrop has hardened too. In May, the US Federal Trade Commission closed its Kochava location data case with an order, signed May 4, 2026, that bars Kochava and Collective Data Solutions from selling sensitive location data without affirmative express consent for ten years. Mobsta describes its activation as "privacy-first," but the release does not describe consent, data sources or retention.
Vendor benchmarks and what they leave out
The 40% claim joins a run of vendor-published comparisons in recent weeks. On November 18, Nexxen said its ads beat DISQO's awareness benchmark by 305% without giving a campaign count. In October, Magnite said its ads topped Cint's ad recall lift benchmark by 136%, and The Trade Desk claimed 41% higher ROAS for retail data without a sample size. On November 9, Magnite said a retailer's ID-free TV ads reached homeowners 43% more efficiently without naming the efficiency metric. Nexxen and Magnite at least named the benchmark provider. Mobsta does not.
Return figures carry their own traps. A Funnel measurement guide showed how a channel with a 3.0 ROAS can return $0.40 on extra spend, because an average return says little about the marginal one. In 2025, practitioners warned that inflated ROAS figures mislead Meta advertisers, arguing that an account reporting a ROAS of 2 may be doing better than one reporting 10, depending on attribution settings.
