TITLE: Cerebral Protection Devices Fail to Reduce Stroke Risk in Aortic Valve Procedures
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation has become an established therapy for patients with severe aortic stenosis, yet periprocedural stroke remains a serious complication occurring in approximately 2.3 percent of patients despite continuous technological improvements. To mitigate this neurological risk, cerebral embolic protection devices were developed to capture or deflect embolic debris generated during valve deployment before particles reach the brain. However, the real-world clinical effectiveness of these devices has remained controversial throughout interventional cardiology. A comprehensive systematic review and network meta-analysis published in the Hellenic Journal of Cardiology by Theoni Theodoropoulou and colleagues evaluated the role of cerebral embolic protection and compared the available clinical devices. Their primary finding reveals that cerebral embolic protection devices during transcatheter aortic valve implantation do not produce a statistically significant reduction in periprocedural stroke up to thirty days. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated no significant protective impact on all-cause mortality, acute kidney injury, bleeding, or vascular complications compared to standard care.
The investigation followed explicit methodological standards established in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and conformed to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines alongside the network meta-analysis extension statement. The research protocol was pre-registered in advance on the international PROSPERO database under accession number CRD420261293818. Researchers conducted a systematic literature search across PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane databases up to March 30, 2026, targeting randomized controlled trials that evaluated cerebral embolic protection devices in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation. The designated primary outcome was periprocedural stroke occurring up to thirty days post-procedure. Secondary clinical outcomes incorporated all-cause mortality, total volume and number of cerebral ischemic lesions, bleeding events, vascular complications, and acute kidney injury. The investigators synthesized study outcomes using a random-effects frequentist network meta-analysis model. Treatment hierarchy ranking probabilities across the competing strategies were formally estimated through the calculation of p-scores, allowing objective comparisons among multiple procedural approaches.
The comprehensive literature identification process yielded an initial total of 4,434 records across the electronic search databases. After preliminary screening, 40 full-text articles were rigorously assessed against the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 10 randomized controlled trials encompassing 12,058 patients met all eligibility requirements and were incorporated into the network meta-analysis. Within this combined study population, 6,120 patients were randomized to receive a cerebral embolic protection device during their procedure, while 5,938 patients were assigned to the control cohorts comprising either standard unprotected valve replacement or an alternative protective device. This substantial statistical foundation represents the first network meta-analysis designed to directly compare different cerebral embolic protection systems against each other and against unprotected interventions in transcatheter aortic valve implantation. By pooling data across more than twelve thousand clinical participants, the authors constructed an unprecedented evidence base to clarify unresolved clinical questions regarding whether mechanical embolic protection truly influences patient outcomes.
The analytical core of the meta-analysis relied heavily on three large multicenter trials detailed in the study characteristics. The largest was the BHF PROTECTED-TAVI trial published by Kharbanda and colleagues in 2025, which enrolled 7,594 patients across 33 centers in the United Kingdom, comparing 3,795 Sentinel device recipients against 3,799 unprotected control patients over a three-day follow-up. The second major trial was the 2022 PROTECTED TAVR study conducted by Kapadia and coworkers, evaluating 3,000 patients across 51 centers in North America, Europe, and Australia, randomizing 1,501 patients to the Sentinel system and 1,499 to control with three-day clinical monitoring. Providing direct comparative evidence between active devices, the 2026 PROTECT H2H trial by Adam B. Greenbaum and co-investigators examined 522 patients across 20 centers in Brazil, Germany, and the United States, evaluating 261 patients receiving the Emboliner device directly against 261 patients receiving the Sentinel device across thirty days of follow-up.
Seven additional randomized trials with thirty days of follow-up completed the network dataset. Nazif and colleagues in the 2021 REFLECT II trial evaluated 214 patients across 18 American centers, comparing 157 TriGuard 3 recipients to 57 controls. Lansky and collaborators assessed TriGuard HDH in the 2021 REFLECT I trial among 204 American patients (141 device versus 63 control) and the 2015 DEFLECT III trial involving 85 patients across Europe and Israel (46 device versus 39 control). Kapadia and coworkers in the 2017 SENTINEL trial studied 244 patients across 17 American and 2 German centers (123 device versus 121 control). Van Mieghem and associates in the 2016 Dutch MISTRAL-C trial evaluated 65 patients (32 device versus 33 control). In Germany, Haussig and co-authors in the 2016 CLEAN-TAVI trial examined 100 patients (50 Claret Montage versus 50 control) at Leipzig Heart Center, while Daniel Wendt and colleagues in 2015 evaluated the EMBOL-X device among 30 German patients (14 device versus 16 control).
When analyzing the comparative performance metrics across all eligible trials, the frequentist network meta-analysis demonstrated that no evaluated device produced a statistically significant reduction in mortality, stroke, acute kidney injury, bleeding, or vascular complications. Treatment ranking probabilities determined by p-scores placed the Sentinel device at the highest rank among all assessed therapies for stroke, bleeding, and vascular complications. Nevertheless, this favorable mathematical ranking did not correspond to a statistically significant clinical benefit. In the network model, the Sentinel device was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in the risk of stroke, demonstrating a relative risk of 0.88 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 0.69 to 1.12. Similarly, Sentinel showed no significant impact on all-cause mortality, yielding a relative risk of 0.86 with a 95 percent confidence interval ranging from 0.53 to 1.39. Thus, despite leading the p-score hierarchy for stroke prevention, Sentinel failed to achieve verifiable clinical risk reduction.
While clinical stroke endpoints showed no overall reduction across the network, secondary analyses revealed distinct radiological effects for specific devices on surrogate imaging endpoints. The Claret Montage dual filter system, which positions dual filters into the brachiocephalic trunk and the left common carotid artery, demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in surrogate cerebral lesion markers. Network analysis showed that the Claret Montage system significantly reduced total cerebral lesion volume compared to control, achieving a mean difference of -370.40 cubic millimeters with a 95 percent confidence interval from -500.48 to -240.32. The device also significantly reduced the total number of cerebral lesions, with a mean difference of -4.30 and a 95 percent confidence interval from -8.31 to -0.29. However, although the CLEAN-TAVI trial demonstrated this reduction in the number and volume of new ischemic lesions detected by magnetic resonance imaging, no difference in overt clinical stroke events was observed between the treated and control cohorts.
The review also evaluated deflection technologies developed to bypass filter limitations, particularly the TriGUARD 3 system, which serves as a newer iteration of the TriGUARD HDH. The TriGUARD 3 device is designed as a self-stabilizing embolic deflection filter positioned across the aortic arch to cover all three major cerebral branches during valve implantation, diverting dislodged embolic debris safely away from the cerebral circulation. Although the REFLECT II trial confirmed the procedural safety of TriGUARD 3 during transcatheter aortic valve implantation, the trial failed to demonstrate a clinical benefit in its primary composite endpoint of all-cause mortality or stroke at thirty days. The researchers explained that despite the device’s self-stabilizing design and enlarged surface area, complete cerebral vessel coverage was achieved in only 59.3 percent of treated patients. This anatomical limitation helps explain the absence of clinical efficacy and reduced treatment effectiveness observed across the published trial data.
Several technical and anatomical limitations inherent to currently available devices provide crucial context for why clinical benefits remain elusive. The widely utilized Sentinel device protects only the brachiocephalic and left common carotid artery, leaving the left vertebral artery and the entire posterior cerebral circulation unprotected during valve replacement. Furthermore, Sentinel is manufactured in only a single filter size, which cannot optimally accommodate the diverse range of aortic arch anatomies found in clinical practice. The requirement for separate arterial access introduces additional procedural complexity while lengthening fluoroscopy exposure time for patients and operators. The authors highlighted that all of these interrelated factors contribute to a steep operator learning curve, which limits widespread institutional adoption and produces inconsistent clinical benefits across different medical centers and research studies. Consequently, incomplete anatomical protection and procedural demands continue to constrain the real-world performance of current cerebral embolic protection systems during transcatheter aortic valve replacement.
In addressing future directions, the authors examined procedural valve types and surrogate diagnostic markers of cerebral embolization. Self-expanding valves have been associated with a higher risk of periprocedural stroke during transcatheter aortic valve implantation, but current evidence does not demonstrate that cerebral embolic protection device effectiveness differs between self-expanding and balloon-expandable valves. Studies utilizing diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging and transcranial Doppler ultrasound demonstrate that transcatheter valve replacement frequently causes silent cerebral embolization without overt neurologic deficits, with Kahlert and colleagues reporting a substantial burden of microembolic signals detected by Doppler monitoring. While the REFLECT I trial showed no significant primary efficacy benefit, it emphasized incorporating neurologic and diffusion-weighted imaging outcomes into future trials. The meta-analysis concluded that cerebral embolic protection devices do not significantly reduce periprocedural stroke, noting that the relative efficacy of alternative devices remains uncertain due to limited data, and recommending that future trials focus on improved risk stratification and next-generation device engineering.
