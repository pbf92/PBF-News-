TITLE: When the Protocol Changes Mid-Trial, Who Protects the Result?
OUTLET: The Clinical Trial Vanguard

Three methodological decisions in the Modified Psychodynamic Psychotherapy for Schizophrenia (MPP-S) trial, published as a Lancet Psychiatry correspondence, have not received the scrutiny they deserve: a change in treatment duration, a shift in primary outcome, and a sample size recalculation. Each decision, taken alone, carries a plausible justification. Taken together, they represent a pattern that the psychiatric trials community has not yet named clearly, call it the Soft Protocol Drift.
The MPP-S trial, a prospective, assessor-blinded, parallel-group randomized controlled trial conducted in Germany, reports Hedges’ g effect sizes of 0.70 at 24 months and 0.93 at 36 months on the Mini International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health Rating for Limitations of Activities and Participation in Psychological Disorders (Mini-ICF-APP). Those are clinically meaningful numbers. But the credibility of any effect size depends entirely on whether the measurement framework was fixed before the data started arriving, and the correspondence raises legitimate doubts about exactly that.
The Drift Begins With Duration
The first signal of Soft Protocol Drift is a change in treatment duration. When a psychotherapy trial extends its follow-up window after enrollment has begun, two things happen simultaneously: the original power calculation becomes invalid, and the original primary timepoint loses its primacy. If the 24-month Mini-ICF-APP score was the pre-specified primary endpoint and the trial subsequently extended to 36 months, the natural question is what motivated that extension.
This matters more in psychiatry than in almost any other therapeutic area, because psychotherapy effects are notoriously sensitive to observation length. A treatment that produces modest gains at 24 months can appear transformative at 36 months simply because the control arm degrades, especially when that control arm is Treatment as Usual (TAU). The MPP-S trial’s TAU arm deserves the same scrutiny its intervention arm receives.
Duration extensions also create an interpretive problem for regulators and health technology assessors who must anchor a reimbursement decision to a fixed timepoint. When the primary result migrates from month 24 to month 36, sponsors and investigators gain an implicit second look at accumulating data, and even without formal interim analysis, the temptation to extend follow-up after noticing a trend is a known source of Type I error inflation.
The Endpoint and the Recalculation
The second signal is the choice of primary outcome. The Mini-ICF-APP is a clinician-rated scale measuring functional limitations across multiple domains, validated in Italian outpatients with schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder in a sample of 74 patients published in PMC. That validation study established concurrent validity with the Global Assessment of Functioning, but 74 outpatients in a single university-based mental health center is a narrow normative base for a scale that now anchors a Lancet Psychiatry primary endpoint. Whether the Mini-ICF-APP replaced a different pre-registered outcome after the trial began is not determinable from the publicly available trial record.
The third and sharpest signal is the sample size recalculation. ICH E6(R3), finalized by the International Council for Harmonisation and adopted as FDA guidance, recommends that any amendment to a trial’s statistical plan be pre-specified, documented in a formal protocol amendment, and kept blinded to accumulating outcome data at the time of the revision. A sample size recalculation that is performed after unblinded data are available is not a statistical correction, it is a steering mechanism. The ICH standard exists precisely because the history of clinical research includes investigators who, seeing a signal weaker than expected, recalculated their way to a confirmatory result.
Whether the recalculation was performed with or without access to unblinded outcome data is not addressed in the published record available for review, and that gap is the problem. In a field where positive psychotherapy trials for schizophrenia-spectrum disorders are rare and methodologically contested, as illustrated by the antipsychotic reduction trial published in Lancet Psychiatry, which screened 4,157 people but randomised 253, and found nuanced results even with that level of rigor, the burden of methodological transparency sits squarely with the investigators.
What This Means for Sponsors and Regulators
Soft Protocol Drift does not require bad faith. It requires only an absence of structural guardrails, a willing journal, and the genuine belief that extending a promising trial serves patients. That is precisely what makes it dangerous, and exactly why the operational response must be structural rather than moral.
For sponsors designing psychotherapy trials for regulatory submission, the MPP-S case reinforces a discipline that is frequently observed in breach: every change to treatment duration, primary endpoint, or sample size must be catalogued in a dated, version-controlled protocol amendment filed before any look at unblinded outcome data. This is not bureaucratic formalism. It is the only mechanism that separates a legitimate recalculation from a data-driven steering event. Guidance aligned with ICH E6(R3) calls for amendment documentation to be traceable within the trial record; reviewers who cannot link each design change to a pre-data document may raise questions that complicate a subsequent regulatory submission.
For technology vendors offering adaptive trial platforms, the MPP-S situation points to a gap in how software handles post-hoc versus pre-specified amendments. Whether existing trial management platforms adequately separate statistical plan access from amendment finalization is a design question the field has not yet answered publicly. The FDA’s existing framework on adaptive designs already distinguishes between prospectively planned adaptations and unplanned modifications, the latter trigger a much higher evidentiary bar in any subsequent regulatory submission.
For journal editors and peer reviewers, the right question is not whether the MPP-S effect sizes are clinically meaningful, a Hedges’ g of 0.93 at 36 months on a validated functional outcome is, by any reasonable benchmark, a large effect. The right question is whether the analytical framework that generated those numbers was fixed before the numbers started arriving. The correspondence raises that question. The published trial record, as it stands, does not fully answer it.
In the coming years, one of two things seems likely to happen in psychiatric psychotherapy trials. Either sponsors and academic investigators will voluntarily adopt prospective amendment registers, a parallel to the trial registration movement that transformed RCT credibility by making prospective registration a norm, or regulators will impose documentation requirements that make Soft Protocol Drift an explicit grounds for rejection. The first sponsor to submit a fully transparent psychotherapy NDA, with every protocol change time-stamped and blinded, will set an evidentiary standard that every subsequent submission gets measured against. The question is whether the field learns that lesson from the MPP-S correspondence or from a failed submission.
