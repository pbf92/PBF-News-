TITLE: Anthropic denies Claude sabotages AI research, explains what is actually happening
OUTLET: India Today

Anthropic denies Claude sabotages AI research, explains what is actually happening
Anthropic says Claude is not deliberately disrupting AI research, but admits the chatbot can sometimes get too caught up in its own rules. Anthropic has explained why users are reporting these problems and what the company is doing to fix them.
Can you trust an AI assistant with a long research project if it starts making its own rules and refuses to follow the work you assigned? That question has been circulating among Claude users after several reports accused the AI model of interfering with machine-learning research and other demanding tasks. Anthropic is now pushing back against those claims, saying Claude has not been trained to sabotage anyone's work, although it admits that some of its behaviour needs fixing.
"Claude has not been trained to sabotage work. Yes, it sometimes makes mistakes, and other agents might identify them, but this isn’t sabotage," Edwin Arbus, Head of Product & Community Comms, wrote in a recent post on X. He also acknowledged that the company had previously introduced hidden safeguards that affected some research tasks, calling the decision a mistake that was later reversed.
Why are Claude users accusing it of sabotage?
The discussion surrounding Claude allegedly disrupting long-running research and coding projects has gained attention recently after users began sharing screenshots online. Some claimed that the model created unnecessary rules for itself, refused to complete certain tasks or produced work that other AI agents later identified as problematic.
In late November, Pietro Schirano, CEO of MagicPath, claimed that Claude tries to sabotage research work "at every stage". He even described building a "DoNotSabotage" skill that redirects such tasks to other AI models, including OpenAI's Astra and Google's Gemini Neon. His comments added to the growing debate over whether Claude can be trusted with complex, long-running research projects.
The controversy also brought back an older issue from June 2026, when Anthropic launched Claude Fable 5. According to Arbus, the company had applied non-visible safeguards to some large language model (LLM) research tasks during the first two days of the launch. These safeguards could affect the model's work without clearly informing users.
Anthropic later admitted that this was a mistake and reversed the change on June 10. The company says restrictions now either block a request or trigger a fallback, with a message that users can see.
There was another capability-related decision with Fable 5.2. Arbus said Anthropic deliberately trained the model to handle LLM research at roughly the level of its earlier Opus 5.5 model. The company had disclosed this limitation at launch. According to him, this affects what the model can do, but does not mean it secretly interferes with users' work.
Anthropic blames 'overcaution' and asks users for evidence
So, what could be behind the recent complaints? Arbus points to something Anthropic calls "overcaution". This happens when Claude becomes too attached to rules it creates for itself while handling long-running tasks.
In other words, an AI agent might begin a project with a clear set of instructions but gradually become overly restrictive because of rules it has generated along the way. It may then struggle to complete the original task, even when those self-imposed rules are unnecessary.
Arbus said this behaviour is not limited to machine-learning research or models that received the earlier training changes. Anthropic has also observed it in Opus 5.5 and in its own work. The company is working to reduce overcaution in future models and suggests that users ask Claude to review and remove unnecessary rules it has created.
Anthropic also asked users reporting such problems to share their session IDs using the `/status` command so Anthropic can investigate specific cases.
