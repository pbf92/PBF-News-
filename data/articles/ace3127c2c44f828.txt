TITLE: Look-alike pages hit hardest in Google's November update, consultants say
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google's November 2026 core update finished rolling out on November 24, 2026, after 13 days and 20 hours, and the consultants who track search rankings have started to name the sites that lost. In episode 1,241 of The Edward Show, published on YouTube on November 27, 2026, search engine optimization (SEO) podcaster Edward Sturm gathered those early readings.
Three groups came up again and again: sites that grew by publishing thousands of AI-written pages, folders of near-identical pages built from one template, and lead-generation sites in finance, health and law. He also went through estimates showing Reddit's search clicks falling sharply. The episode ended with a notice Google posted about an hour after the November rollout closed: another broad ranking update, scheduled for December 2026.
In Short
Google's November ranking update ended on November 24, and SEO consultants say the biggest losers were sites with thousands of near-identical or unreviewed AI-written pages, plus lead-generation sites in finance, health and law. That matters to any business that gets customers from Google, because the losses seem to land on whole sections of a site, while the main product and brand pages hold up. Google has not said what the update targeted, a December update is already scheduled, and Google's own guidance says to wait a week before reading the numbers - for the November update, that week covers Black Friday.
A quiet finish to a loud update
Google's notice of completion was one line long. "The rollout was complete as of November 24, 2026," the entry on Google's Search Status Dashboard reads, timestamped 06:03 Pacific time. Google's Search Central account repeated the news on X later that morning. Sturm opened the episode with that contrast. "Google wrote unceremoniously on X," he said. "But looking at the SEO community, this was one of the widest felt updates so far, despite how casual Google was in their post wrapping it up."
The update had started on November 10 at 10:06 Pacific time, with Google saying it "applies globally and to all languages" and "may take up to 2 weeks to complete." PPC Land noted the next day that the two-week window meant the update could run until three days before Black Friday. It used almost all of it. Google gave no list of affected systems or types of content when it launched the update, and it added none at the end.
A core update is a broad change to how Google ranks all results, not a penalty aimed at particular sites. Google released three of them in 2026, and the November one was the longest of the three.
The December 2025 figures come from PPC Land's coverage of that rollout. The March and May 2026 lengths come from PPC Land's report on the November launch, and the November 2026 times come from Google's dashboard.
Is Google still worth the trouble?
Sturm began with a complaint he said he hears constantly from listeners: that Google no longer matters. His answer came from a post on X by JH Scherck, dated November 25, 2026. According to Scherck, ChatGPT had started showing "five blue links above its generated response. Time is a flat circle." Scherck suggested the change might be connected to almost every company he works with being disappointed with ChatGPT's ads, and wrote that OpenAI could place ads among those links.
Sturm stressed that the links were not ads. "These are just organic links," he said, which look much like a Google results page sitting above ChatGPT's answer. PPC Land has tracked OpenAI's ad business closely: a report from Similarweb and Graphite found that ChatGPT's worldwide ad click rate rose 112% over a period starting on March 30, and that 43.4% of US users eligible for ads had seen at least one by September 2026.
Sturm then gave Google's scale: 5 trillion searches a year, 13.7 billion a day, 158,000 a second. Google first published the 5 trillion figure in March 2025. The daily and per-second figures are that number divided down. He added that AI companies are trying to obtain what he called Google's "secret sauce," and that Google is responding by serving scrapers fake search results pages and hidden redirects, among other measures. He did not give a source for that claim. Google has also gone to court over scraping: it sued SerpApi over search scraping. "So, optimize for Google and you're going to optimize for AI," Sturm said.
Seven readings from Ira Bodnar
Most of the episode rests on a numbered list posted on X on November 24 by Ira Bodnar, which Sturm read point by point and commented on. Its first point set the frame: "The main target was mass AI generated pages. Sites that grew fast by publishing thousands of AI written pages are now losing that traffic."
AI pages that were never reviewed
Sturm separated two uses of AI. "At the same time, so many websites are using AI as an assistant to write content. That's totally fine," he said. The problem, in his reading, was AI content nobody checked, along with the hallucinations - confident statements that are false - that AI tools are known to produce. "One of the targets for the November core update, it seems like it was AI content that hasn't been reviewed because, as Google says, AI is prone to hallucinations."
Google tightened its wording on this point eight weeks before the update. On October 1, 2026, it added a sentence to its guidance on AI content saying it is critical to manually fact-check and review all AI-generated content for accuracy before publishing. Google's spam rules go further on volume: its scaled content abuse policy, in place since March 2024, covers pages produced in bulk mainly to manipulate rankings, whether people or software write them. Since May 2026, those spam policies have also covered AI Overviews and AI Mode.
Sturm was skeptical of the AI-site owners who post in his comments that their traffic is soaring. "We don't know how long it's been skyrocketing for," he said. He described AI sites whose traffic climbed for six or eight months, "and then they get hit and they can't recover."
Three days of quiet
Bodnar's second point was about timing. "The real impact started on Friday the 13th, 3 days after Google announced it," Sturm read, adding that this was when the data in Google's Search Console - the free dashboard site owners use to see their Google traffic - began to show sharp gains and drops. Bodnar proposed November 13 as the before-and-after line.
That matches what others saw. Search Engine Roundtable reported on November 16 that the update may have kicked in three to four days after the announcement. Google rarely explains this pattern. In March, John Mueller said Google generally does not announce stages of core updates, and that the changes sometimes have to work step by step.
The template problem
The third point drew the longest comment from Sturm. In Bodnar's words, folders "built from one template got hit the hardest. Think a page per word, per area code, or per X versus Y repeated thousands of times aimed at searches that have little to do with what you sell."
Sturm gave two examples of what he called doorway pages: pages built to catch many slightly different searches and pass visitors on. The first was an SEO software company producing thousands of comparison pages about software that has nothing to do with SEO. The second was closer to ordinary practice: "A web design agency publishing 10,000 near identical web design in city pages," where only the city name changes. "Every SEO should understand what doorway pages are," he said. "They are against Google's guidelines."
Google's spam policies call this "doorway abuse." "Doorway abuse is when sites or pages are created to rank for specific, similar search queries," the policy page says. "They lead users to intermediate pages that aren't as useful as the final destination." The page lists four examples, including "Having multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page" and "Creating substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy."
The pages Sturm described are often produced with programmatic SEO: software fills one page template with data from a spreadsheet or database to create thousands of pages. Done well, that can power useful directories. In Sturm's telling, the version that lost in November was the one aimed at searches with no link to what the business sells. "Doorway pages are a recurring topic in the November core update," he said.
Lead generation in money, health and law
Bodnar's fourth point named an industry pattern. Sturm read that "lead generation sites in finance, health, and law really got hit," citing UK data from Sistrix, the search visibility tool, which showed "the biggest losers were sites that publish information articles mainly to collect leads."
A lead-generation site makes money by collecting contact details from people and selling them to businesses. Sturm's example: "a website publishing thousands of AI generated articles about car accident injuries to collect and sell leads to personal injury lawyers." The episode gave no site names, visibility scores or sample size for the Sistrix data. PPC Land has used Sistrix figures before, for example when 52 of 75 British news sites lost visibility in the December 2025 update.
Who moved up
On the other side of the ledger, according to Bodnar, "the winners were official sites and original data sources. Brands people search for by name moved up along with sites that own the data they publish."
Sturm linked this to branded searches - searches that include a company's name - and to entities, the way Google maps a brand, person or product as a distinct thing. In his view, "Google also understands entities and can tie an entity back to the domain it originated from." Neither he nor Bodnar gave figures for the winners.
When to read the numbers
Bodnar's sixth and seventh points were about measurement. Sturm read that "Google says to wait a full week, then compare November 25 to December 1st with November 3rd to 9th and check last year's Black Friday week." The seventh: "leave the pages that still rank alone. Fix or merge the templated folders first because Google warns against changing content that already performs well."
Both points reflect Google's documentation on core updates, last updated on December 10, 2025. "We recommend waiting at least a full week after a core update completes before analyzing your site in Search Console," the page says. It suggests comparing a week after the update with a week before the rollout began. For a small drop in position, it says "we recommend avoiding making changes to content that's already performing well."
The calendar is the catch. The first clean week after the rollout is also the busiest shopping week of the year, so search demand in many categories changes for reasons that have nothing to do with rankings. Bodnar's suggestion to check last year's Black Friday week is a way to separate the two.
Sturm added his own argument for subfolders, the sections of a site that sit under one path, such as example.com/blog/. In his view, putting experimental content in its own subfolder means that if it is penalized, "the rest of your site will be left intact," and that it is easier to remove or to analyze separately.
Checking AI answers as well as links
A second list came from an account called Jam, posted on X on November 24. It set out a way to tell whether a site that lost search traffic also disappeared from AI answers. As Sturm read it, the method has four steps:
- The first step uses the generative AI report in Search Console, under Search. Its impressions are set beside overall search performance. According to Jam, falling clicks alongside steady AI impressions mean the site is still showing up in Google's AI answers. The next check separates pages that lost rankings from pages that lost click-through rate.
- The second step starts with the pages closest to revenue.
