TITLE: Google frees US candidates' business ads from election ad rules
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google has carved a narrow exception into its United States election advertising rules for politicians with day jobs. An update to the Google Ads Political content policy, posted on November 16, 2026 and labeled "November 2026", exempts ads that show a current officeholder or candidate promoting a legally-established government program, or working as a business owner, employee or licensed agent selling a product or service, as long as the ad makes no reference to their party, office or campaign.
The Political content policy page, read on November 25, 2026, now carries the change under the heading "Commercial exceptions to US Election Ads". The same page sets out the rest of Google's political advertising framework: ten regions where advertisers must be verified before they run election ads, and ten regions where Google restricts or prohibits political ads on its platforms, from Brazil and the European Union to Japan, South Korea and Taiwan.
In Short
Google now lets ads that show a US politician in a business or government-program role run without the extra checks that apply to election ads, provided the ad says nothing about parties, office or campaigns. That matters to officeholders and candidates with private careers, such as the insurance and real estate agents the policy names, and to the agencies that buy their business ads. Outside the US, the policy still restricts or bans political ads in ten regions, and Google's 2026 change log records no change to any of them.
What Google changed in November
The change log entry, titled "Update to Political Content policy (November 2026)", is short. "In November 2026, Google will update the Political content policy under the United States (US) election ads section to clarify commercial exemptions," according to Google. It continues: "Advertisements that feature a current officeholder or candidate solely in connection with a legally-established government program or as a commercial employee/agent offering products or services for sale, without reference to their political office, party or campaign, will be exempt from US Election Ads verification."
The wording that now sits on the policy page is longer, and it reaches further. The page introduces the carve-out with this sentence: "Advertisements featuring an in-scope officeholder or candidate are exempt from US Election Ads policy requirements if they:"
Two conditions follow, word for word:
- "Promote a legally-established government program, product, or service, provided there are no direct or indirect references to political parties or a candidate's or office holder's political office or campaign activities."
- "Represent a candidate or officeholder strictly in their capacity as a business owner or employee or licensed agent of a business, such as an insurance agent or real estate agent, offering a tangible product or service for sale. They must not contain direct or indirect references to political parties or the individual's political office or campaign activities."
Three differences between the announcement and the page stand out. The announcement speaks of an exemption from "US Election Ads verification". The page goes wider, exempting qualifying ads from "US Election Ads policy requirements", which on the same page include verification, a "Paid for by" disclosure and limits on targeting. The page also names two example professions, insurance agent and real estate agent, which the announcement does not. And it sets a stricter test on content, ruling out "direct or indirect" references, where the announcement says only "without reference to".
Google calls the update a clarification. Its announcement does not show what the US section said before, and the page itself carries no "last updated" date.
Why a carve-out was needed
The answer lies in two earlier changes. In May 2022, Google said it would "eliminate existing products, services and news exemptions in election ads policies for certain regions", with enforcement starting that month. The United States was one of nine regions affected, alongside Australia, Brazil, the European Union, India, Israel, New Zealand, Taiwan and the United Kingdom.
Then, in an update posted on July 1, 2024, Google widened the definition of US Election Ads. According to the policy page, "US Election Ads are ads that feature any of the following:" a current officeholder or candidate for an elected federal office; a current officeholder or candidate for a state-level elected office; a current officeholder or candidate for a territory-level elected office; "A federal, state, or territory-level political party"; or "A state or territory-level ballot measure, initiative, or proposition that has qualified for the ballot" in its state or territory.
Taken together, those two changes meant that any ad featuring a state legislator or a candidate for a statehouse seat fell inside the election ads regime, whatever the ad was selling. The July 2024 definition reached well beyond Congress, to every state and territory office. An ad for a family-run hardware store, fronted by an owner who sits in a state legislature, would have met the definition. City and county offices are not on the list, which stops at federal, state and territory level.
The November text gives such advertisers a way out, on Google's terms. The ad must show the person "strictly in their capacity" as a business owner, employee or licensed agent, offering "a tangible product or service for sale". Any mention, direct or indirect, of the person's party, office or campaign brings the ad back into scope.
What an exempt ad avoids
The practical effect depends on what the US Election Ads rules require. The policy page attaches four sets of obligations to Election Ads, and the commercial exception, on the page's own wording, lifts them for qualifying ads.
The targeting limit is the most consequential for a business advertiser. Under the Election Ads rules, an insurance agency fronted by a state senator would have been limited to location, age, gender and contextual targeting, with no radius around its office and no audience lists. On the page's own terms, an ad that qualifies for the commercial exception no longer carries those limits.
The exception does not touch Google's other policies. Ads must still comply "with all other Google Ads policies and local laws and regulations", according to the page. The page also warns that "Non-compliance with our political content policies may result in information about your account and political ads being disclosed publicly or to relevant government agencies and regulators."
How election ads verification works
The policy page describes the verification path in a fixed order. According to Google, it runs as follows:
- An advertiser checks whether the ad meets the Election Ads definition for the region the ad is about. Each of the ten verification regions has its own definition on the page.
- To run Election Ads in that region, "an advertiser must be verified under that region's Election Ads verification process". The page gives the example of Australia: an advertiser "must complete the Australia Election Ads verification process to run Australia Election Ads in Australia."
- To run a region's Election Ads anywhere outside that region, the advertiser must be verified under "either the Election Ads verification or Advertiser verification process for the region in which the advertiser is located." The page's example: "a UK-based advertiser must complete a UK verification process to run New Zealand Election Ads in any region outside New Zealand."
- Once verified, the advertiser targets only by location (no radius), age, gender and contextual options.
- Google adds a "Paid for by" disclosure automatically "for most ad formats", drawing on the information supplied during verification.
- If the ad contains synthetic or digitally altered content, the advertiser ticks the box in the "Altered or synthetic content" section of campaign settings.
One group is shut out entirely. "Google Ad Grants accounts aren't eligible to run Election Ads or apply for Election Ads verification in these regions," according to the page. Ad Grants is Google's program that gives nonprofits free search advertising.
The general advertiser verification program is separate from Election Ads verification, though the page treats it as an alternative route for cross-border election ads. Google has tightened that program over time. In November 2025, it added an example to its Circumventing systems policy warning that advertisers who give false information during verification will lose verified status and face suspension.
Disclosure rules by format
For most formats, Google writes the "Paid for by" line itself. For four formats, on the Google Display Network, YouTube and Display & Video 360, the advertiser must add it inside the creative. "For visual formats, the disclosure must be visible at all times and be of a sufficient size to be legible to an average viewer. For audio-only formats, the disclosure must be substantially similar in pitch, tone and speed to the rest of the ad," according to the page.
The page adds that the disclosure "isn't a replacement for any other disclosures you may be required to include in your ad by law."
Synthetic and altered content
The synthetic content section dates from an update posted on July 1, 2024. It covers "images, video, or audio that inauthentically depict real or realistic-looking people or events", in other words the kind of material usually called a deepfake. Two in-scope examples are listed: "An ad with synthetic content that makes it appear as if a person is saying or doing something they didn't say or do" and "An ad with synthetic content that alters footage of a real event or generates a realistic portrayal of an event to depict scenes that did not actually take place."
Routine edits are out of scope. The page names "image resizing, cropping, color or brightening corrections, defect correction like "red eye" removal, or background edits that do not create realistic depictions of actual events."
Once the box is ticked, Google generates the label itself for three placements: Feeds on Mobile Phones; Shorts on Mobile Phones; and In-stream on Computers, Mobile Phones, Mobile Web, and TV Screens. Everywhere else, the advertiser must provide a disclosure that is "clear and conspicuous". The page offers sample wording, including "Altered or synthetic content." and "This video content was synthetically generated."
Enforcement here comes with notice. "Violations of this policy won't lead to immediate account suspension without prior warning. Google will issue a warning at least 7 days before suspending your account," according to the page.

Ten regions where Google restricts political ads
The second half of the page lists regions where the problem is not verification but permission. "In the following regions, there are restrictions on political ads," according to Google, which adds that "there may be additional prohibitions or requirements listed in the legal requirements policy."
Canada appears three times because the page splits it into British Columbia, federal Election Ads and federal Issue Ads. Counted by region, the list holds ten entries.
