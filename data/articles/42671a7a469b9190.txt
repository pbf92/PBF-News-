TITLE: Meta Q3 Earnings: $34.91B AI Capex, Muse Hits 10M [2026]
OUTLET: shattered.io

Meta Platforms reported third-quarter 2026 results on Wednesday, October 21, and two numbers dominated the call: how much the company is spending to build AI infrastructure, and whether anyone outside Menlo Park is actually using the AI product that spending is supposed to justify. Analysts tracked by Visible Alpha had expected revenue of $63.42 billion for the quarter, up 23.8% year over year, with capital expenditures projected at $41.68 billion. That consensus capex figure alone would have marked one of the largest single-quarter infrastructure outlays ever disclosed by a consumer internet company, but Meta’s release put revenue at $64.60 billion, up 26% year over year, and capital expenditures, including principal payments on finance leases, at $34.91 billion, below the $41.68 billion in the headline. The spending story ran alongside a separate storyline: Meta’s new personal assistant, Muse, reportedly crossed 5 million downloads in its first 22 days on the market, according toSensor Tower data cited by CNBC. Sensor Tower later ranked Muse among the fastest apps to reach 10 million U.S. downloads in October 2026, a figure that supersedes the 5 million in the headline.
The timing matters. Meta has spent much of 2026 defending a spending plan that could reach $145 billion in capital expenditures for the full year, a number CNBC reported directly from the company’s own guidance, and on October 21 Meta narrowed that 2026 outlook, including principal payments on finance leases, to $135-145 billion from its prior $130-145 billion range. Wall Street has mostly gone along with it on faith that AI products would eventually generate revenue to match the outlay. The October 21 earnings call was the first real test of that faith since Muse launched, and investors watched the free-cash-flow line as closely as the revenue line: analysts had estimated Meta would use $8.52 billion in free cash flow during the quarter, while the company reported free cash flow of negative $2.12 billion, a gap that still reflects just how much of the company’s operating cash is being funneled directly into servers, chips, and data centers rather than returned to shareholders or banked for flexibility.
Why Meta’s Q3 2026 Earnings Report Matters Now
Every public company reports quarterly earnings, but not every earnings call becomes a referendum on an entire industry’s spending logic. Meta’s October 21 report has become exactly that. The company sits at the center of a broader debate over whether the hundreds of billions of dollars that hyperscalers are pouring into AI data centers will show up as revenue within a reasonable timeframe, or whether the industry is building capacity years ahead of demand. Meta’s own capex trajectory, with the 2026 outlook narrowed on October 21 to $135-145 billion from the prior $130-145 billion range, puts it squarely alongside Microsoft, Google, and Amazon in the small group of companies whose spending decisions move markets on their own.
What makes this quarter different from the last several is that Meta finally has a consumer-facing product, Muse, that it can point to as early evidence the spending is translating into something people want. Previous earnings calls leaned heavily on advertising-targeting improvements and Llama model benchmarks, both of which are useful internally but hard for retail investors to evaluate. Download numbers are a much simpler story to tell, and the clearest public data points have come from Sensor Tower’s tracking of Muse’s download curve and, according to reports of the October 21 call, from Mark Zuckerberg’s statement that more than 10 million people in the United States and Canada had set up Muse agents during approximately the preceding month and a half. Meta also said it had launched Muse and Muse Wonder as milestones toward its personal-superintelligence initiative. Meta’s broader AI strategy and spending decisions have already reshaped other parts of the business, including how the company frames its AI terms and training policies ahead of an October 30 deadline.
The stakes extend beyond Meta’s own stock price. A soft quarter, or a capex number that overshoots guidance without a credible revenue story attached, would feed a narrative already circulating among some investors that AI infrastructure spending across the industry has outpaced any near-term payoff. A strong quarter, by contrast, would give Meta and its peers more room to keep spending without facing the kind of shareholder pushback that has started surfacing elsewhere in the sector.
The Capital Expenditure Numbers, Explained
Capital expenditures are the money Meta spends building the physical infrastructure, primarily data centers, servers, and networking gear, that its AI systems run on. For Q3 2026, analyst consensus compiled by Visible Alpha had put that figure at $41.68 billion, but Meta’s October 21 results showed capital expenditures, including principal payments on finance leases, of $34.91 billion, so the consensus number is not the official total. Even the official figure represents a substantial share of what Meta plans to spend across the full year, especially set against the 2026 outlook, which Meta narrowed on October 21 to $135-145 billion from its prior $130-145 billion, after CNBC had reported capital expenditures could run as high as $145 billion.
The free-cash-flow impact is where this spending becomes tangible for shareholders. Analysts had estimated a free-cash-flow usage of $8.52 billion for the quarter, while Meta reported free cash flow of negative $2.12 billion on cash flow from operating activities of $32.79 billion, meaning the company still burned through cash rather than generating it once capital spending was netted against incoming cash flow. That is a meaningful shift in how Meta’s financial profile reads to the market. For years, Meta was a cash-generation machine that funded share buybacks and dividends almost as an afterthought. A quarter of negative free cash flow driven by AI infrastructure spending changes the conversation from “how much will Meta return to shareholders” to “how long can Meta sustain this spending pace before something has to give.” Meta held $82.45 billion in cash, cash equivalents, and marketable securities as of September 30, 2026, a cushion that softens that question without removing it.
Revenue growth is the number that is supposed to justify all of this. Analysts had expected $63.42 billion for the quarter, representing 23.8% year-over-year growth. Meta reported revenue of $64.60 billion, an increase of 26% year-over-year on both a reported and constant currency basis, with ad impressions up 10% and average price per ad up 14%. That growth rate suggests Meta’s core advertising business, still the overwhelming majority of its revenue, continues to perform well even as the company diverts enormous sums toward AI bets that have not yet produced comparable returns. The read-through for investors is straightforward: as long as advertising keeps growing at a healthy clip, Meta can absorb AI losses as a bet on future optionality. If advertising growth slows at the same time AI spending accelerates, the math gets much harder to defend on a call with analysts.
Muse Downloads: What 5 Million in 22 Days Actually Signals
Muse, Meta’s personal AI assistant, reportedly surpassed 5 million downloads within its first 22 days on the market, according to Sensor Tower data cited by CNBC, and Sensor Tower later reported that Muse reached 10 million U.S. downloads in October 2026. That is a fast initial curve for a consumer AI app, and it is the clearest piece of public evidence Meta has that its AI spending is producing something people will actually install on their phones. But download counts are an early-funnel metric, not a monetization metric, and the gap between the two is exactly what analysts were expected to press Meta’s executives on during the October 21 call.
Several details about Muse remain unconfirmed in Reuters’ reporting on the earnings preview, and it is worth being precise about what is and is not established. Reports elsewhere have floated subscription pricing tiers for Muse, including figures around $20 and $100 per month for power users, and one account of the October 21 call described Muse as free to use with those paid plans. Meta’s own announcement on October 27, 2026 said only that Muse was free for most uses, with subscription plans for users wanting more capability, and it did not state prices, while the Reuters material previewing this earnings call did not confirm the figures either. Similarly, claims that Muse carried an internal codename during development, reportedly “Hatch,” have not been confirmed through that reporting. Broader claims about user retention rates, daily active user counts, or a reported 60% increase in daily assistant interactions also remain unconfirmed. Readers should treat any of those specific figures as unverified until Meta or a named outlet confirms them directly.
What was confirmed, and what mattered most going into the October 21 call, was the download trajectory itself and the fact that Reuters and other outlets identified Muse adoption as one of the two central storylines investors were watching, alongside the capex figure. Meta has already made moves to expand what its AI assistants can do and where they show up, a pattern visible in how the company has pushed Muse-branded tools into other product surfaces, including its own open-model initiatives covered in earlier reporting on Meta’s Muse Spark Open model and its parameter count. The company has also tested different pricing approaches with related Muse products, a strategy detailed in coverage of Muse Wonder’s monthly pricing experiments.
Where Muse Stands After the Earnings Report
Meta’s own materials add detail that download counts cannot. On October 12, 2026, Meta introduced Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, describing them as its most capable models to date. Muse Wonder Full became available to Muse Code subscribers, and it is listed for the Power Usage plan at $50/month and the Maximum Usage plan at $200/month. Meta said Muse Wonder would soon power Muse, initially for subscribers. The named developer offerings are Meta Model API, Muse Code, Muse Wonder, and Muse Wonder Full. Muse Wonder (muse-wonder-1.0) is available in Model API and Muse Code with a 1,048,576-token context window and is listed at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens, while Muse Wonder Full is available only in Muse Code, not through the Model API. In November 2026 Meta also launched Muse Ember as muse-ember-1.0, priced at $0.10/$0.50 per 1M tokens, with a 1M-token context window and 512K-token outputs. Developers weighing the tools should still check Meta’s own pages for current rates before budgeting.
The consumer product is still expanding in stages. On October 21, 2026, Muse had not yet launched globally because of its compute requirements. On October 27, 2026, Meta said Muse was free for most uses and offered subscription plans for users wanting more capability, but it did not state official subscription prices. Muse Secure VM, also described on October 27, 2026, is a cloud computer intended to store connected-service data and credentials while keeping passwords and payment methods hidden from the agent, a design that speaks directly to the privacy questions raised in earlier coverage. On November 5, 2026, Meta began rolling out voice and expressive avatars in the Muse app on mobile, web, and Mac, initially in the United States, and on November 6, 2026 it introduced spaces, customizable experiences inside Muse such as gift trackers, games, and planning boards. Meta also said voice conversations were coming to AI glasses and Muse Charm.
