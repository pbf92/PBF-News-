TITLE: EDPB picks AI candidate screening as its 2027 enforcement target
OUTLET: PPC Land

Europe's data protection authorities will spend part of 2027 examining how organisations use automated tools, including AI systems, to screen job candidates. The European Data Protection Board said on November 11, 2026 that the topic of its sixth coordinated enforcement action will be automated decision-making in recruitment, with a focus on the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing and the related right of access.
The same plenary in Brussels produced two other outputs. The board adopted Statement 2/2026 on the first review of the European Union's adequacy decision for the Republic of Korea, dated November 10, 2026, and it approved the final version of its recommendations on processor binding corporate rules, known as BCR-P, after a public consultation that closed in March. None of the three carries a fine. Each, though, sets out where European regulators intend to look next - at hiring software, at data flowing to Seoul, and at the internal rulebooks multinational service groups use to move data across borders.
In Short
Europe's privacy regulators have chosen automated screening of job candidates, AI tools included, as the subject of a joint enforcement action starting in 2027, and at the same meeting they backed the EU's data adequacy finding for South Korea and finalised the rules for processor binding corporate rules. The enforcement action matters to any organisation that lets software decide who moves forward in a hiring process, because regulators will check whether candidates can avoid fully automated rejections and find out how those decisions were made. Transfers of personal data from the EU to Korea can continue under the existing adequacy decision, with reviews moving to a four-year cycle, while groups holding processor BCRs must align them with the final recommendations either during their application or in their 2028 annual update.
The sixth coordinated action: candidate screening
The announcement on the enforcement topic is short and precise. According to the EDPB, the action "will concern automated decision-making (ADM), including AI-driven ADM, in recruitment." The board then narrowed it: "More specifically, the coordinated action will focus on the enforcement of the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing and the related right of access, in the context of the use of automated candidate screening tools, including AI-driven ones."
That wording points at two provisions of the GDPR, although the news item itself cites no article numbers. The right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing sits in Article 22, the GDPR's central provision on automated decision-making, and applies when such a decision produces legal effects or similarly significant effects for a person. The related right of access sits in Article 15, which entitles individuals to learn whether such automated decisions are being made about them and to receive meaningful information about the logic involved and the envisaged consequences.
Timing remains loose. "DPAs will join this action on a voluntary basis in the coming weeks, and the action itself will be launched in the course of 2027," according to the EDPB. The board did not say which authorities proposed the topic, how many have already committed, whether the action will use questionnaires or formal investigations, or whether it will target employers, recruitment agencies, software vendors or all three.
The wording is narrow. As described by the board, the action concerns screening, the stage at which an applicant is filtered in or out, and two specific rights attached to that stage, rather than AI in employment generally or the management of existing staff.
How a coordinated enforcement action works
The Coordinated Enforcement Framework, or CEF, was developed as part of the EDPB's 2021-2023 strategy, according to the board's CEF page. That page describes the process in six stages:
- Every year, EDPB members submit proposals for the topic of the next annual action.
- All EDPB members vote on the topic at a plenary meeting.
- National data protection authorities decide whether to take part, on a voluntary basis. Participation can take the form of fact-finding exercises, new formal investigations, follow-up of ongoing formal investigations, or a combination.
- The authority that proposed the topic coordinates the action with assistance from the EDPB Secretariat, and a questionnaire or audit guide is built.
- All participating authorities launch the action at the same time.
- Each authority drafts a national report, and the coordinators then produce an EDPB report that is adopted at a plenary.
Five earlier actions
The 2027 action will be the sixth. The first four are listed on the EDPB's CEF page with a date for each, and the fifth was selected in October 2025.
The CEF page does not label the dates it shows, but they match the publication of each action's final report. PPC Land's report on the EDPB's 2025 annual report recorded that the right of access report was published in January 2025 and the right to erasure report in February 2026. On that pattern, a report on recruitment screening would arrive no earlier than late 2027 and more likely in 2028, although the EDPB has given no date.
What earlier actions produced
A coordinated action is not a fine in itself. It can, however, become the vehicle for one. The right to erasure action involved 32 data protection authorities and 764 controllers that responded, according to PPC Land's coverage of the EDPB annual report, and its February 2026 report closed with non-binding recommendations. Months later, France's CNIL fined consulting firm EXTIA 300,000 euros after an inspection carried out within that same coordinated action. The CNIL found that 204 of 265 deletion requests received in 2024 were not processed, not answered, or answered months late. The deliberation was adopted on July 21, 2026 and published on September 9, 2026.
The access action of 2024 had a similar afterlife. The board's 2024-2025 work programme, adopted on October 8, 2024, set the right of access as that year's topic and planned a fourth action for 2025. Each of these actions has given participating authorities a shared questionnaire or audit guide and a shared set of findings.
For organisations that run candidate screening, contact from a regulator under the 2027 action could take the form of a fact-finding exercise or a formal investigation, depending on the authority. The EXTIA case shows that a fact-finding exercise is not always the end of the matter.
Why recruitment, and why it reaches beyond HR
The EDPB gave no reasons for its choice. PPC Land's coverage over the past two years, however, shows how much pressure has built on automated decisions in general and on hiring in particular.
The heaviest recent penalty under Article 22 came from the Netherlands. On August 21, 2026, the Dutch Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens fined Uber 824,990,000 euros after finding that software had deactivated driver accounts from 2018 to 2022 following fraud flags or persistently low ratings, with no human review. Uber has appealed. The same authority had earlier opened a consultation on explaining automated decisions to individuals in April 2026, with a 31-page draft on the right to an explanation that drew on the Court of Justice's SCHUFA ruling (C-634/21, December 7, 2023) and its Dun & Bradstreet ruling (C-203/22, February 27, 2025).
Spain moved on the employment side directly. Royal Decree 723/2026, in force since October 5, 2026, requires employers to tell staff in writing about workplace algorithms that affect pay, hours, tasks, promotion, place of work or termination. In the same article, PPC Land reported that the Spanish data protection authority, the AEPD, had issued a warning, reference AI-00009-2026, over a resume-screening tool that had not yet been deployed, finding that the formal presence of a person in the process did not by itself take the decision outside Article 22.
Job candidates have also begun using the GDPR right of access to find out why they were turned down. In April 2026, PPC Land reported how a subject access request exposed why a candidate was rejected despite a unanimous yes from the interview panel. The company took the full 30-day period to answer.
Courts, too, have been redrawing the line. Austria's Federal Administrative Court ruled in September 2025 that the public employment service's AMAS job-seeker classification system did not breach Article 22, because advisers could override the score. Germany's Federal Court of Justice, on October 21, 2026, held in four SCHUFA judgments that calculating a probability value about a person can itself be a decision under Article 22(1). An Advocate General opinion of October 15, 2026 in Case C-568/25 proposed that a retailer's automated refusal of pay-later options after a credit check can fall under the same article.
Then there is the AI Act. Under the provisional agreement reached on May 7, 2026, Annex III high-risk systems, a category that includes employment screening, must comply by December 2, 2027. The GDPR coordinated action will therefore run in the same year that AI Act obligations for hiring systems are due to take effect. Outside Europe, California approved SB 947 on September 30, 2026, which bars employers from relying solely on an automated decision system for discipline or termination from July 1, 2027, and in the United States the Mobley v. Workday case was expanded in July 2025 to cover applicants processed through HiredScore AI features.
Why should marketers care about a hiring topic? Article 22 is not an employment rule. It applies to any decision based solely on automated processing that produces legal or similarly significant effects, whether the system ranks job applicants, blocks a payment method at checkout or deactivates an account. The interpretations national authorities agree on during a coordinated action, especially on what counts as meaningful human involvement and how much of a system's logic must be disclosed under the access right, will be written down in a shared EDPB report. That report will be available to every authority that later examines automated scoring elsewhere.
Korea's adequacy decision: a review that ran late
The second plenary output concerns transfers. On December 17, 2021, the European Commission adopted Implementing Decision (EU) 2022/254, which found that the Republic of Korea ensures a level of protection for personal data transferred from the EU "that is essentially equivalent to that guaranteed by Regulation (EU) 2016/679." The adequacy decision allows personal data to flow from the European Economic Area to organisations in Korea subject to the Personal Information Protection Act, known as PIPA, without additional transfer tools.
According to the statement, the Commission had planned for a first review "to take place after three years from the date of the notification of the Decision to the Member States." Notification took place on December 17, 2021, the same day as adoption. The review meeting between the Korean and EU delegations took place online on October 17, 2025, and the Commission published its report (COM(2026) 384 final) and an accompanying staff working document (SWD(2026) 231 final) on July 23, 2026. The Commission concluded that Korea "continues to ensure an adequate level of protection for personal data from the European Union to entities in the Republic of Korea subject to its amended data protection framework," according to the EDPB's note to editors.
By PPC Land's calculation, the Commission report arrived 583 days, or about 19 months, after the three-year mark of December 17, 2024. The EDPB noticed. Its statement agrees to move to a four-year review cycle but, "having regard to the delay in completing the first review, recommends that this term be strictly observed for future reviews."
