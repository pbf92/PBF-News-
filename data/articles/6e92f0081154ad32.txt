TITLE: Blink Digital’s ₹1,000-Crore Claim Against Meta Continues as HC Rejects Plea to Restore Ad Accounts
OUTLET: MediaNews4U

Mumbai: The High Court of Judicature at Bombay has dismissed an interim application filed by digital advertising agency Blink Digital India Private Limited, which sought a mandatory temporary injunction against Meta Platform Inc. to restore its disabled Business Manager and advertising accounts. The ruling, delivered by Justice Sandeep V. Marne, prevents the immediate restoration of the agency’s access to its assets on Meta’s platforms, including Facebook, Instagram, and Messenger, effectively maintaining the status quo of the suspension imposed by the tech giant in August 2026.
Blink Digital, which provides media planning and advertising services, claimed that the abrupt disabling of its four Business Manager Accounts—which housed approximately 9,000 individual ad accounts—was arbitrary and violated its contractual and statutory rights. The agency argued that the move caused catastrophic damage to its operations, asserting that nearly 90% of its revenue was generated through Meta’s advertising infrastructure. The company had initially sought various reliefs, including a declaration that the enforcement action was illegal and an injunction against Meta from interfering with its status as a “Managed Agency Partner,” alongside a claim for ₹1,000 crore in damages.
In his decision, Justice Marne underscored that a mandatory temporary injunction is an extraordinary equitable remedy that can only be granted in exceptional, rare, and compelling circumstances. The Court held that the applicant must establish a strong prima facie case—of a standard significantly higher than the typical requirement for prohibitory injunctions—and must demonstrate that the balance of convenience and the risk of irreparable injury lie overwhelmingly in its favor. Concluding that Blink Digital failed to meet this high threshold, the court noted that the agency could be compensated monetarily through its pending suit, rendering an emergency mandatory order unnecessary.
Evidence of Policy Violations The Court’s reasoning hinged significantly on the conduct of the plaintiff. Meta submitted substantial evidence indicating that Blink Digital had engaged in “policy-violating conduct” and hosted “policy-violating advertisements.” Specifically, Meta pointed to marketing materials from the agency that promoted its services as providing “ban-proof” accounts, “no spend limits,” and “rental ad accounts.” According to Meta’s standards, such claims explicitly prohibit the sale, rental, or exchange of site privileges and constitute an attempt to circumvent enforcement mechanisms designed to protect platform integrity.
Justice Marne observed that the materials provided by Meta, which included transcripts of interviews and promotional posts from the agency’s leadership, created a prima facie impression that Blink Digital was attempting to secure an “unfair advantage” over competitors. The Court found that Meta was within its rights to take cumulative action against the agency for repeated breaches.
“The Plaintiff was afforded an opportunity to dispute the action taken against it and to seek reinstatement of its accounts,” the Court noted in its analysis of the process. “However, having regard to the conduct of the Plaintiff, Defendant No. 2 considered it appropriate not to restore the Plaintiff’s access to its Business Manager Accounts, advertising accounts, and other associated accounts and services. There is thus substantial compliance with provisions of Rule 4(8) of the Intermediary Rules .”
Compliance with Intermediary Guidelines Blink Digital had argued that Meta, as a significant social media intermediary, failed to follow the procedural requirements under Rule 4(8) of the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, by not providing sufficient notice or a reasonable opportunity to challenge the suspension. The Court, however, dismissed this claim, finding that Meta had provided broad reasons for the enforcement action in its initial communication and that the agency had been granted a forum to lodge disputes. The judge clarified that even in instances of minor procedural non-compliance, it would not automatically warrant a mandatory restoration if the underlying conduct involved systemic policy violations.
The court further emphasized that Meta, as a private entity, is not a government body and operates under its own contractual framework, which it is empowered to enforce to protect its users from fraudulent or harmful content, such as investment scams, deceptive financial advertisements, and inappropriate sexual content found on the agency’s linked accounts.
Liberty to Approach for Restoration Despite dismissing the request for an immediate injunction, the Court provided a narrow window for resolution. The order grants Blink Digital the liberty to approach Meta with a fresh request for the restoration of its accounts, provided the agency offers a formal assurance of strict compliance with Meta’s advertising policies and standards. Meta has been directed to consider any such request and communicate its decision within two weeks of receipt.
To prevent the total loss of business data during this interim period, the court issued a protective order requiring Meta to preserve all data, records, logs, and communications associated with the agency’s accounts until the restoration request is decided. If Blink Digital fails to submit a request for restoration within four weeks, or if Meta ultimately rejects the application after review, this protective data-preservation mandate will lapse. The litigation for the claimed damages remains active, with the court noting that the current order deals solely with the request for interim mandatory restoration of access.
