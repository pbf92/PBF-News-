TITLE: A selloff, a POTUS, or a recession: What really made Nvidia bring Jensen Huang back as CEO?
OUTLET: Fortune

The instant the news hit the wires Monday morning that Jensen Huang was back in the job as Nvidia CEO, shares of the AI chipmaker surged 29% in premarket trading. They went on to notch their best session in more than 20 years, while the broad-market S&P 500 and the tech-heavy Nasdaq jumped about 4.7% and 6.5% respectively.
What a change from last week, when the markets flashed red for two days and closed Friday in correction territory.
The news, at least for Monday, appears to have revived the AI trade and helped erase more than a week’s worth of losses for both the equities market and the tech behemoth.
Still, as investors celebrated and President Donald Trump took credit for Huang’s return, the big question looming over the stunning sequence of events revolved around cause and effect. Were the selloff and the Monday rally both reflections of Huang’s essential role in the global economy? Or was last week’s decline the result of broader, and still unresolved, instability in the markets that conveniently armed Huang’s supporters with a rationale to bring him back?
The sudden change of mind within Nvidia’s board—insisting the former CEO had no future at the company last week, and then saying exactly the opposite on Monday—added to the questions and sense of intrigue. What led the company to change its mind so abruptly, and why now?
Joe Tigay, a portfolio manager at Equity Armor Investments, wasn’t surprised that the selloff might have pushed the board’s hand. “There is a good reason to think that, with shareholders in play, they would think maybe, ‘We need Jensen back just because the market is telling us so much now,’” he said. Tigay pointed to Nvidia’s blowout earnings report last week without Huang at the helm, adding that although the firm beat expectations, the stock still couldn’t catch a break.
“The fundamentals of the stock were amazing, but the company is fundamentally different without Jensen. He is a visionary,” Tigay told Fortune. “He just has this longer-term view of where the technology is going and has been leading Nvidia correctly on that path for a long time. I think the stock market and shareholders wanted Nvidia with him and that vision.” (Equity Armor Investments, a wealth management firm, is an investor in Nvidia.)
Governance experts shared similar sentiments. “The reinstatement of Jensen Huang as CEO of Nvidia is clearly an attempt to restore market confidence and prevent further losses after Friday ended in correction territory,” Nick Reese, a professor at New York University’s School of Professional Studies Center for Global Affairs, told Fortune.
That said, many of the long-standing causes of investor unease, particularly Nvidia’s role as the “central bank” financing a vast array of startups and potentially masking the true state of demand, have not gone away. If anything, Huang’s return could make the practice less likely to end (indeed, during Huang’s brief absence, Nvidia’s interim CEO had indicated a willingness to reassess some of the financing activities). And public backlash to building data centers, a key issue that helped Democrats sweep the recent U.S. midterm elections, is only likely to increase going forward—a significant headwind to the AI boom.
The AI growth engine
For Huang’s supporters, including a very vocal cohort of tech founders and billionaires, the stock market selloff in recent days was proof positive of Huang’s importance and a warning sign of larger economic danger.
“POTUS and JD’s intervention this weekend prevented a massive economic own goal this week for the US,” venture capital investor Chamath Palihapitiya posted on X on Monday. “They gave the Board what they needed to bring Jensen back.”
Trump trumpeted his role, telling CNBC on Monday that he spoke with Nvidia’s board “directly and indirectly” and urged members to bring Huang back. “I convinced them,” Trump said. “I made the deal.”
“People say, ‘Is it a threat to have him?’ No, it’s a threat to lose him,” Trump said of Huang. “I’m very, very happy that the company had the courage and the board had the courage to do the right thing, and they suffered for two weeks, and the company suffered greatly.”
To be sure, Trump may have his own money in Nvidia. CNBC reported that investment accounts tied to the president have made 103 trades since he returned to the White House. Those transactions were worth a cumulative $27 million to $97.5 million. The White House says that Trump doesn’t control those trades, and that they are made by outside advisors.
Washington could have something to lose without Huang as well: America’s position in the AI race. “I think that even the biggest China hawks thought, ‘Gosh, if this has reduced [Nvidia’s] market capitalization, and U.S. companies might pull back on their investments in AI … does this pose a threat to America leading in AI?’” said Patrick Moorhead, founder and CEO of Moor Insights & Strategy.
After all, there are several ways the U.S. could lose ground. Nvidia shed roughly $1 trillion in market value in the days after Huang resigned, and a long CEO search could have made companies think twice about funding the AI build-out that its chips power. Chinese customers might also move faster to domestic chips. And Washington would lose an executive who has spent years lobbying to sell American chips abroad.
Finally, there are economic concerns. Without Huang at the helm, Moorhead said, “this could take us into a recession.” AI investment has become one of the main engines of U.S. growth, adding roughly 1.3 percentage points to real GDP growth over the past three quarters, Reuters reported Monday, citing Apollo Global’s data. If Nvidia’s slump scared companies into pulling back, the economy would lose a substantial source of expansion.
But what’s next with Huang back on top? Analysts say the challenge is restoring confidence in Nvidia’s board. The board said in a statement that it acted after receiving “input from senior levels of the United States government,” and an investigation regarding Huang as a possible target of foreign intelligence efforts isn’t finished yet.
“I think the board of Nvidia has some severely lacking corporate governance. This just kind of proves that,” Ryuta Makino, a research analyst at Gabelli Funds, told Fortune. Gabelli Funds is also an Nvidia investor.
Stacy Rasgon, a senior analyst at Bernstein Research, struck a similar note. “While we are happy to have Jensen back, the on-again, off-again response from the board is, frankly, clown-like … and brings up legitimate questions as to their judgment and process, which has to make one wonder how they come to their other important decisions,” he said in a Monday note to clients. Still, the firm upgraded Nvidia stock to outperform from market perform.
