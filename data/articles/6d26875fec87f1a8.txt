TITLE: Adult Diabetes Risk Scores Overlook Early Dysglycemia in Younger Adults
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Early identification of dysglycemia in young adults is severely undermined by screening frameworks adapted from older populations, according to a scoping review in Frontiers in Endocrinology. Led by Dr. Xiaodan Liu at China-Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University, the systematic review synthesized forty international publications published between 2008 and 2026. The authors found that conventional non-laboratory risk assessment scores and standard biochemical measures consistently underperform when applied to adults aged eighteen to thirty-nine. Young-onset type 2 diabetes follows a more aggressive clinical trajectory than later-onset disease, bringing accelerated microvascular complications, premature renal decline, cardiovascular damage, and elevated early mortality. Despite the urgent need to intervene before overt disease develops, dysglycemia during young adulthood remains largely asymptomatic. Because younger individuals rarely access routine preventive healthcare, prevailing clinical screening guidelines frequently fail to detect those experiencing early metabolic dysfunction.
To map existing risk assessment tools, prediction models, laboratory strategies, and implementation gaps, the investigators conducted a scoping review following Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and PRISMA scoping review guidance. The protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework. Searches were completed across ten electronic databases from inception through August 2026 without language restrictions, spanning PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, SinoMed, Wanfang Data, and the VIP Database. Two reviewers independently screened records, extracted study characteristics, and appraised quality using QUADAS-3 for diagnostic accuracy, PROBAST+AI for multivariable prediction models, and validated JBI critical appraisal instruments for observational designs. Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the forty included publications—originating primarily from India, the United States, Canada, Türkiye, China, and South Korea—findings were synthesized through a structured descriptive narrative.
Non-laboratory risk questionnaires represented the most common detection approach, led by FINDRISC in nine studies, the Indian Diabetes Risk Score in six, CANRISK in five, and AUSDRISK in three. However, applying conventional score cutoffs established in older populations produced poor sensitivity among younger adults. In Canadian validation cohorts of South Asian adults under forty, the standard CANRISK cutoff of 33 yielded a sensitivity of only 32.7 percent alongside 91.9 percent specificity. Lowering this decision threshold to 21 increased sensitivity to 76.9 percent, while specificity dropped to 53.4 percent. A similar trade-off emerged among young First Nations and Métis adults, where sensitivity rose from 26.7 percent at the standard cutoff to 77.2 percent at 21, with specificity declining from 86.8 percent to 43.8 percent. Subsequent Canadian evaluations by Srugo and Cottagiri confirmed that recalibrating CANRISK to a cutoff of 19 optimized detection in general and African Caribbean Black young adults.
Efforts to engineer specialized young-adult questionnaires showed mixed advantages over recalibrated adult instruments, while exposing the structural flaws of existing scoring systems. In United States survey data analyzed by Srugo and colleagues, sex-specific young-adult models outperformed the American Diabetes Association Diabetes Risk Test; among young men, the custom model achieved an area under the curve of 0.69 with 71.3 percent sensitivity, compared to an area of 0.61 and 6.7 percent sensitivity for the ADA test. Among young women, the custom model reached an area of 0.92 and 80.1 percent sensitivity, whereas the ADA tool achieved 0.71 and only 2.8 percent sensitivity. In Yemeni medical students, Al-Shami found FINDRISC discriminated better than IDRS, with areas of 0.782 and 0.671 respectively. Furthermore, studies by Islamoglu and Tejashwini noted that age components awarded zero points to young participants, compressing score variance. Across Syrian university students, Khaloof reported poor inter-tool agreement, with high-risk classification varying between 7.6 percent and 18.1 percent.
Longitudinal investigations evaluating models forecasting incident type 2 diabetes or progression to prediabetes illustrated the boundary between diagnostic case finding and prognostic risk estimation. In the CARDIA cohort, 2,439 United States adults aged eighteen to thirty were followed prospectively for 23.9 years, during which 215 developed type 2 diabetes. While a comprehensive clinical prediction model achieved a C statistic of 0.824, incorporating a 38-variant genotype risk score increased discrimination only to 0.829, providing no meaningful clinical improvement. In rural China, Zhao and colleagues tracked young adults for 6.01 years, demonstrating that the New Chinese Diabetes Risk Score achieved an area of 0.751, but optimal classification required lowering the decision cutoff from the standard adult score of 25 down to 18. Similarly, a five-year prediction model developed by Yan in 590 young American Indian adults achieved an area of 0.68, retaining adiposity, parental diabetes, alcohol intake, and high-normal fasting glucose as predictors.
Advanced computational algorithms and machine learning approaches expanded prognostic options but frequently struggled with calibration, severe class imbalance, and transportability. In a health-examination cohort of 103,693 young Chinese adults with normal baseline fasting glucose studied by Xu and coworkers, logistic regression and XGBoost achieved receiver operating characteristic areas of 0.812 and 0.817 over 2.99 years of follow-up. However, because only 266 individuals developed incident diabetes, the corresponding precision-recall areas fell to 0.017 and 0.009, reflecting severe positive predictive limitations. In Taiwan, Yang tracked 6,247 young men over 5.9 years, where an XGBoost classifier predicting future prediabetes reached an area of 0.667. Cross-sectional machine learning evaluations in South Korea by Heo demonstrated test-set areas of 0.54 for logistic regression, 0.49 for random forest, and 0.47 for k-nearest neighbors despite apparent accuracy above eighty percent. Critical appraisals under PROBAST+AI revealed that internal validation splits predominated, while external calibration and clinical utility assessments were almost universally lacking.
A central finding of the scoping review is that common laboratory biomarkers—specifically glycated hemoglobin, fasting plasma glucose, and the two-hour oral glucose tolerance test—identify non-identical biological subsets and cannot be treated interchangeably. In an evaluation of 116 young Indian adults by Hardikar and coworkers, prediabetes prevalence was measured at 7.8 percent via oral glucose tolerance testing compared to 23.3 percent by glycated hemoglobin criteria. When evaluated against the oral glucose tolerance standard, glycated hemoglobin exhibited a sensitivity of 50.0 percent, specificity of 77 percent, a positive predictive value of 20.0 percent, and a negative predictive value of 93.0 percent. In Toronto, Alamgir observed that prediabetes prevalence among African Caribbean Black young adults varied between 9.2 percent by glycated hemoglobin, 2.6 percent by fasting glucose, and 7.0 percent by post-load glucose.
Systemic gaps in established screening policies exclude substantial proportions of young adults who exhibit biochemical dysglycemia. Analyzing National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data from 2007 through 2016, Tang and colleagues established that 32.7 percent of United States adults aged eighteen to thirty-four with biochemically confirmed prediabetes did not qualify for screening under standard American Diabetes Association guidelines. Among individuals aged eighteen to twenty-four, the proportion excluded by formal eligibility criteria reached 38.1 percent. Even when individuals qualify based on documented risk profiles, real-world clinical implementation remains inadequate. In a retrospective electronic health record review conducted by Joy involving high-risk racial and ethnic minority outpatients aged eighteen to thirty-nine with obesity and family histories of diabetes, fewer than 14 percent underwent documented clinical screening under routine practice, although 36.7 percent received glycated hemoglobin tests. These findings demonstrate that rigid age thresholds and fragmented outpatient protocols create persistent barriers preventing timely metabolic assessment during early adult life.
The consequences of fragmented screening appear vividly in the heavy burden of unrecognized metabolic disease documented across global young-adult cohorts. In a nationwide health survey in Indonesia evaluated by Ferdina and colleagues, 91.2 percent of all diabetes cases identified among adults aged thirty to thirty-nine were entirely undiagnosed. Similarly, population-based testing conducted by Zargar in Kashmir revealed that known diabetes had a prevalence of 0.3 percent, whereas systematic community screening uncovered previously undiagnosed diabetes in 2.5 percent of participants aged twenty to forty, representing roughly ten unrecognized cases for every known patient. Diagnostic definitions also altered screening yield, with impaired fasting glucose prevalence shifting from 11.9 percent to 26.7 percent when the diagnostic cutoff dropped from 110 mg/dL to 100 mg/dL. In sequential screening initiatives, Jabbar demonstrated that initial capillary glucose triage identified 0.48 percent diabetes and 2.4 percent prediabetes in Indian communities, while Nagarathna observed 7.2 percent diabetes and 18.6 percent prediabetes in questionnaire-selected high-risk individuals.
Methodological appraisal across the forty included publications identified widespread study limitations, including non-probability sampling, convenience recruitment from universities, selective biochemical verification restricted to screen-positive participants, and inconsistent definitions of young adulthood ranging from eighteen to forty-five years. Reviewing these constraints, Liu and colleagues concluded that while adult-derived risk tools are not inherently unsuited for young adults, they require age-specific recalibration and rigorous independent external validation within target populations. Given the marked biological discordance among diagnostic blood tests, the authors emphasized that future clinical pathways should pair validated risk assessment tools with laboratory tests chosen deliberately for specific clinical purposes and ethnic backgrounds. Staged screening combining sensitive questionnaires with confirmatory testing offers a practical approach in resource-constrained environments, whereas broader direct biochemical testing may prove necessary in high-risk populations. Rather than generating additional isolated prediction models, future research must evaluate real-world screening workflows, diagnostic yield, false-positive burdens, overall cost-effectiveness, and sustainable linkage to lifestyle and therapeutic interventions that alter the long-term metabolic health of young adults.
