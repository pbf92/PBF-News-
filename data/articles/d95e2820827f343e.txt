TITLE: Did Cash Payments Really Cut Newborn Deaths in Half?
OUTLET: City Journal

A recent study published in JAMA Pediatrics is attracting national attention after touting the remarkable benefits of a direct cash transfer program to pregnant women in Flint, Michigan. The study investigates the effects of Rx Kids, a Michigan-based, largely taxpayer-funded program for new and pregnant mothers during the first year of their child’s life. The study’s results are eye-catching. After the implementation of Rx Kids, neonatal mortality in Flint dropped by 50 percent. Deaths within the first week of life fell by nearly 75 percent.
But the study has just one problem: it does not prove that these cash payments reduced neonatal mortality.

Launched in January 2024, Rx Kids provides pregnant mothers with up to $7,500 in cash as long as they reside in an eligible zip code. Mothers receive $1,500 during pregnancy, followed by $500 per month during their child’s first year of life. The program’s advocates claim that these payments reduce financial hardship for new mothers and promote healthier pregnancies through improved nutrition, housing stability, and access to prenatal care.
The study researchers examined Michigan birth and death records from 2021 through 2025, analyzing both Flint, where mothers were eligible for program enrollment, and the rest of the state. The authors reported on two primary outcomes: 1) neonatal mortality (NM), death within the first 28 days of life; and 2) early neonatal mortality (ENM), death within the first seven days of life.
With a closer look at the study’s graphs, a keen reader will notice where the authors did some clever work. Both panels of the study’s figure depict NM and ENM by plotting out these five years of data with separate trend lines for 2021–2024 and 2024–2025. The latter trend line’s slope is steeply negative, while the 2021–2024 line has a more modest slope, giving readers the impression that the introduction of Rx Kids caused a dramatic drop in deaths. But treat the earlier cohort in the same manner as the 2024–2025 cohort—that is, draw direct lines connecting a single year’s worth of data to the prior—and a different picture emerges.
Disaggregated, a reader can see that between 2022 and 2023, neonatal mortality had already fallen by approximately 38 percent, comparable to the roughly 45 percent decline between 2024 and 2025. Yet nowhere in the study is this earlier decline even mentioned. Doing so might suggest that the decline seen after program implementation may have been the result of year-to-year variability in neonatal deaths, a metric prone to high variance given the relatively low number of deaths at baseline.
Unsurprisingly, the study’s statistical analysis does not test whether the difference between the pooled 2021–2024 period and 2025 is statistically significant. Using a two-sided Fisher exact test and the study’s published data of the deaths in each of these groups, a p-value of 0.19 is found, which is well above the typical p-value threshold of 0.05 needed for statistical significance.
Another curious inconsistency arises when looking at how the authors handled pre- and post-intervention cohort classification. As mentioned previously, Rx Kids was launched in January 2024, yet that year is included in the study’s analysis as a pre-intervention year. The authors’ rationale appears to be that women who gave birth early in 2024 had too little time in the program for any benefit to emerge. But eligible enrollment was 98 percent, meaning most new Flint mothers had some degree of exposure to Rx Kids’ benefits. Never mind that in 2024, Flint’s neonatal mortality rose from the year prior. Even partial exposure to program benefits should not have resulted in an increase in mortality. If the authors had included 2024 as a post-intervention year, the headline 50 percent reduction in neonatal mortality would have been reduced to approximately a quarter to a third, based on rates reconstructed from the published figure.
My analysis of the study’s data relies in part on estimates because the paper does not provide the year-to-year birth and death counts used to construct its figures. Typically, such data is included in a study’s supplemental materials, yet the data sharing-statement reveals the data must be requested from the state health department.
A look at the study’s authors makes transparency a greater concern. Lead author Mona Hanna founded and directs Rx Kids, and the study’s senior author, Luke Shaefer, acts as its co-director. Hanna’s employer, Michigan State University, leads the program and receives millions in state funding. None of these ties to Rx Kids appears in the study’s conflict of interest disclosures. The study itself was partially funded by the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services, which reviewed the manuscript prior to publication. Something doesn’t sit right with a public program’s effectiveness being studied by those who run it, reviewed by the entity that funds it, and an author’s employer receiving millions in program funds at taxpayers’ expense.
Rx Kids is no longer an isolated experiment. It is now expanding to more than 60 Michigan communities, with a state budget of $270 million over three years. Unless studies such as this get critical scrutiny, proponents of Rx Kids will continue to enjoy the façade of academic legitimacy needed to expand the program to a national level.
