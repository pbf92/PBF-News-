TITLE: Anthropic mathematician claims to have solved 167-year-old Riemann Hypothesis using Claude AI
OUTLET: The Indian Express

A mathematician working at artificial intelligence company Anthropic has claimed to have proved the 167-year-old Riemann Hypothesis, one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics, with the help of the company’s AI model Claude, reported Scientific American.
Levent Alpöge said he had discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis while working with Claude, and that the method also extended to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, a much broader mathematical conjecture.
“I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis,” Alpöge wrote in a statement posted on his personal website on Friday.
The claim has not yet been independently verified by the mathematical community. Alpöge has made the purported proof available as code written in Lean, a programming language used to formalise mathematical arguments and check their logical correctness.
Scientific American described the Riemann Hypothesis as one of the most famous open problems in mathematics. Technology publication OfficeChai also reported that the proof had been released in 14 parts and that independent verification was still pending.
Alpöge said the formalisation of the argument was complete but acknowledged that the proof was extremely long and that he had not yet produced a readable mathematical paper explaining it.
What is the Riemann Hypothesis?
First proposed by German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in 1859, the hypothesis concerns the distribution of prime numbers, numbers that can be divided only by themselves and one.
Although prime numbers appear to occur irregularly along the number line, mathematicians have established patterns governing their distribution. The Riemann Hypothesis proposes a deeper relationship between these patterns and the zeros of a mathematical function known as the Riemann zeta function.
It also states that all the non-trivial zeros of the function have a real part of exactly one-half.
Proving the hypothesis would provide a much more precise understanding of how prime numbers are distributed. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems identified by the Clay Mathematics Institute, which offers a $1 million prize for a correct solution.
The institute’s website continued to list the Riemann Hypothesis as unsolved on Friday.
How did Claude help with the purported proof?
In his statement, Alpöge described the work as a collaboration in which he guided the research while Claude supplied several crucial mathematical ideas.
He said the breakthrough came from combining two different approaches in number theory. One of the central ideas involved adapting a technique known as Stepanov’s method, previously used to address an analogous version of the Riemann Hypothesis in the mathematical setting of function fields.
The challenge was to find a way to reproduce the role of multiplicity in ordinary number theory, where the conventional technique could not be directly applied.
Alpöge said Claude suggested using a sequence of mathematical operations known as theta lifts to address this difficulty. He described the AI model as having substantially outpaced him towards the end of the work.
“Tens of such occurred,” he wrote, describing the number of occasions when Claude came up with unexpected mathematical steps.
He also acknowledged that parts of the argument were beyond his own technical expertise and that the proof would need to be simplified and explained in a form that other mathematicians could understand.
The purported proof has been formalised in Lean, a proof assistant that allows mathematical arguments to be checked step by step by a computer.
A year of rapid progress in AI-assisted mathematics
The announcement comes after a series of developments involving Alpöge and Claude that have attracted attention in the mathematical community.
In August, Anthropic reported that an unreleased research version of Claude had improved a longstanding lower bound on the proportion of zeros of the Riemann zeta function lying on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%. That was progress on a related question, not a solution to the Riemann Hypothesis.
In September, Scientific American reported on Alpöge’s AI-assisted work on the 1947 Hopf problem concerning the existence of a complex structure on a six-dimensional sphere. The publication reported that mathematicians had begun reconstructing and examining the argument.
