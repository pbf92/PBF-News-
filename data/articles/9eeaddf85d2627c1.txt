TITLE: Mark Williams-Cook's joke cats.txt passed four tests used to sell llms.txt
OUTLET: PPC Land

Mark Williams-Cook, a director at the UK search agency Candour, published an essay on August 4, 2026, describing how a deliberately absurd web standard for declaring office cats cleared every piece of evidence that marketers routinely present for llms.txt. The file, cats.txt, was fetched by AI crawlers, indexed by Google, quoted as fact in a Google AI Overview and recommended by ChatGPT as a ranking tactic. His conclusion, set out in the essay titled How cats.txt Showed llms.txt Evidence is GEO Astrology, is that none of those four observations shows anything about whether a file influences AI systems, and that the same reasoning props up much of what is sold as generative engine optimization.
In Short
An SEO invented a fake file standard about office cats, and AI crawlers, Google Search, Google's AI Overviews and ChatGPT treated it exactly the way people say they treat llms.txt. His argument is that crawling, indexing, being quoted by an AI and being endorsed by a chatbot happen to almost any text file on the open web, so none of them proves a file does anything. For anyone paying for or selling llms.txt and similar GEO work, the essay says the evidence usually offered does not show the file has any effect.
A file about cats, written to look like a standard
The essay opens with irritation rather than data. "It began, as these things tend to, with irritation," Williams-Cook writes. For months, he says, he had watched "perfectly sensible people" point to four observations - the bots crawled it, Google indexed it, an LLM repeated it, ChatGPT endorsed it - and present them "in decks and threads and client proposals" as proof that llms.txt was reshaping AI search.
Counter-arguments were not landing. "I wanted something they couldn't nod past," he writes. So he wrote a specification for cats.txt, a plain-text file placed at the root of a domain "to formally declare the cats associated with your website; their names, their job titles, their breeds, and a mandatory affection metric called PurrLevel, scored out of ten."
The cats.txt draft specification on his own site is dated September 18, 2025, and is titled "Draft v1.0". He followed it with a LinkedIn article introducing cats.txt as "the missing standard for SEO and GEO", chosen, he says, because LinkedIn is a platform LLMs "seem to hold in unaccountably high regard." The intent was plain enough. "The idea was to seed the internet with just enough earnest-sounding text that the machines would start treating my cats as real. What I did not fully anticipate was that people would join in."
Join in they did. Dave Smart, a technical SEO who runs the Tame the Bots site, added a cats.txt file to tamethebots.com and became what the essay calls "an early adopter of a standard I had built to be nonsense." Then someone set up catstxt.org, described by Williams-Cook as "a cleaner, better-organised, altogether more competently specified version of the standard." His verdict on the episode: "My daft blog post had acquired a rival implementation, which is more than most real standards manage in their first fortnight."
The two versions do not agree on the basics, which is itself a fair imitation of how real web proposals tend to fork.
There is a small internal inconsistency worth recording. The essay calls PurrLevel "mandatory", while the draft specification lists it among optional fields, with only Name, Role and Description required.
How the test was run
According to the essay, the experiment followed five steps, each designed to reproduce one of the claims made for llms.txt.
- Publish a specification. Williams-Cook wrote the cats.txt draft "with the earnest, over-engineered tone of a real proposal" and posted it on his blog.
- Seed the discourse. He published the LinkedIn article presenting cats.txt as a standard for SEO and GEO and explaining, "with a straight face," why sites should adopt it.
- Let others deploy it. Dave Smart placed a cats.txt file on tamethebots.com, and catstxt.org published its own version, which places the file in the /.well-known/ directory.
- Check the four "proofs". With the files live, Williams-Cook checked cats.txt against server logs, Google's index, Google AI Overviews and ChatGPT.
- Ask again later. He returned to ChatGPT after the joke had been widely discussed and reports how its answer changed.
The four proofs, one at a time
Williams-Cook lists the arguments "in ascending order of confidence and descending order of rigour." His central claim about all four is stated in one sentence: "The trap is this: getting baited into treating a set of observations as evidence, when the observations would occur whether or not the underlying thing were true."
"It's definitely used, the LLM bots crawl it!"
The first argument holds that if Anthropic, OpenAI and other AI companies show up in server logs requesting the file, the file is being used. Williams-Cook rejects the inference. "A crawler fetching a file tells you nothing about whether the contents are read, weighted, trusted or acted upon," he writes. "Fetching things is the entire job description of a crawler." His analogy is domestic: "the postman touching your gate is not an endorsement of the contents of your bins."
After cats.txt went up, he says, he "watched the logs fill with PerplexityBot, GPTBot, ClaudeBot, Googlebot and a supporting cast of lesser crawlers." His tone is dry: "By this standard, the major AI labs have all quietly decided to support my cats. I am, frankly, touched."
The log excerpt published with the essay, captioned "The cats.txt server logs", is filtered for cats.txt and llms.txt paths and runs from 23:10 on August 3, 2026, to 00:37 on August 4 (UTC+1). By PPC Land's count it holds about 70 requests in under 90 minutes. Most are for /.well-known/cats.txt, the location used by catstxt.org, with some for /cats.txt and /llms.txt. The user agents include GPTBot, ChatGPT-User, ClaudeBot, Claude-User, PerplexityBot, Perplexity-User, Googlebot, GoogleOther, Google's page renderer, bingbot, Amazonbot, Meta's meta-externalagent, YandexBot, AhrefsBot, SemrushBot, Diffbot and several security scanners. catstxt.org offers a filtered log viewer that shows such requests as they arrive.
"It was indexed by Google, so it must matter!"
The second argument treats indexing as a sign that Google considers a file important. "Google indexes text files," Williams-Cook writes. "It has done so, enthusiastically, since before most of the people currently selling llms.txt owned a smartphone." In his account, "Being in the index is a statement that a URL exists and contains words. It is not a verdict on truth, usefulness or sanity."
A site: search for tamethebots.com/cats.txt returns the file, topped by Google's prompt to try Search Console. Google, the essay notes, will offer to let the owner "get indexing and ranking data," for a file asserting that a British Shorthair named Pixel works as a "GUI Purrfectionist" with a PurrLevel of 8.
"ChatGPT returned information that was only in my llms.txt file"
This is the argument Williams-Cook considers "the strongest-looking." The claim is that when a model produces a fact that exists only in an llms.txt file, it must have read that file as a special, trusted source. His reply is that such an outcome "is exactly what you would expect from ordinary retrieval-augmented generation." The model runs a search, lands on a page that ranks because it is indexed, and reads it. "If the page that ranks is your llms.txt, the model reads your llms.txt, no differently from any other URL. That is the file functioning as a web page, not as a standard."
Dave Smart's cat supplies the demonstration. Asked about the cat on his site, Google's AI Overview stated, in a bulleted answer, that Odd is a "Render Cat," a Tuxedo with a PurrLevel of 5/7, who "chases the cursor, pounces on stray pixels, and stashes them on the digital carpet." The answer cited the cats.txt file. "Every word is invented, sourced from a file the model was never designed to revere, surfaced through the same grounding it applies to everything else."
The episode is not the first of its kind. In December 2025, Ahrefs invented a paperweight maker called Xarumei and tested eight AI platforms on 56 questions, finding that several trusted fabricated details over the brand's own FAQ. Lily Ray has documented a fake core-update post reaching AI Overviews within 24 hours in January 2026. Back in April 2025, Google's AI Overview described Ray herself as a 9-year-old dog. Williams-Cook's point differs slightly from those cases: he is not arguing that the AI Overview was tricked, but that it behaved normally, which is why its output cannot serve as proof of a special mechanism.
"ChatGPT itself says llms.txt helps!"
The fourth argument he calls "the cloudy summit of Mt. Stupid": asking ChatGPT whether llms.txt works and taking a yes as confirmation. "A language model telling you something is a good idea is not evidence that it is a good idea," he writes. "Confidence is the product. It is not the proof."
Roughly two weeks after launch, according to the essay, the question "Can cats.txt help me rank in search or LLMs?" produced: "Yes - cats.txt can potentially help you rank in both search engines and LLM-driven systems." ChatGPT went on about "structured signals for machines," about "better understanding → better visibility," and about how cats.txt "could help them trust, summarize, and cite your content more accurately." Williams-Cook's comment: "That is, word for word, the pitch made for llms.txt delivered on behalf of a file about how much my cats enjoy being stroked."
The convergence problem
The ChatGPT answer leads to what the essay treats as the mechanism beneath all four proofs, which Williams-Cook calls the convergence problem, a theme from an earlier post of his. "When you ask a model whether llms.txt helps, it is not reasoning," he writes. "It is returning the most common thing it has seen written on the subject." Because the web is full of confident posts calling llms.txt the future, the model "converges on that consensus and reflects it back, dressed as a considered opinion." It backed cats.txt for the same reason: by the time anyone asked, enough people had written enthusiastically about it.
Then the answer flipped. "Ask ChatGPT about cats.txt today and it will inform you that it is a joke; a satirical file made by an SEO to prove a point. Nothing about the file changed," he writes, as of August 2026. What changed was the surrounding text. "That is not evidence. It is an echo with a good vocabulary."
What the essay grants llms.txt
Williams-Cook frames the essay as being about reasoning, not about llms.txt itself. "I am not claiming llms.txt will never work, this is not the point, dear reader. I am claiming the bar of evidence currently being used to sell it is so low that a file about a Tuxedo cat called Odd cleared it without breaking stride." He adds: "And that same faulty thinking is being applied to half the GEO tactics currently being invoiced to clients."
He sets aside, for argument's sake, two facts he calls inconvenient. "The first is that no large language model provider has ever documented using llms.txt for search or discovery." That covers OpenAI, Google and Anthropic, which, he notes, publishes an llms.txt for its own documentation but has never said its models read such files during a conversation. He quotes Google's John Mueller:
"FWIW no AI system currently uses llms.txt, [..] It's super-obvious if you look at your server logs. The consumer LLMs / chatbots (the ones that SEOs want traffic from) will fetch your pages - for training and grounding, but none of them fetch the llms.txt file. Maybe they will tomorrow? Maybe I'll win in the lottery tomorrow?"
