Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, and Tristan Buckmaster, a professor at New York University, posted a paper on Nov. 20 claiming to prove that the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe how fluids such as water and air move, can break down in finite time [1][2]. The 79-page paper, "Finite-time blow-up for the Navier-Stokes equations," says there are smooth starting flows, both in open space and on a periodic box, whose solutions blow up without any outside force stirring the fluid [1].
If it holds, it would settle the version of the Navier-Stokes question that the Clay Mathematics Institute lists as one of its seven Millennium Prize problems. The paper itself says it disproves statements (A) and (B) of the problem as formulated by Charles Fefferman, and hence proves (C) and (D) [1]. No outside mathematician has verified it yet [3]. The paper came out the same day Alpöge posted a separate, Claude-assisted claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis, which we covered here [3].
What blowup means
The Navier-Stokes equations have been used for nearly two centuries to model flows, but mathematicians have never shown whether smooth solutions in three dimensions always stay smooth. "Blowup" means a solution that starts out perfectly regular develops a singularity: the fluid's velocity, or its gradients, become infinite at some point after a finite time. Real fluids do not do this, which is why a proof that the equations allow it matters to both mathematicians and physicists [3].
Alpöge and Buckmaster's construction finds a "discretely self-similar" solution: a flow pattern that repeats itself at ever smaller scales, up to a rotation, as it collapses toward a point. They show that such a profile exists near an approximate solution that satisfies explicit inequalities, which they call a certificate, and that the blowup is stable enough to be produced from smooth, compactly supported starting data [1]. The paper gives the blowup rate as (T - t)^(-1/4) in the relevant energy norm [1].
The OpenAI loophole
In September, OpenAI announced its own blowup proof. According to Scientific American, that construction used an unrealistic, finely tuned external force to stir the fluid toward infinite velocity, which many experts considered a loophole in the problem's wording [3]. The new paper is explicitly about the equations "without external force" [1].
The race has been bitter. Scientific American reports that Buckmaster has alleged OpenAI deliberately tried to scoop his work and suggested it may have used his user data; OpenAI denied any foul play [3]. Buckmaster said the write-up was unpolished because the pair "couldn't risk waiting" [3].
How Claude was used
The paper's AI statement reads: "We used Anthropic models (Claude) throughout this work, and no others" [1]. The authors say Claude agents were given a curated dossier of leads, building on the pair's unreleased work on forced versions of the equations, and that a simpler mechanism first produced blowup for a heavily modified (hyperdissipative) equation before "a heavier computation produced this profile" [1]. The written proof was drafted from an initial Lean proof and then rewritten with Claude under the authors' direction [1].
What is verified, and what is not
The paper says its two main theorems were formalized in the Lean 4 proof assistant with the Mathlib library, in the form in which an earlier version stated them [1]. The public GitHub repository holds the paper and a Lean package of about 2,270 files and 600,000 lines; it notes that a Nov. 20 patch to some statements was not rebuilt when the repository was assembled [2].
Two pieces are missing. The certificate, the approximate solution the whole argument depends on, has not been released; the paper publishes only its hash and says it "will be released soon" [1][2]. And the check that the certificate meets the required conditions is a separate interval-arithmetic computation that is not part of the Lean formalization [1]. Scientific American reported no independent review of the claim [3].
What to watch: release of the certificate and the computation, a full Lean build by outside groups, and reaction from fluid-dynamics specialists. Clay's rules require a solution to be published and accepted by the community before any prize is considered.
