TITLE: Google publishes its search ranking principles 23 days before a UK deadline
OUTLET: PPC Land

On November 24, 2026, Google published a page called "Our principles for ranking results" on its Search Central documentation site, stating that every kind of search result, from a plain web link to an AI-written summary, is judged against the same test of usefulness. A second new page, "Attribution and factuality in Search generative AI features," sets out how Google's AI answers pick, check and display the web pages they cite. Both pages carry a last-updated date of November 24, 2026 (UTC), and both went live on the same morning that Google posted advance notice of a broad ranking update scheduled for December 2026.
In Short
Google published new pages on November 24 that explain how it decides which results appear first, and it says its AI answers are held to the same usefulness test as ordinary links. Anyone who relies on Google for visitors is affected, because the pages also describe how AI answers choose the websites they credit and what a site owner can do when the credit goes to the wrong place. Google also said a broad ranking update will arrive in December 2026, without giving a date. The timing lines up with UK rules that, from December 17, 2026, require Google to explain how it ranks results and to warn site owners before major changes.
Three principles, in Google's words
The principles page is short: three numbered sections and a closing statement. Its opening makes the central claim. "Earning and maintaining the trust of those who search with us is essential," the page reads. "This means that we treat all results equally based on their helpfulness for the query. We don't design our ranking systems or AI models to favor any specific site's interests."
The page then says the same principles apply to "all the parts of our system for ranking and organizing content - from classic ranking systems to deep learning models to generative AI."
The second principle carries most of the weight. Google defines the yardstick in one sentence: "All of our systems evaluate and rank information based on utility, which we define as how useful a result is for the query." It then spells out what that covers. "This means that web page listings and other results like AI Overviews, carousels, images, and videos are evaluated using the same criteria."
In plain terms, Google is saying that its own AI summary at the top of a results page does not get a head start over a publisher's article. The summary has to earn its spot the same way.
Who decides whether something is useful? According to the page, Google measures helpfulness "according to the criteria outlined in our Search Quality Rater Guidelines," the public manual used by outside contractors who score search results. Those raters, the page says, help Google "assess how well our systems are working." The page also places AI answers inside the same frame: "the sources surfaced within AI Overviews and AI Mode are designed to be as helpful as possible for people in the context of their questions."
Placement on the page is automated as well, according to Google. "Our automated systems determine if a particular result or feature (like a map, a web result, or an AI Overview) makes sense to display, and where on the page it should appear, in terms of usefulness to the user."
Where people step in
One sentence limits human intervention. "We don't make manual adjustments to alter search rankings other than to comply with legal obligations or apply our clearly defined Content Policies for Search," the page says. "When content violates these policies, we take appropriate action."
The third principle describes testing. Google lists two methods: "Testing for usefulness," which measures "the overall helpfulness of the Search results page, across all different result types," and "Evaluating the quality of results," which uses the rater guidelines "to define and describe our standards for helpful, reliable information." The page says Google aligns its evaluations with those external guidelines "so that our goals can be clear, transparent, and publicly accountable."
The closing section adds a commitment. "We are committed to anchoring every change to Search in a review by our most experienced engineers and data scientists, and we maintain a strict standard that if we can't show a change actually makes things better for people, we don't launch it."
What the page does not give is a number. It names no measure of "utility," no threshold a change must clear, and no figure for how many changes are tested or rejected.
The honest results policy
The principles page links twice to a separate page titled "Our Honest Results Policy," which sits on Google's "How Search works" site rather than in the developer documentation. That page carries no date. It describes the policy as "a long-standing principle of Google Search that we do not provide special treatment based on having some type of relationship with Google."
The page names who is covered: "Advertisers, business partners, clients of our non-Search services, friends and family of those who work at Google - everyone is treated the same." It then lists four commitments:
- "We don't charge anyone to appear in our search index."
- "Advertisers get no ranking boost for their web listings and ads are always clearly labeled."
- "Anyone who wants support from Google Search must use the same free tools and resources that we make publicly available to everyone."
- "The SEO teams responsible for various Google, YouTube and Alphabet sites get no special insight to our ranking systems and must follow the same external guidance provided to everyone."
The last line is the most specific. It says the people who handle search visibility for Google's own properties, including YouTube, work from the same public documentation as any outside publisher. For advertisers, the second line restates a long-held position in writing: spending on Google Ads does not lift a site's unpaid listings.
The question of favoritism has come up before. In August, Google told The Verge that Reddit receives no special preference in ranking or in AI search, after Reddit's own results raised concerns about referral traffic.
How AI answers choose their sources
The attribution page opens with the purpose of the links inside AI answers: they give "people a jumping-off point to learn more from publishers and creators across the web." It then explains the model behind the feature. "Instead of relying only on what the model learned during training, Search generative AI features use a customized Gemini model integrated directly with Google's core web ranking, information retrieval, and quality systems to ensure that responses are factual and based on the latest information from high-quality pages from the Google Search index."
According to Google, the process has four steps:
- Results from the Google Search index. The index "is built by crawling the web and contains various signals about topicality, freshness, and more." For AI Overviews, "the first step is to examine the ranked results returned to make sure they're relevant to the user's search query; snippets from these articles are provided to the model as a basis for the overview." For Google Discover, the page says underlying web content helps identify "fresh, relevant, high-quality information for the current user based on their interests."
- Related searches. "Based on the user's query, the model may also run one or more related searches (also known as "query fan-out") across the Google Search index to retrieve high-quality web pages that address different parts of the user's initial search query." In everyday terms, one question can trigger several behind-the-scenes searches.
- Grounding and corroboration. "The model synthesizes the response using the web results it found for grounding the AI response, and then cites and links to the pages that support the information in the response (also known as "corroboration")."
- Display. Links appear "where they're most helpful to people as they explore a topic," using "inline link icons, grouped source cards, and expandable link carousels showing site names, favicons, and article titles." To pick which links to show, the systems "balance relevance and quality signals from Search ranking with a clear layout on desktop and mobile and a diverse set of helpful sources."
The third step is the one publishers tend to watch. Grounding means building an answer from pages retrieved at the moment of the question, instead of from what a model memorized in training. Corroboration is the second half: after the answer is written, the system links to pages that back it up. The page does not say whether the pages used to write an answer and the pages shown as links are always the same set.
The checks Google says it runs
The page lists checks at three stages: before an answer is shown, during ongoing evaluation, and after a problem is found.
The first row adds a layer the principles page does not mention. Google says its systems "have an even higher bar for quality for sensitive topics," with the examples of "sensitive Your Money or Your Life topics, or breaking news with limited coverage." Your Money or Your Life is Google's term for subjects such as health, money and safety, where a wrong answer can do real harm. So AI answers are judged by the same usefulness standard as everything else, according to Google, but on these topics an AI answer may simply not appear.
In the A/B testing row, Google also says human "evaluators assess samples of Search generative AI responses and their attributed links to verify that cited web pages accurately corroborate the synthesized text and provide useful context to users." The page gives no sample size, no benchmark names and no accuracy rate.
That absence matters, because outside tests have produced numbers Google has not answered here. A WordStream study from July 2025 found that AI Overviews gave inaccurate answers to PPC strategy questions 26% of the time, the highest error rate among the tools it tested. In February, recipe publishers Inspired Taste showed how Google's AI Mode produced a key lime pie recipe that doubled the condensed milk and dropped other ingredients from their original.
What the page tells site owners
The attribution page closes with a section titled "Best practices for accurate attribution." It argues that, because the AI features are built on top of Google's ranking systems, "the same practices that help your site in Search can help improve the chances that your content is linked to and represented accurately." According to the page, there are four parts:
- Eligibility. "Only pages that are indexed and eligible to be shown in Search with a snippet, meeting the technical requirements, can be shown as supporting links. Your site must also be included in Search generative AI features in Search Console." That second sentence refers to the setting Google added in 2026 that lets a site opt out of AI Overviews and AI Mode.
- Helpful, reliable content. The page points to clear sourcing and "background about the author or your site (for example, through author pages or an About page)."
- Accuracy and freshness. Pages should be checked for factual errors and updated when information changes. One line is direct: "Avoid changing a page's date unless the content has substantially changed."
- Visibility tracking. The page points to the Generative AI performance report in Search Console, which Google introduced in June.
