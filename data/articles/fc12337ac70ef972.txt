TITLE: YouTube puts Ask YouTube AI search on TVs and gaming consoles
OUTLET: PPC Land

YouTube today published its quarterly account of the changes made to its app on televisions, listing 12 features rolled out between July and September 2026. The most consequential for marketers may be the least visual. Ask YouTube, the conversational search tool the company described in July as a desktop-only feature for signed-in users in the United States, now runs on TVs and gaming consoles, answering spoken questions with text guides and clips on the largest screen in the house.
The post, titled "[Q3'26] Quarterly update: New ways to navigate YouTube on your TV!", went up in the YouTube Help Community under the name Dave (TeamYouTube), marked as a Google employee. Its other changes reach into the phone, the remote control and the account itself. A phone signed in to the same account as the TV now connects automatically as a companion, without a shared Wi-Fi network. Comments can be dictated through the remote's microphone. Parents can create a child's profile on the TV screen, and a push notification to the phone can unlock a profile protected by a code.
In Short
YouTube today listed 12 changes it made to its TV app in the third quarter, from AI search on TVs and consoles to dictated comments and phone-based profile unlocking. TV is now the screen where YouTube holds a record share of US viewing, so changes to how people search, comment and sign in there shape how creators are found and which account a TV session belongs to. The post gives no countries, dates or advertising details for most of the features, so the exact reach of each rollout is still unclear.
A recap, not a launch
The post concerns the main YouTube app on televisions, not YouTube TV, the company's paid live television subscription. It is the second quarterly recap of its kind this year. The first-quarter edition, published in May and also signed by Dave of TeamYouTube, covered nine features, including a conversational AI tool on smart TVs, a re-ranked "Top Channels" shelf and family groups on the "Who's Watching" screen. Both followed a redesign of the TV interface announced on December 16, 2025, which regrouped the playback controls into three clusters and made video titles unclickable.
According to the post, the third-quarter work "focused on redesigning layouts, simplifying how you find your favorite creators, adding smarter interaction features, and updating account management tools." Several items had already appeared in separate announcements during the quarter. The recap gathers them alongside smaller interface changes and arrives seven weeks after the quarter closed.
The 12 features fall under four headings in the post.
Channel pages and the Top Channels shelf
Four of the five navigation changes concern channel pages and the paths that lead to them. The formats shelf is a single row that, according to the post, organizes "all of a channel's content (including standard videos, Shorts, live streams, and playlists) in one intuitive, at-a-glance row." The screenshot in the post shows five tiles rather than four: Videos, Shorts, Live, Podcasts and Playlists. The text does not mention podcasts.
The "New video" badge sits on the Top Channels shelf and, according to the post, "highlights unwatched uploads from the last 24 hours." The shelf itself was re-ranked in the first quarter. With the badge, the 24-hour window becomes a visible marker of recency on the home screen, a detail of some weight for channels that publish daily and compete for the same row.
Live streams get the same treatment. Channel pages of creators who are streaming now carry a "prominent Watch Live button along with immersive live stream previews," so a viewer can preview or enter a stream "with a single tap." YouTube had already added immersive channel previews to the TV home page in October 2025, alongside AI upscaling and QR code shopping on tagged videos.
Shorts on the big screen
The fifth item extends two Shorts projects. Shorts series, the first feature YouTube presented at Made on YouTube on September 23, 2026, are now, according to the post, "live globally on TVs, mobile, and the web," so that multi-part stories and episodic series play back-to-back. The format strings episodes of short-form video into ordered sequences, the structure associated with microdramas.
The second project belongs to Google TV rather than to the YouTube app. Google announced a "Short videos for you" row in April, beginning with YouTube Shorts and planned for US Google TV devices during the summer. The post says YouTube has "expanded our dedicated 'Short videos for you' row on Google TV to more countries around the world." It does not name them.
The scale behind both moves is not small. Kurt Wilms, YouTube's senior director of product management for TV, said in June that Shorts are watched for 2 billion hours a month on TV screens, a format built for vertical phone screens now consumed in volume on horizontal ones.
The phone in the room
The TV mobile companion is the change built most directly around the phone. According to the post, "when you stream a video on your TV, if you are signed in to the same account on your phone, your YouTube mobile app will automatically connect as a companion, even without a shared Wi-Fi network." From the phone, a viewer can like videos, subscribe to channels or read comments "without interrupting TV playback."
The Wi-Fi change itself was not new in the recap. YouTube dropped the shared Wi-Fi requirement for phone control of TV playback in a Spanish-language community post on September 2, with signing in to the same account on both devices as the only stated condition. The companion concept dates from earlier in the year: Wilms described it in June while citing third-party research that nine out of 10 viewers have a phone in hand while watching TV.
That research explains the design. The phone is already in the hand; the companion turns it into a second screen tied to whatever is playing on the television, so that a like or a subscription no longer requires a trip through the remote's menus. YouTube has worked on subscriptions from the TV before. In December 2024, YouTube said channel subscriptions on TV rose 40% year over year after it made the subscribe button more prominent, and in the same disclosure put daily viewing on television screens at one billion hours.
The remote is the other input. Voice comments let viewers speak into the remote's microphone "to write and post comments on videos and Shorts," according to the post. A screenshot shows two options next to a video: "Use your phone" and "Use voice." The alternative on the TV itself is an on-screen keyboard driven by the remote, as the child-profile screenshot in the same post shows. The post gives no figures on how many comments come from television.
Ask YouTube moves to the television
Two of the living room items involve Ask YouTube, and the distinction between them matters. The first is search. According to the post, YouTube has "expanded Ask YouTube, our new, conversational search feature to TVs and gaming consoles," where complex, open-ended questions can be asked by voice to get "helpful responses that include text guides and relevant clips." A screenshot shows the spoken query "I love Hot ones what else should I watch" in the TV search bar, an "Ask YouTube" button beneath it, and a row of results from the First We Feast channel.
When YouTube set out the rules for the search version in July, it limited Ask YouTube to signed-in users in the United States aged 13 or older, searching in English on desktop, and excluded signed-out viewers and supervised accounts. It also said views of Shorts, videos and previews inside Ask YouTube responses count toward view metrics and Partner Program eligibility in the same way as ordinary views. The recap does not say whether any of those limits, or that counting rule, apply on TVs.
The second item is the watch page. AI-powered video summaries let a viewer use Ask YouTube while a video plays "to get a quick summary about the content you're watching and learn more about the video's topic," with follow-up questions and an "Ask more" option "to take a deeper dive into the parts of a video you're most interested in." A conversational AI tool for TV watch pages had already appeared in the first-quarter update. The watch-page version is also the one with published usage figures: YouTube said in September that more than 140 million people used Ask YouTube in June 2026, a rise of over 500% from December.
For creators, the shift is from keywords to questions. The query in the screenshot, "I love Hot ones what else should I watch", is a sentence rather than a keyword, and the answer the post describes arrives as a written guide with clips rather than a grid of thumbnails. Which videos are chosen as clips, and how YouTube credits them, the post does not explain.
Family profiles and the mobile key
The last three features concern who is signed in. Kids profiles on TVs let parents "create a child profile directly on the TV screen," which, according to the post, "removes the need to grab your computer or mobile device to manage the setup." The setup screen in the screenshot reads: "This kid account will be linked to yours. They won't have an email and password."
Family account linking covers the opposite preference. A parent who manages accounts from the phone can scan an on-screen code; according to the post, "using the QR code will take you directly to the Family Center in your YouTube mobile app (instead of a web browser) to complete a seamless account linking experience." The TV screen in the screenshot offers either the QR code or the address yt.be/activate with a typed code. The phone shown beside it displays a page at accounts.google.com headed "Let's set up your child on YouTube," with an age selector.
The mobile key builds on a feature that arrived only in September. YouTube introduced account lock on September 10, securing an account on a shared TV behind a four-digit code so that recommendations, subscriptions and search history stay private. PPC Land's report at the time described the phone app as already serving as a key, with a push notification sent when notifications are turned on. The recap gives the function a name and files it under "New security tools": "If you've set up an account lock code, you can securely unlock locked profiles on your TV using a simple push notification prompt sent straight to the YouTube app on your phone." The lock screen in the screenshot reads "Enter your code" and "Or open the YouTube app to unlock," and the phone shows a notification headed "Confirm it's you."
How the phone and remote flows work, according to the post
The post and its screenshots describe four flows in which the phone or the remote takes over a step once handled on the TV screen.
- Companion connection. A video plays on the TV. If the same account is signed in on the phone, the YouTube mobile app connects as a companion automatically, with no shared Wi-Fi network required. The phone then shows like, subscribe and comment controls while the TV keeps playing.
- Voice comments. Next to a video, the TV offers "Use your phone" or "Use voice." With voice, the comment is spoken into the remote's microphone, written out and posted to the video or Short.
- Child profile from the phone. The TV displays a QR code, the address yt.be/activate and a typed code. Scanning the QR code opens Family Center in the YouTube mobile app rather than a web browser, where the account linking is completed.
- Mobile key. A profile protected by an account lock code shows "Enter your code" on the TV. Instead of typing the four digits, the account holder can answer a push notification in the YouTube app on the phone, which unlocks the profile on the TV.
