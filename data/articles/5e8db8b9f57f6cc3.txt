TITLE: Meta-Analysis of 13,210 Breasts Links Transumbilical Augmentation to Low Complication Rates
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis examining 13,210 breasts across 6,605 female patients has revealed that transumbilical breast augmentation achieves low pooled complication rates and consistently high patient satisfaction. Conducted by surgical investigators led by Felipe G. A. Gonçalves and Cristina P. Camargo and published in Aesthetic Plastic Surgery, the investigation synthesized outcomes across seventeen clinical studies through August 2025. Of the 6,605 patients evaluated across the international literature, 6,510 underwent breast enlargement via the transumbilical route, which delivers implants through an incision hidden within the naval contour. Although the statistical analysis demonstrated favorable safety metrics, including a 0.2 percent pooled hematoma incidence and a 1.7 percent capsular contracture rate, the authors stressed that the evidence base relies almost entirely on retrospective single-arm series, preventing definitive causal conclusions regarding surgical superiority or equivalence to established breast augmentation techniques.
Breast augmentation remains among the most common aesthetic procedures globally, with 306,196 operations performed in the United States in 2024 alone. Alternative pathways designed to conceal scars include periareolar, transareolar, transaxillary, and transumbilical routes. First documented by Johnson and Christ in 1993, transumbilical breast augmentation, or TUBA, offers the aesthetic advantage of placing incisions inside the umbilicus, leaving the breast envelope free of visible scars. Nevertheless, widespread adoption has faced significant technical friction. Operating surgeons must manipulate dissection instruments and position implants across a remote subcutaneous tunnel, presenting pocket control challenges.
Subsequent technological and operative refinements have steadily addressed several initial procedural limitations.
To synthesize available evidence, the researchers conducted a systematic review according to PRISMA statement guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, registering their protocol prospectively in PROSPERO under registration CRD420251136594. The team queried MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases through August 2025 using comprehensive controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for mammaplasty, breast implants, and umbilical access routes. Two independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts, and full texts within Rayyan software, with disagreements resolved by a third arbiter. The protocol included studies evaluating adult female patients undergoing TUBA or TUSBA while excluding case reports, non-systematic reviews, and letters lacking extractable data. To prevent artificial statistical inflation, the authors accounted for duplicate reporting by treating overlapping cohorts from identical patient populations as single analytical units rather than independent studies.
The primary endpoints established for the review were patient-reported satisfaction and postoperative hematoma incidence. Fifteen studies encompassing 5,179 patients documented patient satisfaction, reporting uniformly high acceptance of the umbilical scar approach. Brennan and colleagues observed that 95.9 percent of patients were satisfied or very satisfied, while Songcharoen reported that 87 percent were very satisfied and 96 percent would recommend the procedure. Sim, Johnson, and Christ documented universal satisfaction, and Akkary recorded a mean satisfaction score of 9 on a 10-point scale. However, the systematic review revealed that methodological heterogeneity precluded quantitative meta-analytic pooling.
In contrast, postoperative hematoma allowed robust statistical pooling across fifteen studies involving 6,336 patients. Single-arm random-effects generalized linear mixed models using logit transformations calculated a pooled hematoma incidence of 0.2 percent, bounded by a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.1 to 0.5 percent with an I2 value of zero percent, indicating complete absence of observed heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis revealed pooled rates of 0.2 percent for saline TUBA and 0.6 percent for silicone TUSBA, showing no statistically significant difference between modalities (p = 0.7972). While this 0.2 percent benchmark compares favorably with published transaxillary rates of 1.4 percent and inframammary rates of 2.7 percent, the authors cautioned against direct inferences. The low recorded rate could partially reflect underreporting or selective publication in retrospective series rather than intrinsic physiological superiority of the umbilical dissection corridor.
Secondary meta-analytic endpoints reinforced the low complication profile across several key postoperative measures. Capsular contracture was evaluated across fourteen studies comprising 5,683 patients, yielding a pooled incidence of 1.7 percent (95% CI: 0.9–3.2%; I2 = 76.8%). Subgroup analysis clarified that statistical heterogeneity was confined entirely to saline TUBA studies (1.5%, 95% CI: 0.7–3.6%; I2 = 83%), whereas silicone TUSBA demonstrated a pooled rate of 1.8 percent with zero heterogeneity (95% CI: 0.7–4.6%; I2 = 0%). Surgical reintervention, analyzed across twelve studies and 5,947 patients, demonstrated a pooled incidence of 2.3 percent (95% CI: 1.2–4.4%; I2 = 83.1%), with subgroup rates of 2.1 percent for TUBA and 1.7 percent for TUSBA (p = 0.0834).
Statistical modeling demonstrated similarly minimal pooled event rates for infectious and hemorrhagic complications. Postoperative infection, evaluated across fourteen studies encompassing 5,336 patients, generated a pooled incidence of 0.0 percent (95% CI: 0.0–0.7%; I2 = 38.1%). Subgroup analysis showed zero heterogeneity within both TUBA and TUSBA cohorts, with no difference between surgical techniques (p = 0.7479). The authors underscored that this mathematical estimate reflects rarity in the literature rather than absolute immunity from infectious pathogens. Intraoperative bleeding was examined across five studies including 971 patients, showing a pooled incidence of 0.2 percent (95% CI: 0.1–0.8%; I2 = 0%). Seroma formation, reported in eight studies involving 4,131 patients, exhibited a pooled rate of 0.4 percent (95% CI: 0.1–2.7%; I2 = 1.4%), with identical subgroup estimates of 1.3 percent in both TUBA and TUSBA groups.
Implant integrity, placement accuracy, and spatial stability likewise exhibited low overall complication frequencies. Implant rupture and deflation were evaluated across ten studies encompassing 2,461 patients, with a pooled incidence of 0.8 percent (95% CI: 0.4–1.5%; I2 = 0.0%), comprising a 0.9 percent deflation rate in saline devices and zero ruptures among silicone cohorts. Notably, four studies assessing 1,514 patients recorded zero instances of implant malpositioning. Although reassuring given historical concerns regarding remote pocket manipulation, the authors emphasized that this finding is purely descriptive and likely mirrors selective reporting in retrospective publications rather than an absence of mechanical displacement risk.
Operative mechanics and unpooled adverse events were synthesized qualitatively. In most evaluated series, surgical operative duration ranged between 24 and 136 minutes, with a median clustering around 60 minutes, while one comparative investigation reported an extended duration near 180 minutes. A spectrum of secondary adverse events was reported across the studies, though inconsistent reporting precluded formal statistical pooling. Described events included breast asymmetry, implant buckling, superior migration, double-bubble deformity, tissue rippling, bottoming-out, and conversion to an open inframammary fold approach. Minor capsular contracture classified as Baker grade II was cataloged as an adverse event, along with wound dehiscence, hypertrophic or keloid scarring, transient nipple numbness, chronic pain, and self-limiting fibrous cord formation. Several reports also identified procedure-specific morbidity, such as abdominal tunnel induration with transient burning sensations, tunnel contour deformities, suprapubic discomfort, and localized abdominal bulging.
In their methodological appraisal, the authors assigned Level IV evidence to the compiled literature, emphasizing substantial limitations that temper overarching clinical conclusions. Quality evaluations using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist rated only three of fourteen case series at low risk of bias, with nine judged moderate and two high risk; among three comparative studies assessed via ROBINS-I v2, two presented moderate risk and one serious risk. No randomized controlled trials met inclusion criteria, reflecting plastic surgery’s broader literature deficit identified by Song and Chung in 2010. Furthermore, substantial evidence originated from specialized high-volume pioneer surgeons, introducing expertise bias that limits generalizability across less experienced practitioners facing steep learning curves. Short and variable follow-up periods likely underestimate time-dependent complications like contracture and rupture, confirming that while transumbilical breast augmentation appears safe, robust comparative trials remain necessary.
