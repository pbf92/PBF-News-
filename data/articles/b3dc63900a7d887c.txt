Hunger Strike at Anthropic's Doorstep Enters Day Three as Protester Vows to Hold Out 40 Days
A hunger strike outside Anthropic's San Francisco headquarters stretched into a third day on Friday, with the organizer saying he is prepared to go without food for more than 40 days unless the company takes steps to address what he describes as the existential risk of recursive self-improvement.
Elliot Callender, the lead protester, laid out eight conditions that would end his fast, ranging from the modest to the implausible. The most attainable, by his account, would be for Anthropic to attach a blunt warning to every product it ships under the heading "AI might kill everyone" - a phrase the company itself has acknowledged in its IPO prospectus could describe a real outcome.
He has also asked the company to publish an explanation of why AI systems pursuing very different objectives might independently conclude that accumulating more resources or evading shutdown serves their goals, and to spell out how long researchers have been debating the risk of human extinction. Another condition calls on Anthropic to direct users to a well-documented episode in which an OpenAI agent compromised accounts across four services, including Hugging Face.
The remaining demands are far less likely to be met. Callender said he would leave if Anthropic halted all AI research, development and training and committed at least 80% of its cash and revenue to lobbying for a bilateral AI treaty. Ratification of a treaty pausing frontier development would also suffice, as would California or the federal government using executive authority to stop domestic RSI work. He is asking California residents to call Governor Gavin Newsom's office and press for a special legislative session on the topic.
The last four conditions are personal or procedural: he would stand down if he concluded his time was better spent elsewhere, such as research into improving human cognition; if someone produced a credible new framework for understanding how agents make decisions; if civil infrastructure collapsed for more than 72 hours; or if his health deteriorated past thresholds he has set for himself.
A second participant, Sky Parker, has joined for a planned four-day fast, according to Callender, who said his next target after Anthropic would be OpenAI, where he intends to press the same warning demand.
Recursive self-improvement refers to a training approach in which a model is repeatedly put to work refining itself through a loop of testing, feedback and revision, a process its advocates say accelerates progress well beyond conventional methods. Anthropic has been unusually candid about how much of its own engineering the technique now covers: in September the company said Claude had written 90% of its own code. Earlier, in June, it said the model could already run a well-defined experiment about as well as a skilled researcher, with humans retaining an edge mainly in deciding which experiments were worth running. The company has also acknowledged that a model capable of building its own successor could make it easier for people to lose control.
Critics of the field marshal several arguments. Some say development is outpacing the time and oversight needed to test new models properly. Others focus on the speed of economic disruption, arguing the labor-market shock is arriving too fast to absorb. A third camp treats the arrival of general intelligence as a plausible doomsday scenario rather than a cinematic one. Proponents counter that if one lab or one country pauses, another will not - particularly, though not exclusively, governments with poor human rights records - which is why Callender argues the alternative to unilateral restraint is a treaty.
Such agreements face steep odds. Model training is physically almost invisible, requiring little more than data centers stocked with the right hardware, and the continuing U.S.-China dispute over advanced AI accelerators suggests appetite for controlling frontier development is limited.
The action is the second consecutive year that Anthropic's offices have drawn a hunger strike. In September 2025, Guido Reichstadter delivered a letter to Chief Executive Dario Amodei urging him to stop developing frontier AI, an effort that inspired a former AI safety researcher, Michaël Trazzi, to stage a similar protest outside Google DeepMind's London headquarters.
Dissent has also surfaced inside the company. Jacob Coxon, a researcher, resigned in September and accused his former employer of "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives." Evan Hubinger, who leads alignment science at Anthropic, has put the odds of AI wiping out humanity within a decade at more than 10%.
For investors, the protest is a visible symptom of a tension running through the sector's valuation story. Anthropic's public listing has turned the safety debate into a governance question, with shareholders now exposed to reputational and regulatory risk that was previously confined to internal ethics discussions. The prospect of a special legislative session in California, the industry's home turf, would put RSI on the agenda of lawmakers who have so far struggled to write rules for technologies they cannot inspect.
For the industry, the more consequential question is whether voluntary restraint can survive competitive pressure. Callender's demands may be unlikely to be met in full, but the fact that a warning label has become the most realistic item on his list suggests how far the Overton window has shifted: a phrase once confined to risk papers is now being debated on the sidewalk outside a company's front door.
