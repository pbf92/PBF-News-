TITLE: Targeted Perioperative Haemodynamic Therapy Benefits Highest-Risk Surgical Patients Most, Review Finds
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Goal-directed haemodynamic therapy retains clear clinical value during surgery, but its demonstrable benefits are concentrated almost exclusively among patients facing the highest perioperative risks, according to a narrative review in the Journal of Anesthesia, Analgesia and Critical Care. Synthesising evidence across randomised trials, systematic reviews, and physiological frameworks, the authors evaluated whether protocols guiding circulatory management toward predefined physiological endpoints still offer distinct advantages. High-risk surgical patients represent approximately 12 percent of the surgical population yet account for 80 percent of perioperative mortality, ranging from 4.5 percent in elective abdominal surgery to over 25 percent in emergency procedures. While targeted haemodynamic management reliably reduces major postoperative morbidities in these vulnerable individuals, standardised protocols show neutral outcomes in intermediate-risk cohorts, particularly where enhanced recovery pathways are already thoroughly implemented.
The physiological basis of goal-directed haemodynamic therapy links tissue protection directly to cellular oxygenation dynamics during operative trauma. Surgical injury initiates an acute inflammatory cascade where damage-associated molecular patterns activate Toll-like receptors, releasing cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and nitrogen intermediates that drive endothelial dysfunction, vascular damage, and tissue hypoperfusion. This sequence builds upon William Shoemaker’s landmark observation that prompt restoration of oxygen delivery during surgery or in early postoperative hours predicts survival. Patients who repaid their oxygen debt within six hours developed no complications; those taking up to twenty-four hours experienced complications, while those unable to repay it died. This oxygen debt model established the physiological rationale for targeting systemic oxygen delivery as an active therapeutic endpoint rather than relying on passive fluid administration.
To evaluate this strategy in 2026, investigators conducted a narrative review following the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles, or SANRA. The authors searched MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library without date limits, combining Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms for cardiac output, stroke volume, oxygen delivery, fluid optimisation, haemodynamic monitoring, and high-risk surgery. Selection prioritised English-language randomised trials, meta-analyses, systematic reviews, guidelines, and consensus statements addressing four central questions: which patients benefit, which risk thresholds define efficacy, which haemodynamic targets prove most appropriate, and what role artificial intelligence tools play. Methodological appraisal focused on reconciling physiological mechanisms with clinical trials to explain why recent large pragmatic studies diverged from historical meta-analytic conclusions.
A fundamental difficulty across the literature is the absence of a universally accepted definition for high-risk surgical patients, limiting trial comparability and clinical uptake. Guidelines establish conflicting risk thresholds: the European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care defines high risk as procedure-related mortality exceeding 5 percent, while French guidelines classify high risk as 1 to 3 percent mortality and very high risk as over 3 percent. Preoperative scoring tools, including the American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, the Revised Cardiac Risk Index, and the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program calculator, show variable predictive performance. Because meta-analyses demonstrate significant mortality reductions only when baseline risk exceeds 10 percent, the choice of risk-stratification instrument directly determines patient eligibility for advanced circulatory optimisation.
Intraoperative blood pressure management represents an essential baseline in surgical care, yet pressure targets alone cannot guarantee tissue perfusion. Sustained intraoperative hypotension—variously defined as mean arterial pressure below 60 millimetres of mercury for over five to ten minutes, below 55 for over one minute, or a 20 percent decrease from baseline—correlates strongly with postoperative mortality, myocardial injury, and acute kidney injury. Guidelines recommend maintaining mean arterial pressure above 60 to 65 millimetres of mercury in high-risk patients using continuous invasive monitoring. However, two recent randomised trials testing individualised pressure targets against standard thresholds showed no outcome improvement. As editorial analyses emphasise, higher arterial pressure achieved with high-dose vasopressors does not ensure organ perfusion, because hypotension reflects complex interactions among cardiac output, vascular resistance, and organ autoregulation.
Evaluating preload responsiveness through dynamic indicators provides more reliable guidance for fluid therapy than static parameters like central venous pressure. In mechanically ventilated patients, respiratory variations in arterial waveforms—specifically pulse pressure variation and stroke volume variation—quantify fluid responsiveness, defined as a cardiac output increase exceeding 10 to 15 percent following volume expansion. These dynamic indices demonstrate diagnostic areas under the curve from 0.80 in open abdominal procedures to 0.69 in open-chest surgery, though cardiac arrhythmias, tidal volumes under 7 to 8 millilitres per kilogram, and laparoscopic insufflation impair predictive accuracy. Additionally, both parameters share a grey zone between 9 and 13 percent where prediction is uncertain, requiring clinicians to use mini fluid challenges of 50 to 100 millilitres or end-expiratory occlusion tests before administering fluids.
Flow-directed monitoring using cardiac output and stroke volume constitutes the core of goal-directed protocols by distinguishing reduced systemic blood flow from vasodilation. Multiple meta-analyses demonstrate that cardiac output- or stroke volume-guided therapy significantly reduces postoperative complications across major abdominal, orthopaedic, and neurosurgical procedures. Recent analyses confirm notable clinical gains, including an absolute risk reduction in surgical site infections from 13.3 percent down to 9.4 percent, alongside a significant reduction in acute kidney injury with an odds ratio of 0.64. Furthermore, trial sequential analyses across these compiled datasets indicate that future randomised trials are statistically unlikely to overturn observed reductions in acute kidney injury and surgical site infections, confirming that flow-targeted intervention provides definitive organ protection when focused on patients facing high baseline procedural risks.
Optimising systemic oxygen delivery and monitoring cellular oxygen extraction represent the definitive physiological endpoints of goal-directed protocols. Oxygen delivery integrates cardiac output with arterial oxygen saturation and haemoglobin concentration. Historical protocols from the 1980s targeting supranormal oxygen delivery values were abandoned after larger clinical trials showed they provided no benefit or caused harm. Contemporary protocols instead focus on maintaining physiological ranges, such as a cardiac index above 2.5 litres per minute per square metre, supported by metabolic markers including central venous oxygen saturation, oxygen extraction ratio, and serum lactate. Persistently elevated lactate or reduced central venous oxygen saturation despite adequate cardiac output indicates impaired oxygen utilisation and tissue hypoperfusion, signalling to clinicians that targeted interventions beyond simple fluid administration are necessary to preserve cellular function.
Recent pragmatic multicentre trials have introduced significant debate into perioperative medicine by failing to demonstrate superior outcomes from standardised protocols. The OPTIMISE II trial found that combining fluid boluses with routine low-dose inodilator infusions produced no significant reduction in postoperative infections, while the iPEGASUS trial showed that targeting fixed or maximal cardiac index values did not reduce complications in major abdominal surgery. Both trials enrolled lower-risk populations than those in which meta-analytic benefits were largest, while relying on fixed cardiac output targets rather than individualised multiparameter approaches. Furthermore, widespread adoption of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery programmes has attenuated the incremental benefit of goal-directed protocols, as standard enhanced recovery pathways already incorporate dynamic fluid optimisation. However, goal-directed therapy represents a comprehensive strategy extending beyond fluids to manage cardiac output, contractility, afterload, and oxygen delivery.
Economic evaluations indicate that implementing goal-directed haemodynamic therapy generates measurable financial savings, although the extent of cost recovery depends on baseline morbidity and institutional expenses. A United States economic modelling investigation involving 204,680 non-cardiac surgical patients estimated that each dollar invested in goal-directed implementation generates a net return of 2.5 to 4 United States dollars, producing per-patient savings of 754 to 1286 dollars. Health-economic analyses from France and simulation models from Brazil reached similar conclusions regarding cost-effectiveness across various monitoring technologies. Additionally, an Italian report identified potential savings of approximately 400 euros per patient per day through optimised postoperative setting allocation following endovascular repair. Nonetheless, the authors caution that these economic models assume treatment effects consistent with positive historical trials, suggesting real-world savings may prove more modest in unselected patient populations.
Artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies are emerging tools to support perioperative care across risk stratification, waveform analysis, and closed-loop therapeutic management. An interpretable machine learning cardiovascular risk model derived from the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program database achieved an area under the curve of 0.90 for predicting major adverse cardiovascular events. For intraoperative monitoring, the commercial Hypotension Prediction Index forecasts mean arterial pressure below 65 millimetres of mercury at least one minute in advance with 83 percent sensitivity and specificity; however, concerns regarding overtreatment, hypertension, and a lead-time advantage under one minute over threshold alerts call its added value into question. In automation, closed-loop norepinephrine titration reduced hypotension duration tenfold across three randomised trials of 30 to 53 patients, while closed-loop fluid systems demonstrate improved fluid responsiveness rates. However, no closed-loop system has yet proven reductions in hard clinical endpoints like acute kidney injury or mortality.
The review concludes that goal-directed haemodynamic therapy retains clear clinical value in 2026, but primarily when deployed as an individualised, multiparameter regimen tailored to patients at the highest perioperative risk. Applying rigid, single-variable targets across unselected or intermediate-risk cohorts offers little proven benefit over well-implemented enhanced recovery protocols.
