TITLE: Study Identifies Key Factors Linked to Patient Activation Among Chinese Stroke Survivors
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizing sixteen cross-sectional studies across Mainland China reveals that patient activation levels among stroke survivors remain predominantly at a moderate stage while exhibiting pronounced variability across measurement instruments and study cohorts. Investigating data from 5,198 adult patients across eleven provincial-level administrative divisions, the investigation evaluated patient activation, which is defined as the knowledge, skills, and confidence individuals possess to actively manage their own health conditions. Stroke represents a leading cause of long-term disability and mortality throughout China, where approximately seventy-five percent of surviving individuals experience varying degrees of lasting neurological deficits that require persistent, self-directed rehabilitation and lifestyle adaptation. Because neurological recovery relies heavily on active engagement in long-term rehabilitation regimens and self-care practices, understanding the motivational drivers that bridge health knowledge and daily action is essential. Conducted by researchers affiliated with Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine and published in Frontiers in Neurology, this study offers an evidence synthesis specifically examining activation benchmarks and associated demographic and clinical characteristics within this high-burden population.
To capture all eligible research, the investigative team executed systematic literature searches across ten Chinese and English electronic databases up to June 29, 2026, including PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Embase, Scopus, CINAHL, CNKI, SinoMed, Wanfang Data, and the VIP Database. The methodological framework adhered strictly to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO under registration code CRD420261280164. Two reviewers independently conducted title and abstract screening, full-text reviews, and data extraction, resolving discrepancies through structured discussion or consultation with a third senior reviewer. Study methodological quality was formally appraised using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality checklist for cross-sectional studies, which evaluates methodological standards across eleven discrete criteria. All sixteen included investigations met predefined thresholds for moderate or high methodological quality, earning Grade A or Grade B classifications. The included publications spanned from 2020 through 2026, comprising fourteen studies published in Chinese and two in English, with individual sample sizes ranging from 119 to 702 hospitalized patients in stroke recovery periods or post-stroke care across five major geographic regions.
Because the included studies administered different validated assessment tools that possess distinct scoring algorithms, item structures, and psychometric metrics, the researchers conducted separate pooled syntheses rather than combining raw scores across disparate instruments. Thirteen studies evaluated activation utilizing the 13-item Patient Activation Measure, yielding a pooled weighted mean score of 52.14 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning 48.19 to 56.09 under a restricted maximum likelihood random-effects model. However, between-study heterogeneity among these thirteen studies was exceptionally high, demonstrating an I-squared statistic of 99.28 percent. In contrast, two studies utilized the Chinese Health Activation Inventory, producing a separate pooled score of 59.29 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 57.76 to 60.82 and showing zero observed heterogeneity. A single investigation assessed 348 patients using the 22-item Patient Activation Measure, reporting a mean score of 48.67 with a standard deviation of 9.28, which was analyzed descriptively due to its unique structural framework.
When comparing the pooled activation levels to international benchmarks, the authors noted that the pooled PAM-13 mean of 52.14 among Chinese stroke survivors was lower than the mean score of 60.2 reported by van Meijeren-Pont and colleagues among Dutch stroke patients during their initial six months of rehabilitation. Nonetheless, the authors emphasized that direct comparisons require extreme caution because the Dutch data originated from a single prospective, longitudinal rehabilitation cohort, whereas the present synthesis aggregated thirteen heterogeneous, cross-sectional samples evaluated at diverse post-stroke recovery intervals, clinical settings, and regional healthcare environments. Given the extreme heterogeneity of 99.28 percent, the pooled PAM-13 score represents a mathematical summary of available published studies rather than a definitive, universally representative population value for Chinese stroke survivors nationwide. The primary authors hypothesized that unmeasured discrepancies in regional economic development, access to primary rehabilitation facilities, patient age profiles, functional impairment degrees, and local psychological support structures could account for these wide variations, although absence of detailed meta-regression precluded formal verification of specific sources of heterogeneity.
The meta-analysis evaluated fifty-one potential influencing factors identified across the literature, ultimately incorporating sixteen variables into quantitative meta-analytic pooling because they were reported by at least two studies with compatible, extractable data. For each pooled variable, standardized mean differences were calculated under the restricted maximum likelihood random-effects model, comparing predefined groups expected to exhibit higher activation against reference groups. The quantitative synthesis demonstrated that seven factors achieved statistically significant pooled associations with patient activation in the primary random-effects model. Specifically, functional independence displayed the strongest positive association, demonstrating a standardized mean difference of 0.584 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.490 to 0.678. Educational attainment was likewise strongly and positively linked to activation, yielding a standardized mean difference of 0.516 with a 95 percent confidence interval ranging from 0.402 to 0.629. Monthly income also demonstrated a statistically significant positive association, generating an effect size of 0.424 with a 95 percent confidence interval between 0.262 and 0.587, underscoring the influence of socioeconomic and physical resources.
Additional statistically significant associations in the primary restricted maximum likelihood model were observed for clinical and external support characteristics. Patients without systemic complications exhibited significantly higher activation than those presenting with complications, reflecting a standardized mean difference of 0.313 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.101 to 0.524 across six pooled studies. Residential geography also demonstrated a meaningful association, with urban dwellers achieving higher activation scores than rural residents, reflected by a standardized mean difference of 0.296 and a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.110 to 0.482 across six investigations. Marital status emerged as another significant factor across seven studies, where married survivors attained higher activation than unmarried, divorced, or widowed peers, showing an effect size of 0.261 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.146 to 0.376. Disease duration displayed a modest association across three studies, where individuals in the early post-stroke phase under six months reported slightly higher activation than those with longer disease durations, yielding a standardized mean difference of 0.129 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.006 to 0.251.
To evaluate the stability and credibility of these associations, the investigators conducted rigorous sensitivity analyses comparing the primary restricted maximum likelihood estimates against fixed-effect models and the Hartung-Knapp adjustment method. This sensitivity testing demonstrated that certain variables were sensitive to statistical modeling assumptions. Patient age showed a statistically significant inverse association under the conventional random-effects model across four studies, with an effect size of negative 0.270 and a p-value of 0.0435, but lost statistical significance upon applying the Hartung-Knapp adjustment, widening the 95 percent confidence interval from negative 0.705 to positive 0.164 with a p-value of 0.142. Similarly, the number of stroke episodes was statistically significant under a fixed-effect model with an effect size of 0.204 and a p-value of 0.0003, but failed to reach significance under the primary random-effects model, producing an effect size of 0.148 and a p-value of 0.2529. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and Baujat diagnostic plot identified a 2025 study by Deng and colleagues as a major outlier; excluding this study reduced heterogeneity from 79.70 percent to 55.63 percent, while the pooled association remained non-significant at an effect size of 0.039.
The review established that eight of the sixteen pooled factors exhibited no statistically detectable association with patient activation in the primary random-effects analyses. These non-significant variables comprised patient sex, residential living situation, mode of medical expense payment, specific stroke type, identity of the primary caregiver, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and the number of stroke episodes. Furthermore, prediction intervals were calculated for factors supported by three or more studies to estimate the expected dispersion of effect sizes in future settings. The 95 percent prediction intervals remained entirely above zero for education level, marital status, monthly income, and functional independence, indicating that positive associations for these four factors are likely to replicate across future clinical cohorts. Conversely, the prediction intervals for residence, presence of complications, and disease duration crossed the null value of zero, demonstrating that the magnitude and direction of these associations may vary considerably across different healthcare settings. Thirty-five additional factors, including health literacy, depression, anxiety, stigma, and coping style, were documented descriptively from single studies but lacked sufficient data for quantitative pooling.
The authors contextualized these statistical findings within known rehabilitation mechanisms while emphasizing the limitations inherent in observational data. Higher educational attainment and household income likely foster greater health literacy, enabling patients to seek, interpret, and apply complex medical guidance while mitigating financial distress surrounding long-term rehabilitation costs. The protective role of marital status suggests that practical assistance and psychological support from a spouse bolster patient confidence in navigating complex self-care tasks. Urban residence may confer structural advantages through proximity to advanced rehabilitation facilities and varied educational channels, whereas rural survivors frequently encounter geographic isolation, transportation hurdles, and limited primary rehabilitation infrastructure. However, because all sixteen reviewed studies relied strictly on unadjusted cross-sectional comparisons, the reported effect sizes represent unadjusted correlational associations rather than independent causal mechanisms.
In their concluding appraisal, the investigators outlined critical methodological caveats and practical implications for clinical research. A central limitation remains the cross-sectional architecture of the underlying evidence base, which precludes establishing chronological precedence or ruling out residual confounding from unmeasured clinical covariates.
