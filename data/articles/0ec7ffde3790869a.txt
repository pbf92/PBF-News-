TITLE: High consumption of reels on the internet linked to worse mental health in youth
OUTLET: Plataforma Media

Highlights, Society, World / World Society
High consumption of reels on the internet linked to worse mental health in youth
An observational study in JAMA Pediatrics following over 18,000 adolescents in China links high and addictive consumption of short online videos (reels) to increased mental health, self-harm, and suicide risks
The high consumption of short videos on the internet, common among adolescents, combined with addictive use, is associated with a greater risk of suicidal tendencies, self-harm, and mental health problems, according to a study. The research, which is observational in nature (it does not establish a cause-and-effect relationship) and was published by JAMA Pediatrics, was led by Chinese researchers.
The study was based on tracking more than 18,000 adolescents (aged between 10 and 18) in China over three years, who reported their consumption habits for this type of content. Viewing time and addictive use were classified into four categories: low viewing time/low addictive use; high viewing time/low addictive use; low viewing time/high addictive use; and high viewing time/high addictive use.
Compared with adolescents who showed neither high viewing time nor addictive use, transitioning to these higher levels, or maintaining such levels, was associated with higher risks. The study indicated that 21.4% of adolescents reported spending a lot of time watching short videos (more than 120 minutes per day).
Approximately 4% reported having attempted suicide, 17.3% reported suicidal thoughts, and 6.3% reported episodes of self-harm. The trajectory of the participants over the three years suggests that many adolescents reduced the time dedicated to these videos as they grew older, or shifted from high levels of use to lower levels in subsequent years.
“The overall percentages remained the same, but participants appeared to reduce excessive video use as they grew older,” explained Luis Valero, professor of Psychology at the University of Malaga, who did not participate in the study.
When examining the progression over three years, a correlation with suicides or suicide attempts was observed, as with increasing age, there was a higher likelihood of these and all other evaluated problems.
The authors argue that this relationship is due to successive and cumulative exposure to excessive video use, Valero emphasized to the Science Media Centre.
Valero considered the study “comprehensive, methodologically and statistically sound,” although he stressed that other analyses could have been carried out and that the results only partially demonstrate the association between excessive video use and suicides or suicide attempts.
