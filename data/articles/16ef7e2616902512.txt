TITLE: Enzymatic Debridement Shows Decompressive Potential in Circumferential Acute Burn Review
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic review of international clinical literature paired with an institutional case series indicates that bromelain-based enzymatic debridement can serve as a minimally invasive decompressive intervention for patients presenting with circumferential or constrictive acute burns who are at risk of developing burn-induced compartment syndrome. The investigation, conducted by surgical researchers at Ghent University Hospital and published in Burns Open, evaluated six eligible clinical studies alongside four institutional cases to explore whether the enzymatic formulation NexoBrid could relieve tissue constriction without immediate recourse to traditional surgical cutting. Across the surveyed observational cohorts and the hospital cohort, enzymatic debridement dissolved constrictive burn eschar while preserving underlying viable dermis, allowing tissue expansion and stabilizing vascular perfusion in selected presentations. However, the authors emphasized that the current literature base consists entirely of non-randomized evidence subject to serious risk of bias, meaning that while enzymatic debridement represents a valuable early or preventive decompressive tool when clinical indications remain subtle, it cannot yet be classified as a universal replacement for emergency surgical decompression.
Burn-induced compartment syndrome represents a severe, limb-threatening and life-threatening complication that emerges following deep circumferential or constricting thermal injuries to extremities, the abdomen, or the thorax. The pathophysiology underlying this medical emergency involves the formation of an inelastic, rigid, cocoon-like eschar that cannot expand as post-burn edema progressively accumulates during aggressive fluid resuscitation. As interstitial fluid collects beneath the unyielding leathery burned skin, subeschar pressures experience a progressive, sharp rise that gradually exceeds normal capillary perfusion thresholds. This sustained mechanical pressure compresses the microvasculature, precipitating local hypoperfusion, severe tissue ischemia, and ultimately irreversible neuromuscular and soft-tissue necrosis. Concurrently, elevated mechanical tension compromises the fragile vascular supply feeding adjacent zones of partially injured skin, leading to the progressive deepening of the initial thermal injury. Clinically, burn-induced compartment syndrome manifests as hardening local tension, intense pain, paresthesias, delayed or absent capillary refill, poikilothermia, and the eventual cessation of detectable distal arterial pulsations, with objective diagnosis confirmed when measured intracompartmental pressures exceed 30 to 35 millimeters of mercury in limb compartments or 15 to 20 millimeters of mercury inside the abdominal cavity.
For decades, urgent surgical escharotomy has stood as the established standard of care for rapidly neutralizing pathological subeschar pressures in constricting burns. The operative procedure requires a clinician to execute full-thickness longitudinal surgical incisions through the rigid eschar, extending continuously from intact unburned tissue at one margin to intact unburned skin at the opposite end. Ideally penetrating deep enough to expose underlying healthy tissue, these surgical releases permit swollen subcutaneous tissues to herniate outward, alleviating compartment constriction and promptly re-establishing vital arterial and venous blood flow. In scenarios marked by acute, unmistakable vascular compromise, surgical escharotomy remains the most rapid, direct, and dependable operative option available to burn teams. Nonetheless, conventional surgical escharotomy possesses notable clinical drawbacks that complicate acute management, particularly when executed by surgical staff lacking extensive burn-center experience. Furthermore, placing incisions incorrectly or failing to extend them across the entirety of the constricting band generates inadequate tissue decompression while cultivating a dangerous, false sense of clinical security among attending staff.
Because clinical manifestations of compartment syndrome frequently appear relatively late in the ischemic cascade or become obscured by generalized burn pain and baseline tissue swelling, diagnostic uncertainty routinely arises during early or subtle presentations. In such borderline scenarios, clinicians often exhibit understandable hesitation to perform invasive, scarring surgical incisions through borderline-depth tissues that might otherwise retain intrinsic healing potential. NexoBrid, an established enzymatic debridement formulation extracted from bromelain and manufactured by MediWound, was initially developed as a minimally invasive alternative to conventional surgical excision for deep burn wounds. Clinical investigations have documented that this topical enzyme mixture possesses high biochemical selectivity, dissolving devitalized eschar within hours while sparing underlying viable dermal architecture and significantly decreasing subsequent requirements for autologous split-thickness skin grafting. Crucially, the authors highlight an essential mechanistic distinction between conventional surgery and enzymatic treatment: whereas escharotomy merely incises the constricting band while leaving dead tissue intact—often requiring formal tangential surgical excision days later—enzymatic debridement concurrently achieves decompressive tension release and complete eschar removal within a single clinical intervention.
To formally evaluate the safety and clinical efficacy of enzymatic debridement as an alternative or adjunct decompressive strategy, the Ghent research group registered a systematic review protocol in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews under registration CRD420261403005 and followed PRISMA guidelines. The investigators executed comprehensive literature searches across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library through May 2026 without language restriction on initial yields. From an initial capture of 289 citations, database filtering and duplicate removal yielded 133 unique articles for title and abstract screening. Full-text evaluation was conducted on 12 retrieved studies by two independent reviewers, with discrepancies resolved by senior surgical consensus. Ultimately, six published clinical studies met the rigorous criteria requiring clinical data on bromelain-based enzymatic debridement in circumferential or constricting burns at risk of compartment syndrome. The research team applied the Cochrane ROBINS-I tool to systematically assess methodological quality across domains covering confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended protocols, missing data, outcome measurement accuracy, and selective reporting.
The methodological assessment established that none of the six published investigations were randomized controlled trials, leading the review team to classify the cumulative risk of bias across all available studies as serious. Confounding by indication proved to be the most profound source of bias, because attending burn clinicians consistently selected patients for enzymatic decompression based on burn depth, anatomical presentation, and baseline clinical stability rather than standardized allocation. In an adult series by Arkoulis and colleagues covering five circumferential hand burns treated with the enzymatic agent, complete decompression was achieved without rescue surgical escharotomy, and 80 percent of the hands healed spontaneously without requiring skin grafting, although monitoring parameters and objective pressure data were omitted.
Direct physiological confirmation of enzymatic decompression was established by Mataro and collaborators in a prospective study assessing 23 patients with 40 circumferential hand burns. By recording objective interstitial compartment pressures before, during, and following topical enzymatic application, the authors documented an average compartment pressure decrease of 60 percent across the four-hour treatment window, with zero patients exhibiting signs of persistent compromise or necessitating rescue surgical incisions. Similarly, a clinical series by Bower and colleagues documented five cases of impending burn-induced compartment syndrome managed with enzymatic debridement monitored through frequent 15-minute bed-side tissue evaluations until peripheral limbs softened, reporting successful tension release without rescue escharotomy. The only comparative cohort identified in the review, published by Grünherz and colleagues, evaluated 29 enzymatic debridement procedures against 33 conventional surgical escharotomies. While patients allocated to conventional surgery had significantly greater total burn surface areas and higher Abbreviated Burn Severity Index scores, two patients in the enzymatic group, both suffering from severe burns encompassing more than 50 percent of total body surface area, experienced failed decompression and required rescue surgical escharotomy to restore vascular perfusion.
Population-level associations were provided by Marchica and colleagues through an analysis of 145 circumferential burn patients registered in the Italian National Burn Database. Within that multicenter cohort, 79 patients received enzymatic debridement while 66 received standard surgical management; multivariable logistic regression demonstrated that enzymatic intervention was independently associated with a significantly reduced likelihood of undergoing surgical escharotomy, showing an odds ratio of 0.47 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning 0.36 to 0.61. To ground these diverse published findings in active practice, the Ghent University Hospital team detailed their own institutional experience managing four consecutive cases of impending compartment syndrome using a standardized, time-dependent enzymatic debridement protocol approved by the institutional ethical review committee under protocol 2019/1535. The clinical cohort comprised three adult male patients aged 21, 32, and 33 years, as well as a 60-year-old female patient, with burn injuries caused by flame or boiling oil covering total body surface areas ranging from 8.5 percent to 31 percent across upper limbs, lower limbs, and combined abdominal and thoracic regions.
The Ghent procedural protocol adhered to strict chronological intervals designed to safely integrate enzymatic action within acute resuscitation workflows. During initial assessment (T0), burn surfaces were soaked with polyhexanide-betaine solution (Prontosan) while analgesia or regional or general anesthesia was administered; the team noted that in three of the four cases, burns exhibited a dry leathery aspect and debridement proceeded rapidly without prolonged pre-soaking. Following mechanical cleaning, the enzymatic gel was applied across the constricting burn under an occlusive, transparent polyurethane dressing (T1), enabling continuous direct inspection alongside continuous digital pulse oximetry every 30 minutes. After four hours of enzymatic contact, the dissolved eschar and residual paste were thoroughly scraped and washed away with antiseptic solution (T2), followed by a mandatory two-hour secondary wet soak and surgical bed reassessment (T3). In limbs displaying deep tissue loss, definitive autografting was scheduled within one week (T4), whereas wounds exhibiting viable dermal pinpoints were managed conservatively using temporary glycerol-preserved allografts or antibacterial enzyme alginogels until complete epithelialization occurred.
The clinical outcomes from the Ghent case series mirrored the decompressive efficacy seen in international studies, with none of the four patients requiring rescue surgical escharotomy.
