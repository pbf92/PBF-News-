TITLE: Review Assesses Emerging Biological and Physical Therapies for Knee Osteoarthritis
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Knee osteoarthritis represents a widespread and debilitating musculoskeletal disorder characterized by progressive degenerative and inflammatory alterations that compromise articular cartilage and expose subchondral bone. The pathology involves synovial inflammation, cartilage loss, structural remodeling of periarticular bone, meniscal degradation, and progressive structural failure across surrounding tendons and ligaments. While the condition has historically been documented primarily in individuals older than sixty-five years, epidemiological surveillance reveals a concerning shift toward younger cohorts aged fifty-five to fifty-nine years, especially across upper-middle-income countries and the Western Pacific and Asia-Pacific regions. Contributing drivers include prior joint trauma, obesity, excessive mechanical loading, impaired neuromuscular coordination, and underlying genetic predisposition. These pathogenic factors accelerate the depletion of essential extracellular components, specifically proteoglycans and type II collagen, while promoting subchondral bone sclerosis, marginal osteophyte formation, and synovial hyperplasia. Conventional pharmacological management depends on oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, analgesics, intra-articular glucocorticoids, and chondroprotective preparations. However, these traditional pharmacological modalities operate primarily as symptomatic palliatives rather than disease-modifying agents. Furthermore, extended pharmacotherapy carries significant risks of serious systemic complications, encompassing gastrointestinal hemorrhages, cardiovascular events, and progressive renal dysfunction. These persistent clinical shortcomings have catalyzed interest in pathogenetically targeted adjuvant, biological, and non-pharmacological strategies capable of relieving symptoms while modulating underlying molecular pathways.
To evaluate the therapeutic validity and biological mechanisms of emerging modalities that currently sit outside mainstream clinical practice guidelines, researchers at Poltava State Medical University conducted an extensive mini-review analyzing recent clinical and experimental evidence. The investigators systematically interrogated major biomedical repositories, including PubMed and MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, examining published literature across an eight- to ten-year timeframe spanning 2018 through 2026. Data extraction and methodological analyses were executed under standard guidelines adapted from the Cochrane Collaboration for systematic reviews. The search utilized targeted indexing terms covering carbon dioxide therapy, carboxytherapy, transcutaneous carbon dioxide administration, platelet-rich plasma, human placental extract, non-pharmacological treatments, balance training, and exercise therapy. An initial literature capture identified fifty-six relevant candidate articles, of which twenty-five met all predefined quality criteria and were included in the final synthesized analysis.
Among biological interventions, intra-articular injections of platelet-rich plasma emerged with strong clinical documentation demonstrating consistent therapeutic utility and favorable safety profiles. Synthesized evidence from multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses highlighted that platelet-rich plasma, particularly formulations classified as leukocyte-poor, provides significant analgesic efficacy and functional improvements compared to conventional intra-articular injections of corticosteroid preparations or hyaluronic acid, notably in patients with mild-to-moderate knee osteoarthritis. The clinical literature demonstrates that therapeutic protocols delivering three intra-articular platelet-rich plasma injections administered at weekly intervals yield greater functional gains than isolated single-injection regimens. Furthermore, systematic meta-analyses indicated that these clinical benefits, encompassing reductions in joint pain, relief of functional stiffness, and improved capacity for physical activity, were sustained at twelve months of follow-up. Platelet-rich plasma operates by modulating the intra-articular microenvironment, downregulating localized catabolic cascades, and promoting anabolic regenerative responses within injured articular tissues. Despite these documented advantages and an observed low incidence of adverse clinical events, the researchers emphasized that broader implementation in routine orthopedics remains constrained by substantial financial barriers, variable preparation techniques, and the continued absence of universally accepted composition standards across healthcare institutions.
The review also evaluated more novel biological substances, notably human placental extract and cellular therapy derivatives from diverse anatomical tissue sources. Preclinical and clinical investigations showed that human placental extract exhibits marked anti-inflammatory and tissue-regenerative properties when introduced into osteoarthritic joint microenvironments. The molecular mechanisms underlying human placental extract include the suppression of cellular senescence, mitigation of chondrocyte apoptosis, and attenuation of excessive reactive oxygen species accumulation. In addition, experimental models revealed that bioactive mesenchymal stem cell-derived factors within human placental extract downregulate the transcription of catabolic cartilage-degrading genes, suggesting potential for retarding structural joint degradation. In parallel, stem cells harvested from bone marrow, adipose tissue, synovial membranes, and periosteum demonstrate verified chondrogenic differentiation potential that could theoretically facilitate cartilage repair. However, the authors noted substantial physiological limitations: stress-induced cellular senescence within the diseased articular environment frequently compromises the long-term viability and regenerative capacity of transplanted stem cells. Consequently, current clinical evidence for these biological interventions remains largely confined to short-term functional improvements, highlighting the pressing necessity for well-designed clinical trials to determine optimal dosing protocols, cellular survival parameters, and long-term structural efficacy.
Carboxytherapy, involving the medical application of carbon dioxide through subcutaneous injection or transcutaneous administration, represents a distinctive adjunctive intervention highlighted in the review for its pleiotropic mechanisms of action. Subcutaneous delivery of gaseous carbon dioxide provides a localized, controlled stimulus that directly alters microvascular tone and tissue perfusion. The physiological cascade induced by localized elevations of carbon dioxide partial pressure involves the rapid activation of nitric oxide-dependent signaling pathways, promoting precapillary sphincter relaxation and microvascular vasodilation in ischemic, inflamed periarticular tissues. Preclinical models of monoiodoacetic acid-induced osteoarthritis demonstrated that carboxytherapy significantly suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically reducing tumor necrosis factor-alpha concentrations while modulating transforming growth factor-beta-one signaling, both when administered alone and when combined with standard nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Experimental investigations also noted marked antioxidant benefits, including reduced lipid peroxidation and restored endogenous antioxidant enzyme activity. Clinical case series documented accelerated resolution of periarticular hematomas, soft-tissue edema, and pain, though the authors emphasized that formal randomized clinical trials remain essential to define standardized administration parameters.
Structured kinesiotherapy and exercise interventions represent established non-pharmacological pillars recognized as first-line recommendations by international organizations, including OARSI, EULAR, and the American College of Rheumatology. The review’s synthesis confirmed that exercise programs produce consistent reductions in joint discomfort while enhancing physical capacity, with distinct exercise modalities exerting specific physiological benefits. Aerobic conditioning demonstrated superior efficacy in mitigating pain perception, whereas progressive endurance training yielded greater improvements in reversing joint stiffness, correcting functional disability, and enhancing general quality of life. Resistance training, cycling interventions, and neuromuscular programs also provided meaningful symptomatic benefits across diverse patient populations. Clinical trials examining structured inpatient programs, such as an eighteen-day regimen evaluating therapeutic exercise alone or combined with cryotherapy or joint mobilization, confirmed notable short-term gains in physical function and pain reduction among adults aged forty-five to fifty-five years. Nevertheless, systematic evidence indicates that peak clinical benefits typically manifest during the initial three months of training, after which functional gains tend to plateau or gradually diminish over six- to twelve-month periods. This attenuation underscores the critical necessity of fostering sustained exercise adherence, addressing patient kinesiophobia, and tailoring rehabilitation protocols to individual functional capacities.
Specialized range-of-motion rehabilitation strategies also demonstrated meaningful therapeutic efficacy by addressing biomechanical deficits that accelerate structural joint breakdown. Pathological loss of knee extension and flexion induces abnormal medial compartment contact stresses, alters gait kinetics, and exacerbates subchondral bone loading. Targeted range-of-motion protocols, utilizing structured stretching, manual therapy, and mechanical mobility devices, work to restore normal kinematic patterns, alleviate protective periarticular muscle spasm, and lower the metabolic cost of ambulation. Parallel pilot trials showed that manual therapy combined with therapeutic exercise expands both open and closed kinetic chain mobility while driving statistically significant reductions in Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index scores and visual analog pain scales.
Aquatic exercise therapy, or hydrokinesiotherapy, offers another specialized rehabilitation pathway that delivers comprehensive physiological benefits, particularly for individuals experiencing severe pain, marked functional limitations, or excessive body mass. The buoyancy of water offsets gravitational forces, substantially diminishing axial and compressive loading across articular cartilage and vulnerable subchondral structures while allowing patients to execute strengthening and range-of-motion drills without mechanical joint overload. The warmth of therapeutic water pools promotes muscular relaxation, reduces nociceptive nerve terminal sensitivity, and dampens pain-related fear of movement. Systematic reviews of randomized trials indicated that aquatic training improves walking velocity, cadence symmetry, and exercise tolerance. Although aquatic regimens may not always outperform land-based exercise in unselected patient populations, they demonstrate pronounced clinical utility in frail or obese cohorts unable to tolerate terrestrial impact. Moreover, clinical trials showed that combining aquatic exercise with thermal balneotherapy enriched with bromine and iodine salts produced superior analgesic responses and functional gains compared to passive balneotherapy alone.
Balance and sensorimotor training were identified as essential modalities for managing proprioceptive decay, functional instability, and neuromuscular deficits that frequently afflict osteoarthritis patients. Compensatory biomechanical loading secondary to sensorimotor impairment accelerates localized cartilage degradation.
