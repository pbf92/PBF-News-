TITLE: Paediatric Forearm Exostosis Review Shows Ulnar Lengthening Dominates Surgical Management
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive scoping review synthesising a decade of clinical evidence reveals that gradual ulnar lengthening remains the primary surgical procedure for managing progressive forearm deformities in paediatric patients with multiple hereditary exostoses, even as current anatomical classification systems fail to reliably guide operative planning. The systematic investigation, conducted across global clinical literature published between January 2015 and June 2025, evaluated clinical practices and structural outcomes in children under eighteen years of age suffering from this inherited skeletal condition. Multiple hereditary exostoses, an autosomal dominant disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in the tumour suppressor genes EXT1 or EXT2, leads to the development of multiple benign, cartilage-capped osseous projections originating from the metaphyseal regions during skeletal development. Because these exostoses arise near active growth plates, differential longitudinal growth rates between the radius and ulna frequently cause progressive structural deformities, secondary joint instability, and marked functional disability across the growing paediatric forearm skeleton.
The underlying biomechanical pathology in the paediatric forearm stems directly from asymmetric bone growth, wherein osteochondromas originating at the physis disrupt normal developmental kinetics. While solitary osteochondromas account for approximately eighty-five percent of all benign osseous tumours, the remaining fifteen percent occur within multiple hereditary exostoses and present a far higher risk of severe structural deformity. Disproportionate longitudinal growth typically produces significant ulnar shortening accompanied by compensatory increased radial bowing, carpal translocation, distal radioulnar joint instability, and catastrophic radial head subluxation or dislocation. These anatomic derangements translate into significant functional limitations, including diminished forearm pronation and supination, painful wrist or elbow motion, and substantial restrictions in daily motor tasks and athletic participation. In addition to physical disability, young patients often suffer notable psychosocial distress related to progressive forearm asymmetry, cosmetic disfigurement, and altered social engagement, underscoring the necessity of objective clinical evaluation and coordinated surgical decision-making throughout childhood skeletal maturation.
To rigorously examine contemporary clinical management, investigators searched four primary databases—MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library—supplemented by targeted grey literature searches in Google Scholar up to June 17, 2025. Two independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts, and full texts according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines for scoping reviews, resolving screening disputes through an independent third reviewer. From an initial pool of nine hundred eighty-three identified records, the authors conducted full-text screening on one hundred sixty-five reports, ultimately isolating twenty-eight studies that met all inclusion criteria after excluding older literature and single case reports. The final cohort comprised twenty interventional studies evaluating surgical techniques and eight non-interventional observational studies examining natural history, risk factors, and anatomical classification systems. Methodologically, twenty-seven of the twenty-eight studies were retrospective, twenty-five were conducted at single centres, and patient sample sizes ranged widely from four to one hundred thirteen individuals across cohorts.
Anatomical classification systems frequently utilized in the literature proved insufficient for determining operative thresholds or predicting clinical outcomes. The Masada classification framework was by far the most widely adopted schema across seventeen included studies, followed by more recent systems introduced by Jo and Gottschalk designed to capture nuanced anatomical distributions and deformity configurations. When investigators formally evaluated diagnostic performance, the Masada classification achieved only fair inter-rater reliability, whereas the Gottschalk and Jo systems demonstrated moderate agreement among independent evaluators. While the Gottschalk system improved structural characterisation of osteochondroma locations and the Jo classification integrated lesion sites with gross deformity patterns, neither schema accounted for multiple lesions along the same bone nor demonstrated reliable prognostic power regarding deformity progression. Across the reviewed interventional literature, surgical teams primarily classified forearms retrospectively to document deformity patterns, whereas actual operative decisions were determined by specific local anatomical disruptions and functional deficits rather than assigned classification categories.
Non-interventional cohorts identified specific radiographic parameters that objectively predict secondary radial head dislocation, providing clinicians with measurable anatomical thresholds for proactive surveillance. Multiple studies confirmed that the presence of distal ulnar osteochondromas represents a major predictive risk factor for radial head displacement, particularly when accompanied by co-existing distal radial lesions that exacerbate interosseous membrane tethering. Strongly predictive quantitative markers identified in clinical cohorts include radial or ulnar shortening exceeding 4.6 centimetres, radial bowing exceeding 8.1 percent, a radial articular angle surpassing 35 degrees, and the presence of three or more exostoses along the forearm. Additional predisposing markers include an increased total ulnar bow exceeding 17 degrees, proximal radioulnar joint irregularities, and a proportional ulnar length below 0.90, which reflects relative ulnar deficiency compared to radial length. Identification of these structural thresholds provides objective criteria to assist surgeons in identifying unstable forearms before irreversible radiocapitellar dislocation occurs.
The review underscored that conventional imaging modalities require meticulous standardisation because structural deformities in multiple hereditary exostoses predominate primarily within the coronal plane. Retrospective comparisons revealed that angular deformities, including total ulnar bow, intramedullary ulnar angles, tangent ulnar angles, and overall ulnar angles, demonstrated statistically significant enlargements in patients with radial head dislocation when measured on standardized anteroposterior radiographs. In contrast, lateral forearm projections failed to capture these significant angular discrepancies, illustrating that relying strictly on sagittal radiographic evaluations risks severely underestimating progressive structural pathology. Because plain radiographs of complex two-bone structures remain highly sensitive to subtle rotational positioning during acquisition, researchers emphasized the emerging utility of advanced cross-sectional diagnostics. Modalities such as three-dimensional computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging provide superior anatomical definition of physeal tethering, soft-tissue constraints, and multiplanar joint congruency, which may substantially enhance future preoperative planning and post-correction structural evaluation.
Across the twenty surgical intervention studies, gradual ulnar lengthening achieved through distraction osteogenesis using external fixation emerged as the most widely implemented reconstructive procedure. However, procedural comparisons indicated that multi-joint external constructs carried higher risks of secondary ulnar axis deviation in older paediatric patients due to asymmetric lateral torque generated by non-coplanar pin configurations during distraction.
Investigators observed that the structural durability of ulnar lengthening often required supplementary operative procedures to achieve stable skeletal correction. In young adolescents presenting with persistent angular deformities near the conclusion of their developmental growth spurts, surgeons frequently paired ulnar distraction with corrective radial osteotomies to re-establish symmetrical forearm architecture. For severe salvage cases with recurrent dislocation, the creation of a single-bone forearm via radioulnar synostosis provided pain relief and structural stabilization, though it permanently eliminated forearm rotation. Notably, incorporating intramedullary nail fixation alongside gradual ulnar lengthening significantly enhanced osteogenesis, improved the bone-healing index, and shortened external frame duration by reinforcing mechanical stability across the distraction gap.
Despite positive radiographic corrections across all interventional studies, long-term clinical outcomes were tempered by recurring complications and variable regenerate consolidation. Analytical data revealed that patient age over 12.5 years, reduced body mass index, and smaller ulnar shaft diameter at the osteotomy level significantly predisposed patients to delayed regenerate healing.
A critical finding of the scoping review was the profound disconnect between reported radiographic success and documented functional or patient-centred outcomes. Furthermore, existing functional evaluations frequently relied on adult-derived instruments, such as the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand questionnaire, the Short-Form 12 survey, or the Mayo Elbow Performance score, none of which possess robust validation in paediatric populations. This reliance on adult metrics severely constrains understanding regarding whether anatomical realignment translates into meaningful improvements in pain relief, daily physical participation, cosmetic satisfaction, or long-term psychosocial well-being for affected children and their families.
The review authors concluded that while gradual ulnar lengthening remains the primary therapeutic strategy for paediatric forearm deformities in multiple hereditary exostoses, clinical adoption of current literature requires extreme caution due to substantial methodological heterogeneity. Existing published evidence is largely confined to small, retrospective, single-centre cohorts characterised by non-standardised lengthening protocols and an absence of prospective controlled trials. To overcome these persistent clinical limitations, future research initiatives must prioritise establishing a universally reproducible, treatment-oriented classification system that accounts for intra-osseous lesion loads and reliably predicts longitudinal natural history. Furthermore, multicentre collaborative prospective registries utilising paediatric-specific validated instruments, such as the Paediatric Outcomes Data Collection Instrument, are urgently required to establish clear clinical thresholds for prophylactic intervention and definitively determine the long-term functional efficacy of surgical reconstruction.
