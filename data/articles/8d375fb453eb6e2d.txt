TITLE: Arizona lab helps find a blood test that may detect CTE in living people
OUTLET: AZ Family

SUN CITY, AZ (AZFamily) — A blood test may one day detect advanced chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) in living people, according to a study published by researchers from Boston University (BU) and Phoenix-based Banner Health.
CTE is a neurodegenerative disease caused in part by repetitive head impacts and is more prevalent among athletes. Until now, it could be accurately diagnosed only after death, through direct examination of the brain.
In the study published Thursday in The Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA), researchers say this is the first time CTE neuropathology could be detected using only a blood test. They caution that the finding is preliminary.
Banner Sun Health Research Institute in Sun City played a key role, according to Banner Health. Fluid Biomarker Program lab helped process and analyze the plasma samples.
How researchers did it
The team studied blood from 21 former American football players. All of them had given blood while alive and later donated their brains to the BU CTE Center Brain Bank after they died.
- 18 of the 21 players had CTE when their brains were examined after death.
- 16 of those 18 had advanced CTE (McKee stage 3 or 4).
- Two had low-stage CTE (McKee stage 1 or 2).
- Three players did not have CTE.
Researchers compared those results with blood levels of a protein called endogenously cleaved microtubule-binding region tau, or eMTBR-tau, according to Banner. It is a marker of the tau tangles that build up in the brain and are also found in Alzheimer’s disease.
Levels were reported significantly higher in players with high-stage CTE than in those with low-stage CTE or no CTE. The protein was not elevated in either group.
What the discovery means
The result echoes what has already happened with Alzheimer’s disease.
Blood tests now cleared by the FDA for Alzheimer’s have been “revolutionary” for diagnosing patients and designing trials, said Nicholas Ashton, PhD, co-senior author of the study and senior director of Banner Health’s Fluid Biomarker Program. Based on these findings, he wrote, “we are optimistic that the same will be true for CTE.”
Michael Alosco, PhD, corresponding and co-senior author and co-director of clinical research at the BU CTE Center, was more emphatic but also cautious.
“This is a game changer,” Alosco said in a written statement. “We are still in the discovery phase and it’s a small sample size, but if future studies validate these findings, this blood test will allow us to detect CTE with a high level of accuracy and open the doors to clinical trials and treatment.”
That small sample size is one of several limitations the researchers acknowledged, including that the study had limited representation of people with low-stage CTE and no age-matched controls. Still, a separate study published Nov. 13 in JAMA, from a different research group, also found elevated eMTBR-tau243 in people with autopsy-confirmed CTE.
The blood test is not currently FDA-approved and is not available to the public. The researchers say it should not be used to make diagnostic decisions about CTE until validation studies are completed.
People concerned about their brain health should consider being evaluated by a clinician who specializes in neurodegenerative conditions. Finding and addressing risk factors you can change can support brain health and improve quality of life.
