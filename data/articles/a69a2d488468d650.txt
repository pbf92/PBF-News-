TITLE: Engineering antibiotics could expand the pool of new drug candidates
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

As more strains of dangerous bacteria become resistant to existing antibiotics, the quest to find new drugs has become more urgent. In hopes of expanding the pool of antibiotic candidates, MIT researchers have found a way to significantly improve the safety profile of drugs with antimicrobial activity that had previously been discarded.
Many drugs that kill bacteria also harm human cells, which eliminates them from consideration as antibiotics. To overcome that obstacle, the MIT team developed a twofold strategy to counteract the toxicity of those drugs.
First, they attach the drug to a protein that delivers it directly to the site of infection, where it is released. Second, they deliver an “antidote” protein along with the drug, which circulates in the bloodstream and prevents the drug from reaching other organs that it might damage.
In the new study, the researchers demonstrated this approach using a drug called calicheamicin, which is now used to treat some types of cancer but is too toxic for use as an antibiotic. Tests in mice showed that the modified form of the drug could successfully treat bacterial pneumonia while protecting the liver from toxicity.
“The discovery pipeline is advancing at an extraordinary pace, but safety concerns can bring even the most promising programs to an immediate halt. This strategy could offer a chance to let really promising drugs keep going,” says Sangeeta Bhatia, the John and Dorothy Wilson Professor of Health Sciences and Technology and of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science at MIT, and a member of MIT’s Koch Institute for Integrative Cancer Research and the Institute for Medical Engineering and Science (IMES).
Bhatia is the senior author of the new study, which appears today in Nature Communications. MIT Research Scientist Tahoura Samad is the paper’s lead author.
A bigger pool of candidates
To find new antibiotics, scientists have scoured the genomes of microbes and plants, and enlisted artificial intelligence algorithms to identify natural or never-before-seen compounds that might have antimicrobial activity. These efforts have yielded many promising candidates, but also many others that were ruled out due to safety concerns.
“Increasingly, we’re seeing more work focused on antimicrobial discovery and the identification of new compounds,” Samad says. “We see this work as saying: How can we make the best use of this enhanced discovery capacity, so that more of these candidates are reasonable to develop?”
The researchers decided to begin with drugs that have been deemed too toxic to use as antibiotics, but have been adopted for cancer treatment, where doctors and patients are more willing to accept side effects in exchange for killing tumor cells.
“If we could find compounds like that, then the development time would be much faster for those antibiotics because all the preclinical studies and regulatory conversations have already happened,” Bhatia says.
As their first test case, they decided on calicheamicin. This compound, originally isolated from the bacterium Micromonospora echinospora, kills cells by damaging their DNA. It was originally approved as part of a drug conjugate to treat acute myeloid leukemia in 2000, but was later taken off the market due, in part, to issues with toxicity. In 2017, the drug was reapproved, with lower doses and a different dosing schedule.
To make this drug safe enough to use as an antibiotic, the researchers borrowed an approach that has been successfully used for targeted delivery of cancer drugs. In recent years, many cancer drugs have been incorporated into a drug formulation known as an antibody-drug conjugate. By attaching a drug to an antibody that targets a specific tumor protein, researchers can guide it directly to the site of a tumor and reduce off-target side effects.
In this case, the researchers attached the drug molecules to an albumin-binding domain, a small protein that binds to the protein albumin. One of albumin’s functions is to transport other molecules, so it is constantly circulating through the bloodstream. Once the conjugated drug molecule binds to it, it can hitch a ride to the site of infection.
The drug molecule is tethered to the albumin-binding protein by a linker that can only be cleaved by an enzyme called neutrophil elastase. This enzyme is produced by neutrophils, a type of immune cell that tends to accumulate at the site of an infection. When the conjugate reaches the site of infection, the linker is cleaved and the drug can begin killing bacterial cells.
While this conjugated drug was able to clear infections in mice, with reduced toxicity to the liver relative to unconjugated drug, damage levels were still too high. To further reduce the toxicity, the researchers had the idea to deliver a molecule that could act as an antidote to the drug.
To find such an antidote, the researchers went back to the bacterium that produces calicheamicin. To defend itself from destruction, this bacterium also manufactures protective enzymes that neutralize calicheamicin. The researchers chose one, known as CalU19, and engineered it to bind to albumin. This allows it to circulate through the bloodstream and intercept and neutralize any calicheamicin that escapes from the infection site.
“We thought that having this antidote stay in circulation would be a great way to intercept the drug before it gets to the liver. You have this ‘security guard’ waiting for any drug that escapes from the infection environment, and it can soak up the drug before it can do damage,” Samad says.
Safer drugs
The researchers tested the drug-antidote combination in mice infected with Pseudomonas aeruginosa or Staphylococcus aureus, both of which cause pneumonia. In both models, the drug-antidote combination was able to successfully clear the infections, and prevented signs of liver damage.
To evaluate the drug conjugate’s potential against other diseases, the researchers also tested it against several species of bacteria grown in a lab dish, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneunomiae, and found that it could kill all of them.
They are now starting to look for potential antidotes that could be delivered along with other drug compounds, including existing antibiotics that are not prescribed often because they can harm the kidneys or other organs. This approach could also be applied to existing cancer drugs, to help lessen their side effects, the researchers say.
“We’re excited about how we can keep building out this approach where you can deliver a drug along with its antidote,” Samad says.
