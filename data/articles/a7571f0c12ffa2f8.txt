'Long way to go': Partisan divide shadows childhood vaccine support, Gallup poll finds
(TNND) — Public support for childhood vaccines has rebounded a bit over the last couple of years but remains far below where it stood before the pandemic.
And a new Gallup poll shows stark partisan differences in how Americans perceive the importance of childhood vaccinations.
The Gallup poll, published Tuesday from responses collected last month, showed 72% of Americans view childhood vaccinations as extremely or very important.
That’s up from 69% in 2024 but well below 84% in 2019.
Twenty-five years ago, 94% of Americans viewed childhood vaccinations as extremely or very important.
Dr. Juan Salazar, the chair of pediatrics at Vanderbilt Health, called the erosion of support for childhood vaccines “very, very unfortunate.”
And he decried the “megaphone” of misinformation used by social media influencers that have sown doubt about vaccines in the minds of parents and the wider public.
Salazar said falling support for routine childhood vaccines didn’t begin with the pandemic, but he said sentiment for childhood vaccines might’ve taken additional damage at a time of broader distrust of government health recommendations.
“This is not a matter of people not caring for their children. They do,” Salazar said. “But the misinformation leads them to make the wrong decisions, and that's what the problem is."
Salazar said a lack of clarity, or silence about the need to vaccinate, from government officials exacerbates the challenge of getting parents to vaccinate their kids.
And he said a couple of generations of Americans have become complacent about the need to vaccinate, with vaccines working so well that prominent diseases of the past no longer feel like a “real and present danger.”
“We should not be seeing an outbreak of measles in 2026 in the United States,” Salazar said. “That should not be the case. And it is directly related to this decrease in the belief of the importance of childhood vaccinations based on misinformation that has been fueled extensively” through social media.
The Gallup poll was conducted shortly before the U.S. lost its measles elimination status for the first time in 26 years, with cases of the vaccine-preventable disease spreading at their highest rate in over three decades.
Measles was never eradicated in the U.S.
But elimination status meant outbreaks would stay small and contained, and that there would be no local transmission of measles for 12 months or more.
Since the elimination status was reached by the U.S. in 2000, the nation had seen fewer than 100 annual cases 14 times.
But the U.S. recorded 2,289 confirmed measles cases last year, the most since 1991.
And the latest Centers for Disease Control and Prevention figures show 5,838 confirmed measles cases this year.
Jennifer Nuzzo, the director of Brown University's Pandemic Center, said she was encouraged to see the Gallup poll reflect an uptick in support for childhood vaccines.
“Perhaps people are starting to see the peril in taking childhood vaccines for granted,” Nuzzo said.
But she noted how far support had already fallen.
“And particularly, we have a long way to go when it comes to partisan views on this issue,” Nuzzo said. “And so, that is deeply worrisome.”
Only half of Republicans told Gallup that childhood vaccinations are extremely or very important, compared to 91% of Democrats.
From 2001 to 2015, Republicans and Democrats were in lockstep about the importance of childhood vaccines. But a gap began forming a decade ago.
Gallup found a wide partisan gap among views that vaccines are more dangerous than the diseases they are designed to prevent.
Overall, 17% of Americans hold that view, down slightly from 20% two years ago.
But nearly a third of Republicans believe vaccines are more dangerous than the diseases they are designed to prevent, compared to just 5% of Democrats.
And Republicans are more likely to believe vaccines cause autism.
Overall, 16% of Americans think there’s a link between vaccines and autism. Gallup said 29% of Republicans hold that view, compared to 5% of Democrats.
The Trump administration has sought to overhaul childhood vaccine recommendations. And it has questioned whether there is a link between vaccines and autism.
“Measles vaccination is very effective, incredibly safe. There's absolutely no risk of autism. That's been debunked multiple times,” Salazar said. “So, that's a vaccine that I highly recommend, extremely safe, as well as the other childhood vaccines that are currently recommended by the (American Academy of Pediatrics). And so, it is something that I can stand by. The evidence – and that's the important thing is the actual scientific evidence – supports it.”
Nearly all, 96%, of this year’s measles cases have been in unvaccinated people or people with an unknown vaccination status, according to the CDC.
Eleven percent of this year’s cases have resulted in a hospitalization.
Six measles-related deaths have been reported this year in the U.S., the CDC said.
“The vast majority of Americans continue to support measles vaccinations. They think it's important for parents to vaccinate their kids, not just to protect their kids, but to protect society writ large. But over the years, we've seen a decline. And that decline has consequences,” Nuzzo said. “What we're seeing now is people are starting to remember why, see why, vaccines are so important. But we have a long way to go to recover from the partisan rhetoric that has eroded confidence and acceptance of vaccines.”
Nuzzo said partisan rhetoric and misinformation make it harder for health officials to convince the public of the need for vaccines. And she worried that such rhetoric might cause some hesitation among clinicians to promote vaccinations as vocally as they otherwise would.
Both Salazar and Nuzzo said there’s no doubt about the safety or efficacy of routine childhood vaccines.
“But the best thing I can say is I'm a mom of two kids, and I vaccinated both of them,” Nuzzo said. “I'm a mom of two kids who researches every single thing I do before I subject my kids to it. I very happily vaccinated my kids. I'm very glad that as a family we don't have to worry about measles. I'm very glad that as a family, not only do we not have to worry about measles, but we don't have to worry about bringing it to a loved one that may be unable to be protected (by) the vaccine, due to underlying health conditions that make the vaccines not quite as effective in them.”
