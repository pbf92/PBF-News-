TITLE: Why Should I Care About GTA 6?
OUTLET: Kotaku

Twas the night before GTA VI, and all through the site, all the writers are prepping for a long, sleepless night—
No, sorry, I won’t subject you to that. But here at Kotaku, we’re all abuzz in preparation for several busy days (and weeks, probably months!) of Grand Theft Auto VI coverage. We’ve been chatting with each other a lot about what we’re looking forward to, what we’re hoping for, and what our experiences with the series have been in the past, and it’s come up that I, specifically, have never played a GTA game before. Never touched one, not even for a few minutes.
You can laugh, but I don’t think I’m alone. GTA V has sold nearly 235 million copies, sure, but there are three and a half billion gamers or so in the entire world. A lot of us have never played it, or any GTA game, ever. But maybe, with GTA VI, we are thinking we might want to.
It’s an odd experience, being on the outside for this one, having a vague-ish idea of what GTA is from clips and discussions and posts and reviews, but not actually having taken the controller in my hands and experienced what everyone means when they discuss one of the most popular games of all time. It means that, while I’ve seen the various trailers, I don’t actually have a great grasp on what I’m in for here. You just drive a car around? And steal stuff? Why do people care so much about this? I mean, I know why they care about it, but why do they 5-million-copies-every-three-months, take-a-week-off-from-their-job, dump-hundreds-of-dollars-on-a-console, ignore-significant-other-for-days care about this?
I tapped Kotaku‘s two foremost GTA experts, Zack and Carolyn, to explain the phenomenon to me. I hope that those of you out there (there are dozens of us!) who are in the same highly stealable vehicle as me on this one will get something out of their insight!
Rebekah Valentine: What were both of your very first experiences with GTA? How did you come into the series?
Zack Zwiezen: A friend brought over his copy of GTA III on PS2 and booted it up for me and it blew my mind. I was 10? Around that age. I remember walking around that world, getting into a car, driving the streets, and just never hitting a level-end screen or mission. I could just explore this big city forever. It was unlike anything I had played before. I ended up buying the game myself from that kid later using lunch money I saved up, and played many, many hours of GTA III. I’ve been a fan of the franchise ever since.
Carolyn Petit: I had seen the first few top-down games here and there but only ever in passing; they didn’t really catch my interest. It wasn’t until Jeff Gerstmann’s review for Grand Theft Auto III appeared on GameSpot, the one gaming site I read religiously in those days, that I really sat up and took notice as it sounded like something special. I went out and bought the game immediately, and as soon as I fired it up and experienced that opening scene in which you’re busted out of an armored police truck and have to make your getaway through a grungy, modern American city, I knew I was seeing games change forever, in much the same way I had when I first saw Mario run to the right in Super Mario Bros. and people putting quarters on the marquee of a Street Fighter 2 cabinet at my local 7-Eleven.
Zack: I’ve gone back over the years and played the first games, the top down ones. And they’re fun, but playing them now it’s hard to imagine that those games would lead to the biggest video game series in the world.
Carolyn: Right! In so many ways the whole existence of GTA as this massive cultural juggernaut feels like a fluke. Those early games struck me as an edgy curiosity, games that drew attention for “pushing the limits” because they let you play as a criminal and kill civilians but not actually particularly good or interesting as games.
Rebekah: I grew up Catholic, and I always heard about GTA from parents and teachers as this vice-ridden thing. It was the subject of a lot of moral panic, for years! I obviously have a different impression of it now (and don’t really care about playing a game about sin anyway), but I have to admit that that being my worldview for so long really helped ensure I didn’t know anything else, really, about the game. Like, I think in my head for years it was just a game in which you drove around doing murders and robberies, gambling, and seducing women. Which, maybe that’s accurate!
Nowadays, having matured beyond that perspective, I guess I assumed the older games had a lot more going on in them. But…maybe they didn’t? When did these games become, to use Carolyn’s words, “good and interesting”, do you think?
Carolyn: When GTA III came along, I admit it, I couldn’t get enough. And it wasn’t because I loved its crime narrative. That narrative is functional, but it’s not great or anything. What defined my experience of GTA III were all the sort of emergent experiences I had with it that created these moments that were really kind of beautiful. I remember driving along in the game and the rain started to fall while the song “Fade Away” was playing on the radio, and it just felt like this incredible cinematic moment, this glimpse of beauty in the midst of urban decay. It’s moments like that which have really stayed with me from that first game (well, third but you know what I mean), though of course it was also just really fun to push the limits of what was possible, to wreak havoc, try to steal a tank or whatever. It was a game that encouraged play within a context and with systems that no game I’d played before had.
Zack: There are really three distinct eras of GTA. It has always been open world, letting you do whatever, which is still the big appeal of these games. But in the top-down era, things were more cartoonish and wacky. The 3D era, when the games made the leap to PS2, is when they start to mature a bit. Still, GTA III, for all the great vibes, is at times a very goofy game that feels like it’s populated entirely by people pretending to be mobsters, cops, and criminals.
GTA IV, the start of the “HD Era,” is really when GTA evolved into what is today. That’s when the games, starting with Niko’s journey in GTA IV, got more serious and tried to tell more mature stories about real people. I don’t think GTA V nailed that as well as GTA IV, but if you’re asking when they became more than just “driving and shooting cops,” that’s the timeline, I think.
But for me, not sure how Carolyn feels, the reason I keep coming back to GTA and get excited for a new one isn’t to see what story Rockstar is going to tell next. Instead, I’m here for the world. Getting to live in that virtual space and explore it. That’s the real appeal. And a big reason is because Rockstar are far and away the best at making detailed, sprawling open worlds that feel alive.
Carolyn: Oh, absolutely. To be clear, I’ve really liked some aspects of some GTA stories. I think San Andreas has some interesting themes about police corruption and tries to engage with the pressure cooker that Los Angeles was in the ’90s. I’m not saying it handles that wholly successfully or says anything particularly insightful, but it was novel at the time just to see a game make the effort. And there are individual characters and moments in that game that are quite well-written, little moments of beauty amid the silliness and carnage. And yeah, GTA IV remains for now my favorite narrative in the series. Niko had some real shit going on, and his focus on revenge can play out in ways that I found affecting. But yes, what really elevates the GTA games in my view has always been their worlds. They just exude a kind of environmental authenticity that far surpasses what most other games offer. Really, little has changed for me since GTA III in terms of what become my most memorable moments from any GTA game. Those moments usually involve the right song on the radio at the right time when I’m in the right place to just create an immaculate vibe, maybe something about the sunset hitting the ocean just so or whatever it is, in a way that I just find breathtaking.
Rebekah: Maybe the answer to this is just reiterating what you both just said, but like, okay, I get why these games are special, but why are they this special? Why is GTA V selling five million copies every three months? Why is the entire internet batshit crazy over GTA VI? Like I get it, they’re very technically impressive, the freedom is cool, the online has a lot going on, and so forth. But what is it that elevates these games so dang far above every other game in the level of attention and frenzy they’re getting? Is it just what you’re describing here, that it’s that good? Do I have to experience it to understand? Because I’ve had moments like what you’re describing in other games, I feel like!
Carolyn: I don’t think there’s any denying that for a large segment of the population, a big part of the appeal has often been in the ugly stuff. In the GTA III era, it was in dudes laughing with their buds while showing off how they could lure a sex worker into their car, “have sex,” and then murder her to get their money back. I remember seeing a video of GTA V gameplay on YouTube years ago—I think it’s since been deleted—that just had the player attacking the game’s trans sex workers, which were absolutely designed to be ridiculed, and there were comments on the video saying things like “You’re doing god’s work.” There is an ugly side to GTA that can’t be ignored and that I do think was part of what made it so successful, but it also doesn’t define the entirety of what the series is. And I think the series’ success is also in the fact that—I mean I’m sure you’ve had similar experiences in other games, and maybe you’ll play GTA VI and shrug and say, “Yeah, this isn’t anything special,” and of course if you do, fair, but for me at least, again the world, the details, the incredible curation that goes into the soundtracks, there is a way in which these things come together to create a kind of immersion-in-a-modern-day-American-setting experience that other games like, say, the Saints Rows and what have you, can’t come close to.
Zack: Yes. GTA has mostly been a franchise that has punched down and there were people who used the games to recreate terrorist attacks or target women or other horrible shit. And its not really until RDR2 (which isn’t GTA, but shares a lot of its DNA) that I feel like Rockstar has started to try and move away from that crap.
Still, all that said, Rockstar is just the best at making open worlds that reward players for poking at everything.
Stephen Totilo, in his GTA V review, really nailed the idea that these games almost function like scavenger hunts for people. But you aren’t looking for random items or plastic eggs. Instead, what players are hunting down are things to do, details to gawk at, and interactive elements.
I remember in San Andreas, driving up to a train that had stopped and hitting triangle to see if the game would let me steal it and…I could! I drove that train for about 20 minutes. Then I thought, what happens if I go to fast? I started going as fast as I could and I flew off the tracks! These games are filled with these kinds of moments where Rockstar provides a sandbox of opportunities. And most of the time the games, like a good improv comedian, say “Yes, and…”
Carolyn: Right, that lack of limits on what you can do allows for both lots of ugliness and lots of just creative play in ways that are more about testing the reactivity of the world.
Zack: I remember in GTA V shooting the big blimp and it exploded and crashed into the ground and started a fire, and fire trucks showed up and the police were called. It felt so wild to be able to do that. And I bet a lot of players never did!
Carolyn: Haha I never did!
