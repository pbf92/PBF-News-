TITLE: Character.ai's banker wins court approval for its $1.75m sale fee
OUTLET: PPC Land

A federal bankruptcy judge in Delaware on November 23 approved Character.ai's hiring of Houlihan Lokey as its investment banker, overruling the US Trustee, who had argued that a fee of about $2 million was too much for a sale to Disney that was agreed before the bank was hired. At the same hearing, Judge Brendan L. Shannon held back on a second request: keeping secret the name of the investment fund behind Smirks and Pangs Acquisition, the $10 million bidder for the Character.ai app, its AI models and its users' chats. "I am struggling with the question of sealing this," he said, and he set a video hearing on that question for 3 p.m. Eastern on November 24. The hearing on the sales themselves, which had been set for December 3 and 4, moves to December 7 and 8.
In Short
A judge approved the investment bank that is selling off bankrupt Character.ai, even though the government's bankruptcy watchdog said its fee of about $2 million was too high for a Disney deal the company had struck before the bank arrived. The same judge was far less willing to let the $10 million bidder for the app, its AI models and its users' chats keep the name of its financial backer out of the public record, and he will take that question up again on November 24, three hours after bids are due. The court's privacy watchdog told him that users need to know who is buying their personal data, so the backer may yet have to be named before the hearing on the sale, now set for December 7 and 8.
What the judge decided
Three matters were on the agenda in Wilmington before Judge Shannon of the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, in case 26-11603. The court's audio recording of the hearing, filed on the docket the same afternoon as Docket No. 228, starts at 3:16 p.m. Eastern and runs for about 63 minutes. The quotations in this article come from that recording.
The banker's fee
Character Technologies, the company behind Character.ai, filed for Chapter 11 on October 12. Chapter 11 lets a company keep operating while it sells assets or restructures under court supervision, and the advisers it hires are paid from the same pool of money that goes to creditors. That is why their pay needs a judge's approval.
How the fee works
Houlihan's pay is set in an engagement letter. Its terms were described in court by both sides, and according to their accounts the fee is worked out in four steps.
- A monthly fee. Houlihan receives $100,000 a month. In the figures presented at the hearing, monthly fees add up to $300,000, in line with the three-month minimum in the letter.
- The money that counts. The sale fee is based on what the letter calls "aggregate gross consideration," in plain terms the money the bankruptcy estate receives from sales. Money the company received before Houlihan was hired is left out. According to Catherine Lyons of Wilson Sonsini Goodrich & Rosati, arguing for the company, that means "the $5.5 million non-refundable deposit that Disney gave the debtor is not included in the calculation of aggregate gross consideration."
- The sale fee. On aggregate gross consideration up to $25 million, Houlihan earns a flat $1.75 million. Above $25 million, it earns 3% of the amount over that line.
- Paid only at closing. According to Lyons, the sale fee "is only payable on the closing of those transactions."
What that adds up to depends on which deals close.
The $14.5 million is the part of Disney's $20 million price that arrives after the bankruptcy filing. When the US Trustee first objected on November 13, it measured the fee against the full $20 million and put it at 10.25%, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai's banker faced objection to a $2.05m fee on a $20m Disney sale. That hearing was first scheduled for November 17.
The Trustee's case
The objection came from the office of Andrew R. Vara, the US Trustee, a Justice Department official who oversees bankruptcy cases in Delaware. Linda J. Casey, a trial attorney in that office, argued it. Nobody disputed that the company needed a banker or that Houlihan was qualified; according to Lyons, the Trustee was not contesting that Houlihan had no conflicting interests. The fight was over timing and price.
"Houlihan was not retained until after all of the principal terms were done," Casey said. The bank was hired on September 23, five days after the company signed its exclusivity and prepayment agreement with Disney, according to the Trustee's written objection.
Casey then went through what the auction actually tests. She read from the binding term sheet with Disney, filed with the company's sale motion as Docket No. 15:
"For the avoidance of doubt, references in this term sheet to the term licensed transaction refers solely to the IP license and release waiver and covenant not to sue agreement and excludes all employee hiring, onboarding, transition, retention and related agreements described in this section. The licensed transaction will be subject to a competitive process and will be implemented free and clear of all claims, liabilities, interests and encumbrances other than permitted encumbrances."
Put simply, the only part of the Disney deal open to rival bids is a non-exclusive license to the company's technology, plus a promise not to sue that covers claims not already given up before the bankruptcy. Disney's hiring of most of Character.ai's staff is outside the auction. That hiring is an acqui-hire, and Disney expects to spend about $180 million over three years on those employees, as PPC Land reported when Disney paid $20m for Character.ai's IP and $180m for its staff.
"This is categorically not market testing the acqui-hire transaction here," Casey said. She recalled that "there had been a previous Google transaction which was 2.7 billion," a reference to Google's $2.7 billion deal with Character.ai in August 2024. And because the license Disney is getting is non-exclusive, she said, "it's difficult to see how you market test a non-exclusive license as you can sell as many non-exclusive licenses as you want."
On the money, she was blunt. "If the Disney deal is the only deal that closes, the debtor will receive $14.5 million in its post-petition sale that Houlihan did not acquire and Houlihan will get $2 million. And the United States Trustee does not think that that is reasonable."
Her objection was aimed at approval under section 328 of the Bankruptcy Code. Approval under that section fixes a professional's pay terms in advance, and the court can change them later only if they prove improvident in light of developments that could not have been anticipated. Casey argued that it was too early to lock the terms in, because the sale itself is far from certain. "We have objections to the sale at all. We have objections asking Your Honor to put in significant privacy restrictions that could scare away a person even if you approve the sale."
She drew a line between pay and this pay. "We're not saying they don't deserve any compensation. We're saying this compensation is not reasonable and should not be approved today under 328."
Judge Shannon pushed back during her argument. "The fact that they didn't get a competing bidder to Disney doesn't mean that the Disney transaction wasn't market tested. They just didn't find another bidder and they have a bidder for other assets," he said. Her point, he added, "seems to me to be a sale objection," one he was happy to hear at the sale hearing.
The company's answer
Lyons said the case needed a specialist. "This is not a run-of-the-mill retail case that can be liquidated by just any banker." She also gave the first figures on the sale process since the bankruptcy began: "Since the petition date, Houlihan has conducted outreach to at least 185 parties. There's 53 parties under NDA and in the data room." In plain terms, 53 potential buyers had signed confidentiality agreements and could see the company's private documents. Before the bankruptcy, a different bank had approached about 28 potential buyers and produced one proposal that could not be acted on, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai drew one unusable offer from 28 potential buyers.
Her central argument was about how bankers are paid. "First, there's no requirement in any other case that a banker bring a bidder to the process to earn a fee." The Disney deal, she said, "was always predicated on the occurrence of an in-court marketing process," and Houlihan had run that process. It had also found a second bidder: the process "resulted in the designation of an additional stalking horse purchaser on November 14th."
On the size of the fee, Lyons put the two-deal scenario at 8.367% and said "the percentage at issue here is well below typical compensation structures that are routinely approved in Delaware." She offered the judge a chart of minimum fees in other Delaware cases. In her reply to Casey, she defended the value of marketing a license that can be sold again and again: "It could potentially be sold infinitely, but each time it is marketed and someone pays money for it, we learn its value and we learn whether its value contributes to the estate." She also conceded the scale of the payment in the weakest case: "The fees in this case are market, even if they are a substantial amount of money relative to a $14.5 million kind of worst-case scenario closing purchase price."
The company's evidence was a supplemental declaration from its chief executive, Colin M. Adams, filed on November 20 as Docket No. 204. It was admitted without objection, and Casey said she would not cross-examine him. Gregg A. Steinman of McDermott Will & Schulte, for the creditors' committee, which represents the company's unsecured creditors, said: "The committee does not object to Houlihan's retention. We had some informal comments that we were able to resolve with the debtors, so we don't have a position today." The company will submit a revised order reflecting comments from the committee and the Trustee.
The ruling
"Here's what we're going to do. I'm going to overrule the objection," Judge Shannon said. He called the Trustee's concerns "squarely within the trustee's wheelhouse" and said they had made the company and the bank build a proper record. "But based upon the record that I have before me today, including Mr. Adams' supplemental declaration, I am prepared to approve and authorize the retention of Houlihan."
He gave five reasons.
- A narrow question. "I am focused exclusively on what I'm being asked to do today, which is simply to approve and authorize the retention of a financial advisor, an investment banker for the debtor."
- No dispute on need. "So the debtor needs a financial advisor, an investment banker, and clearly there's no dispute that Houlihan Lokey has the requisite experience and skills and resources to provide the services necessary."
- Common practice. He started "with the proposition that it is not rare for a debtor to come in with a transaction in hand" and still hire a banker to test it. "I believe Judge Walrath from a number of years ago has a published decision on that issue," he said, without naming it.
- The amount. "With respect to the amounts, again, the trustee has raised concerns with respect to the potential amount that would go to Houlihan Lokey. But I'm not necessarily troubled by that."
- The creditors' view. "I place some significance on the position of the official committee of unsecured creditors that I'm sure has vetted this and is satisfied to permit it to proceed."
He approved the hiring under sections 327 and 328 and said he would enter the order once it is submitted. The Trustee's doubts about how Disney's deal was marketed are not lost: they "are certainly fully preserved for purposes of a contested sale hearing," he said.
