TITLE: Colorectal ESD Bleeding Prediction Models Show High Bias in Systematic Review
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic review and critical appraisal has found that existing statistical models designed to predict delayed bleeding after colorectal endoscopic submucosal dissection suffer from pervasive methodological flaws, creating an overall high risk of bias across all published tools despite acceptable derivation discrimination. Endoscopic submucosal dissection is an established minimally invasive technique for en bloc resection of early colorectal cancer and precancerous lesions, achieving en bloc resection rates between 89.2 percent and 92.5 percent. Compared with conventional surgery, it offers less trauma, faster recovery, and shorter hospital stays. However, technical complexity, prolonged operative time, and submucosal dissection elevate the risk of vascular and muscular injury. Delayed bleeding is among the most common complications, presenting as melena or hematochezia and potentially progressing to hemorrhagic shock. Published literature estimates its incidence between 2.80 percent and 5.37 percent, prolonging hospitalization, increasing costs, and threatening patient safety. Despite intraoperative hemostasis, the lack of effective risk assessment instruments hinders targeted individualized management for high-risk patients.
To synthesize available evidence, investigators prospectively registered their review protocol in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251252241) before initiating literature searches. Two researchers searched five databases through March 2026: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure. The strategy used keywords covering delayed bleeding, prediction models, risk assessment, nomograms, and endoscopic resection. The search yielded 25,177 records. After removing duplicates, 12,741 records underwent title and abstract screening, leaving 53 articles for full-text review. Following full-text screening, 48 publications were excluded: 21 examined outcomes other than delayed bleeding after colorectal endoscopic submucosal dissection, 13 were not predictive modeling studies, 10 lacked diagnostic criteria for delayed bleeding, and four had non-extractable data. Consequently, five studies reporting five discrete predictive models were included.
The review followed the Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modeling Studies checklist and the population, intervention, comparator, outcome, timing, and setting framework. Eligible studies were observational investigations in adult patients aged 18 years or older undergoing colorectal endoscopic submucosal dissection that developed or validated multivariable prediction models or scoring systems. Four included studies were retrospective cohorts and one was a prospective cohort. Derivation sample sizes ranged from 302 to 1,189 patients, and delayed bleeding incidence ranged from 2.90 percent to 9.90 percent. All five studies utilized binary multivariable logistic regression, incorporating between three and five predictors in their final models.
The derivation cohorts demonstrated varied outcome definitions and predictor combinations. Qian and colleagues evaluated 302 retrospective Chinese patients, where 59.6 percent were aged 60 years or older. They recorded 30 delayed bleeding cases, representing a 9.90 percent incidence, defined as resection site bleeding occurring 12 to 14 days post-procedure with hematochezia and a hemoglobin reduction exceeding 20 grams per liter. Their five predictors were lesion count, lesion size, lesion location, submucosal fibrosis degree, and intraoperative bleeding, achieving a derivation area under the curve of 0.831 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.717 to 0.944, specificity of 96.13 percent, sensitivity of 60.00 percent, and a Hosmer-Lemeshow test statistic of 1.975 with a p-value of 0.853. Bian and colleagues evaluated 412 retrospective Chinese patients with a mean age of 58.40 years, identifying 37 bleeding cases, reflecting an 8.98 percent incidence defined by a postoperative hemoglobin drop of at least 2 grams per deciliter or persistent rectal bleeding. Their model incorporated rectal location, large lesion size, and prolonged operative time, demonstrating a concordance index of 0.89 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.87 to 0.94, and positive net benefit across decision curve analysis threshold probabilities between 1 percent and 70 percent.
The remaining investigations evaluated diverse derivation cohorts and predictors. Albouys and colleagues prospectively studied 940 French patients with a mean age of 69.00 years, documenting 75 bleeding episodes for an 8.00 percent incidence, defined as hematochezia occurring between 6 hours and 30 days post-procedure requiring hospitalization extension, readmission, transfusion, repeat endoscopy, surgery, or angiography. Their model incorporated age of 75 years or older, antithrombotic use, rectal location, lesion size exceeding 50 millimeters, and an American Society of Anesthesiologists score of III or IV, yielding a derivation area under the curve of 0.751 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.690 to 0.812, fair calibration, a discrimination slope of 0.07 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.04 to 0.13, and a Hosmer-Lemeshow test statistic of 7.652 with a p-value of 0.47. Li and colleagues retrospectively analyzed 406 Chinese patients with a mean age of 65.9 years, finding 37 bleeding cases for a 9.10 percent incidence based on dizziness, hematemesis, melena, and hemoglobin loss exceeding 20 grams per liter; their model integrated rectal location, diameter of at least 3 centimeters, severe submucosal fibrosis, and obvious intraoperative bleeding, achieving a concordance index of 0.819. Seo and colleagues retrospectively evaluated 1,189 Korean patients with a median age of 63.0 years and interquartile range of 55.0 to 69.5 years, reporting 34 bleeding events, reflecting a 2.90 percent incidence requiring emergency visits, hospitalization, or re-intervention. Incorporating rectosigmoid location, tumor size of 30 millimeters, and antiplatelet agents other than aspirin alone, their derivation area under the curve was 0.726 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.645 to 0.808, calibration slope of 0.914, intercept of 0.002, and a Hosmer-Lemeshow test statistic of 1.482 with a p-value of 0.830.
Model validation demonstrated pronounced divergence between internal testing and external application. Four predictive algorithms reported internal validation discrimination between 0.739 and 0.870. Qian and colleagues reported an internal validation area under the curve of 0.821 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.664 to 0.979, sensitivity of 90.00 percent, specificity of 75.82 percent, and a Hosmer-Lemeshow test statistic of 8.510 with a p-value of 0.203. Bian and colleagues achieved an internal validation area under the curve of 0.87 in 175 patients with a mean age of 59.13 years. Albouys and colleagues recorded an internal validation area under the curve of 0.739 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.671 to 0.805 across their 940 patients. In this external cohort, where 10 bleeding cases occurred for a 2.41 percent incidence, discriminative capacity dropped sharply to an area under the curve of 0.634 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.472 to 0.796 and a Hosmer-Lemeshow test statistic of 1.666 with a p-value of 0.435.
Across the five models, predictor selection revealed distinct patterns and underlying physiological mechanisms. Lesion location and lesion size were retained in all five models, establishing them as core features. The systematic review highlighted that lesion size directly determines the postoperative mucosal defect area, with previous research indicating that each 10-millimeter increase in wound diameter elevates delayed bleeding risk by approximately 30 percent. Anatomically, the rectum and right colon feature rich vascularity and dense lymphatic drainage that heighten exudation and reperfusion pressure, while distal rectal wounds endure continuous anal sphincter compression that disturbs local revascularization. Secondary predictors included intraoperative bleeding, antithrombotic therapy, and submucosal fibrosis, which each appeared in two models. Intraoperative bleeding reflects procedural difficulty and impaired electrocoagulation eschar formation, predisposing tissue to hyperfibrinolytic sloughing, though operational definitions varied widely across studies. Antithrombotic regimens directly impaired hemostasis, yet drug classes and discontinuation protocols lacked standardization. Submucosal fibrosis reduced tissue compliance, compromised injection lifting, distorted microvessels, and impaired electrocoagulation. Solitary predictors included an American Society of Anesthesiologists score of III or IV, age of 75 years or older, prolonged operative time, and lesion count.
Methodological quality was appraised using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool, or PROBAST, evaluating 20 signaling questions across participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis domains. All five studies exhibited overall high risk of bias. In the participants domain, four retrospective studies had high risk due to non-standardized historical records from restricted populations, while the prospective cohort by Albouys had low risk. In the predictors domain, all five studies had unclear risk because none reported assessor blinding. In the outcome domain, all five studies carried high risk due to unblinded outcome assessment or post hoc data access. In the analysis domain, all five studies carried high risk: all relied on univariate screening with p-values below 0.05 or 0.20, none addressed missing data, three omitted data complexity evaluations, one had inadequate sample size, and two lacked cross-validation.
Despite high risk of bias, all five studies showed low concern regarding clinical applicability under PROBAST. However, clinical utility depends on predictor timing: intraoperative predictors suit postoperative surveillance, whereas preoperatively accessible predictors like lesion size and location could guide patient consent. Review limitations included marked heterogeneity precluding meta-analysis, English-language restrictions, and scarce external validation. The authors concluded that existing prediction models for delayed bleeding after colorectal endoscopic submucosal dissection require cautious interpretation due to pervasive methodological bias and suboptimal external performance, emphasizing that future research must adhere to PROBAST standards, define clinical timing, apply multiple imputation, avoid univariate screening, and conduct multicenter external validations.
