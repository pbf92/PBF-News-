TITLE: Google patent describes an AI TV channel that stars the viewer
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google LLC has a pending US patent application for a television feature that turns a viewer's watching habits into videos made by artificial intelligence, with the viewer cast as the lead character. The United States Patent and Trademark Office published the application, US 2026/0370899 A1, titled "Creating a channel of video experiences tailored to a user," on November 19, 2026. It was filed on May 13, 2025, and names two inventors in San Jose, California: Aravind Reddy Ayuluri and Prameela Kavya Thummuru.
In Short
Google has applied for a US patent on a TV channel full of AI-made videos built from what a person watches, their age and gender, where they are, and a photo of them, sometimes with their own face and voice as the star. It matters to advertisers and TV platforms because three of the 20 claims cover stitching ads into those videos before they reach the screen. It is an application, not a granted patent, and it does not show that Google has built or plans to launch any such product.
An application, not a product
A published patent application is a request. It tells the public what a company wants to protect, and the patent office has not yet decided whether to grant any of it. Claims are often narrowed, rewritten or rejected during examination, and many granted patents never become products. Nothing in this filing says Google has built the system, tested it with viewers, or set a launch date.
The document also never names a Google product. Google TV, Gemini, YouTube and Veo do not appear anywhere in its 28 pages. The drawings show a generic TV app with four tabs, "Home," "Apps," "Library" and "My Channel," and a living room with a smart speaker, a phone and a remote control.
The timing follows the standard rule. US applications are normally published 18 months after filing, and May 13, 2025 plus 18 months is November 13, 2026; publication came the following Thursday, 555 days after filing, according to a PPC Land calculation. The cover page lists no earlier provisional application.
The patent office classified it under H04N 21/2668, the group for creating a channel for a dedicated end-user group, with targeted commercials based on viewer profiles as the scheme's example. Further codes cover user data management, the viewer's geographic location, the viewer's demographic data and video clients built into portable devices.
The problem Google says it is solving
The filing begins with a complaint about ordinary recommendation engines. According to the application, a system that suggests shows based on past viewing "may not fully consider that the user may watch diverse media content across various genres," or that the user "may have fluctuating interests during a period of time (e.g., a year)."
Its stated answer is not a better list of existing shows. The application describes "a media content recommendation system utilizing generative artificial intelligence (AI) to create the custom channel of video experiences tailored to the user." The system would study a viewer's history and spot patterns. The example the filing gives is that "the user may watch adult movies on Saturday nights, and kid movies on Friday nights."
From there, the description goes further than recommendation. According to paragraph 36, the system "may generate multiple customized media content items (e.g., videos, short form videos, movies, etc.) featuring the user as the protagonist." Those videos "may include an avatar of the user and/or the voice of the user."
What the claims actually cover
The legal reach of a patent sits in its claims, not in the description. This application has 20 claims. Three are independent: claim 1 (a method), claim 11 (instructions stored on a computer-readable medium) and claim 16 (a system). The other 17 add details to one of those three.
Claim 1 reads, in full:
"A method comprising: obtaining, by a server computer, data related to preferences of a user; obtaining, by the server computer and from a computing device, contextual information related to the user; obtaining demographic data related to the user; generating, by the server computer, a prompt for use by a large language model that is based on the data related to preferences of the user, the contextual information related to the user, and the demographic data related to the user; sending the prompt to the large language model for use in generating a customized media content item for storing in a customized content repository; obtaining, by the server computer and from the customized content repository, the customized media content item; and sending the customized media content item to the computing device for playing on the computing device."
In plain terms, claim 1 has seven steps. A server collects what a person likes, details about the device they are using, and demographic data about them. It writes a prompt, meaning the written instruction given to an AI model. It sends that prompt to a large language model, stores the video that comes back, and plays it on the person's device.
What claim 1 leaves out matters as much. The viewer's face, the viewer's voice and the viewer as protagonist are not required by any claim. Those features appear only in the description and drawings, as examples. The broadest claim would cover any AI-generated video built from preferences, device details and demographics, starring anyone or no one.

How it works, according to the filing
The application describes three linked processes, shown as diagrams in FIGS. 3, 4 and 5. Read together, they run in seven stages.
- Watching. When a viewer picks a show, the TV app sends the title and the watch time to Google's TV application server. The server fetches the title's metadata, which the filing lists as "a title, genre(s), actor(s), a runtime, and a URL."
- Scoring each part of a show. The TV app can report watch time for "one or more periods, sections, chapters, or parts" of a title. The server assigns a genre to each watched part and keeps the genre with the most watched parts. The filing's example is a horror comedy: if a viewer watched more of the comedy sections than the horror sections, the system records comedy.
- Starting the job. An "event trigger" starts generation. According to the filing, this can be a request the viewer makes in the TV app's settings, or a schedule set on the server side, "weekly, monthly, yearly."
- Gathering the person. A "customized content service" pulls the viewer's data and demographic data. A "user representation creator" requests a picture of the viewer and builds "a digital representation of the user (e.g., an avatar of the user 101)."
- Writing the prompt. A "prompt generator" combines all of it into a prompt and sends it to a separate AI server.
- Making and storing the video. A "customized content generator" produces the video and stores it with its own web address in a "customized content repository."
- Showing and, optionally, selling it. When the viewer selects "My Channel," the app shows tiles for each generated video. Picking a tile fetches that video's address and streams it. In some versions, the TV app sends the address to an advertising server, where an "ad stitcher" inserts "one or more advertisements" and streams the result to the TV.
The drawings make the result concrete. FIG. 1B shows a screen headed "John's Channel" with four tiles: "A crime/comedy caper," "A fun romantic comedy," "A sci-fi adventure" and "An action/thriller experience."
Mixing genres into one story
The filing describes how the system decides what to make. A machine learning model groups the genres a person watches into clusters "based on, for example, the compatibility and potential for narrative synergy for the genres in each group." Its examples are crime, thriller and action in one group, and comedy, romance and drama in another. If the viewer watches more of the first group, its videos get priority. The system can make several videos from the same group so the channel has variety.
The viewer's face and voice
According to paragraph 52, the avatar is built from "photos, videos, etc. of the user" taken from the viewer's phone or from stored account data. The account record itself may hold "a name for the user, an image or picture of the user, and/or an age demographic for the user."
The voice has a less obvious source. The filing describes a voice-based TV assistant that receives spoken commands through the remote, a phone or a smart speaker. According to paragraph 47, that assistant "may provide the voice data to the television application server 106 for storage in memory device(s) 282 in association with the user," and the system "may use the voice data when generating customized media content." The generator then "may synthesize the voice of the user for the dialogue of a protagonist." Put simply, the commands a person speaks to the TV could become the raw material for their character's lines.
The data the system would use
The application lists the information it would draw on. Every item below comes from the filing.
The household line is easy to miss. According to paragraph 56, the system may check whether another person on the same device is "elderly (e.g., over 65 years of age)" or "a child (e.g., under the age of 13 years)," and that information "may be used to fine tune recommended customized media content" for the account holder.
Two kinds of prompt
The application shows two ways to write the instruction for the AI model, and it lists the trade-offs of each.
The first, in FIG. 6, is a fixed template with blanks. It opens: "You are a customized video generating model. Generate a customized video for a user where the user will play the protagonist." The blanks hold the viewer's name, age, gender, device and "[link to user's profile picture OR base64 encoded image]," then a ranked list of "top watches" and preferred genres, with "Comedy" and "Thriller" as the examples. The instructions tell the model to "Stick to the user as the protagonist for the whole video" and to match the format of the viewer's favourite videos, "taking into account aspects like aspect ratio, editing style, and overall presentation. While direct replication isn't necessary, the generated video should feel familiar and resonate with [user_name] viewing habits." The last line reads: "Output a video file in [video_format]."
The "top watches" are not titles. They are "video embeddings," which the filing defines as "compact numerical representations of video content that captures visual, audio, and temporal information," kept in "vector-based databases."
The application names the weakness of this approach itself. If a viewer "chooses not to allow the storing of data in the user data repository," the template may be left with blanks or default values, and the videos will reflect the viewer less.
The second approach, in FIG. 7, uses a second AI model to write the prompt. This version can pull in live events. The filing's example is a cricket fan during a tournament: "Current Context: The Cricket World Cup is underway! India just won a thrilling match against Australia. Player1 scored a century." Then: "Recent Activity: User recently watched a video about Player1's batting technique." The generated instruction suggests: "Perhaps [user_name] could be cheering for India in the stands or interacting with Player 1 in some way."
That example would put a real sports figure into a generated video alongside the viewer's avatar. The filing says nothing about permission from the athlete, the league or the broadcaster.
It is also open about cost. According to paragraph 101, running a second model to write prompts "may increase an infrastructure cost" and may need "additional processing units (e.g., tensor processing units)."
