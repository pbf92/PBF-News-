TITLE: Anthropic CEO rejects giving machines control over human governance
OUTLET: Interesting Engineering

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has rejected suggestions that humanity should surrender its political authority to artificial intelligence. He argues that increasingly capable AI systems can transform society without replacing democratic institutions or human decision-making. His clarification follows a November 25 Axios report examining disagreements among technology leaders over humanity’s future relationship with superintelligent machines.
Amodei addressed the controversy in a lengthy post on X. He said his public writing has consistently supported democratic oversight of AI. Critics, he argued, have confused his support for automating complex tasks with advocating machine-led governance.
Democracy remains the boundary
Amodei compared arguments for AI-led governance with historical defenses of autocracy. Advocates have long argued that centralized authority enables faster decisions than democratic institutions. However, he believes efficiency cannot justify sacrificing political legitimacy or people’s right to determine their future.
His 2024 essay, Machines of Loving Grace, explored how advanced AI could strengthen democratic participation. He proposed using AI to help citizens understand public policy, resolve disagreements, and identify common ground. The objective was to improve democratic decision-making, not transfer political authority to machines.
He revisited these concerns in The Adolescence of Technology, published in January 2026. The essay examined how powerful AI could enable authoritarian governments and threaten individual freedoms. Amodei also warned against technology companies accumulating excessive political power. His latest clarification extends that argument to companies attempting to hand authority to AI itself.
Automation creates difficult tradeoffs
Amodei’s position becomes more nuanced when discussing individual tasks. He supports AI handling increasingly complex work across medicine, transportation, software development, and energy. Such applications could accelerate research, improve productivity, and save lives.
However, he sees several reasons to restrict certain deployments. One concern involves recursive self-improvement, where AI systems help develop increasingly capable successors. In his September 2026 essay, We Must Pace the Frontier, Amodei warned that this process could outpace researchers’ ability to understand and control AI development.
Anthropic also does not consider it safe for Claude to develop successive frontier models entirely autonomously without meaningful human supervision. Amodei believes safety research must keep pace with improvements in AI capabilities.
Employment presents another challenge. End-to-end automation could disrupt jobs faster than workers can adapt. Some deployments might therefore require delays. He also cautioned against blindly trusting AI-generated alignment research.
AI leaders face accountability questions
Amodei also criticized technology executives who promote slogans about human agency and control. He questioned whether those commitments translate into meaningful differences in their products and deployment decisions.
He defended Anthropic’s refusal to let its models power fully autonomous weapons, despite potential legal consequences in its dispute with the US government. He presented that decision as evidence that the company’s stated principles carry practical consequences.
Amodei believes decisions about automation should involve governments, democratic institutions, AI developers, and individual consumers. No single authority should make every decision.
His central argument separates technological capability from political legitimacy. AI may eventually perform increasingly complex work, but that does not give machines the right to decide humanity’s future.
