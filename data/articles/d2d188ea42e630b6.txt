TITLE: NUS Medicine study identifies potential limits of breast cancer screening policies across Southeast Asia
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

In Southeast Asia, breast cancer screening could have limited potential to reduce breast cancer mortality due to screening age eligibility not aligning with the actual ages at which women develop the disease, according to a study by the Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine, National University of Singapore (NUS Medicine).
Analysing breast cancer mortality data and screening policies across 11 Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) countries, researchers led by Professor Evangelos Kontopantelis, Department of Medicine, NUS Medicine, found that the proportion of breast cancer deaths associated with diagnoses occurring within screening-eligible ages varied considerably, ranging from 40.6 per cent in Cambodia to 72.6 per cent in Brunei Darussalam. In Singapore, the proportion was estimated at 50.2 per cent, the second-lowest among the countries studied. The findings suggest that screening programmes could potentially reach more women who may benefit from earlier cancer detection and treatment if their age eligibility criteria better reflect the ages at which breast cancer occurs in each country.
Published in eClinicalMedicine, the study also projected that even modest improvements in screening attendance could help reduce breast cancer deaths. Under a five-percentage-point increase in screening attendance, the study estimated that approximately 7,300 cumulative deaths could be averted in Indonesia and 4,400 in the Philippines, between 2026 and 2050. These reductions reflect Indonesia's large population and breast cancer mortality burden, as well as the Philippines' relatively high breast cancer mortality rates and proportion of deaths associated with diagnoses within screening-eligible ages. The findings suggest that while encouraging more women to attend breast cancer screening remains important, countries may also need to periodically review their screening age criteria to ensure that programmes reflect local patterns of breast cancer.
Improving the reach of breast cancer screening
The study combined breast cancer mortality estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 study with country-specific screening ages and attendance rates. Using probabilistic modelling, the researchers assessed how screening eligibility and changes in participation could influence breast cancer deaths through 2050, while accounting for uncertainty in the data and the time between diagnosis and death. The findings showed substantial differences between countries. In Singapore, where the national mammography screening programme targets women aged 50 to 69, approximately half of breast cancer deaths were associated with diagnoses within screening-eligible ages.
Extending the upper screening-age limit by five years produced the largest increases in this proportion in Cambodia (16.4 percentage points) and Indonesia (13.5 percentage points), where existing screening programmes target women aged 15 to 49 and 30 to 50, respectively. This suggests that including older women in screening programmes could help reach a larger proportion of those at risk. Under projected mortality patterns for 2050, fewer than 25 per cent of breast cancer deaths in both countries would be associated with diagnoses within their current screening-eligible ages, suggesting that screening policies may need to be reviewed as populations age.
Prof Kontopantelis said, "Increasing screening attendance is important, but our findings show that participation alone is not enough. If screening programmes do not adequately cover the ages at which breast cancer is commonly diagnosed, their potential impact will remain limited. Our study provides a framework for countries to identify these gaps and consider how screening policies could better reflect local disease patterns."
Tailoring screening policies to local needs
The team found that modest improvements in screening attendance could avert thousands of deaths, particularly in populous countries such as Indonesia and the Philippines. When adjusted for population size, the greatest projected mortality reductions were observed in the Philippines and Thailand. However, they emphasised that screening eligibility and participation must be considered alongside timely diagnosis, referral and treatment. The proportion of deaths associated with screening-eligible ages should not be interpreted as the proportion preventable through screening.
Dr Alicia Ong, Department of Medicine, NUS Medicine, said, "Breast cancer screening programmes need to consider not only how many women participate, but also whether they are reaching those who could benefit. Our findings suggest that screening strategies should be tailored to each country's disease patterns and healthcare resources, supported by efforts to improve awareness, access to screening and timely treatment." Dr Ong is also a Family Physician, Associate Consultant, at National University Polyclinics.
The team hopes the findings will help policymakers identify opportunities to improve screening participation and review age eligibility criteria. Their modelling framework could also be applied to other cancers and geographical settings, with the team currently working towards its application to colorectal cancer.
