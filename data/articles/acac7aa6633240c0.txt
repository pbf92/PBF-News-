When Rockstar Games unveiled footage from its sure-to-be-a-blockbuster video game Grand Theft Auto VI this summer, a micro-controversy broke out: Could playing this game within the confines of a monogamous relationship constitute cheating? As reviews have noted, fostering a romance between the game’s two protagonists, Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos, is a huge component of the game. “Whether you’re controlling Jason or, eventually, Lucia, much of what you can do—apart from activities routine to GTA like stealing cars and robbing banks—is taking action to care for and nurture your romantic partner,” wrote the New York Times’ Yussef Cole in a review that also quotes bell hooks’ seminal work All About Love. Unlike past entries in the game franchise, Cole notes that, in VI, “favoring violence and chaos and nihilistically going it alone hurts the relationship and potentially destroys it.”
Many clocked the romantic bent of this installment on sight. Perhaps most famously, in an August TikTok that amassed nearly 9 million views, Shelby Haas-Sapp proclaimed, “In absolutely no human way is my fiancé playing GTA VI. … You literally have to maintain a relationship with a girl in the video game and text her, and she’ll get mad if you don’t text her back and you have to take her on dates and you can have sex with her. … Don’t let your boyfriend or fiancé or husband play GTA VI.” Haas-Sapp’s then-fiancé, now husband, Alex Eubank, posted his own TikTok confirming that he wouldn’t be playing it. Meanwhile, a September YouGov poll of more than 4,000 British adults found that 4 percent of men who responded and 7 percent of women who did answered that it’s possible to cheat on someone with a video game character. Now, that’s a vast minority, but still more than zero, which is what you might expect if you are living in reality. A more considerable number answered that while this doesn’t constitute cheating, they weren’t OK with it: 13 percent of men and 24 percent of women. IGN, the Guardian, and Yahoo are among the outlets that have taken time out of their busy schedules to mull over whether button-mashing and infidelity are one and the same.
All this discourse raises an important question: What the hell are people talking about? It seems clear that there is a literal realm of difference between what happens in a fictional context onscreen and what happens in bed among real people of actual flesh. To suggest otherwise risks legislating thought crimes. It is to adhere to the shame-instilling, practically impossible standard of the New Testament, wherein Jesus Christ proclaimed, “You have heard that it was said, ‘You shall not commit adultery.’ But I tell you that anyone who looks at a woman lustfully has already committed adultery with her in his heart.” Perhaps it is telling that in his video, Eubank used words like “covenant,” “sacrificially serve,” and “humbly submit.”
To be fair, nothing is above rigorous thought, and people seem consumed by what constitutes cheating now more than ever. There is plenty of talk about “micro-cheating,” a newish term which describes behaviors that stop short of actual sex with another person but are somewhere on the spectrum of infidelity. These things may include flirtation, social media attention, or, according to a couples therapist quoted by Vogue, “a glance, a laugh, or non-sexual touching that’s too familiar or intimate.” That quote reveals a glaring issue within this concept: “too familiar or intimate” is highly subjective unless an agreement has already been arranged among partners. Without explicit communication, one person’s “too familiar” may be another’s completely benign.
“I wouldn’t say I’m surprised that some people would consider it cheating if their partner played a video game in which their character had another partner,” Justin Lehmiller, a senior research fellow at the Kinsey Institute and author of Tell Me What You Want: The Science of Sexual Desire and How It Can Help You Improve Your Sex Life, as well as the upcoming Mindf*ck: How to Hack Your Brain for More Pleasure, Deeper Connection, and Hotter Sex, told me. “If your partner believes that your character is a representation of you in some way, then they might interpret your character’s behaviors as a reflection of your desires.”
Last year, Lehmiller wrote a piece for Psychology Today sharing the results of a Kinsey Institute/DatingAdvice.com study of 2,000 adults titled “The State of Us: National Study of Modern Love and Dating.” The results are a mixed bag of predictable and surprising. Eighty-four percent of respondents said that having sex with someone else counts as infidelity, but then 42 percent said that “spending a lot of time with someone else” does as well. “Buying meals or gifts for someone else” constituted cheating to 34 percent of those surveyed, while 31 percent answered that simply fantasizing about someone else was adulterous.. Online behavior returned similar results. Forty-one percent of respondents said that they would feel cheated on if their partner sent DMs to attractive people on social media. Thirty-three percent would feel that way if their partner subscribed to someone’s OnlyFans and 20 percent would if their partner watched porn. Never mind that partnered people who crave novelty within monogamous relationships often find it in things like OnlyFans and porn. They consume this stuff specifically to avoid cheating—or, at least, their conception of it. For the sake of maintaining that words actually mean something, it would be useful to distinguish between things we’d rather our partner not do and actual cheating. If everything is cheating, nothing is.
I see these and adjacent issues come up quite frequently in the sex-advice column I write for Slate, How to Do It. Does using a Fleshlight constitute cheating? Is it ethical to fantasize about a friend? I get the sense that for many, there’s an undercurrent of paranoia when it comes to contemporary thought on sex, a fear of breaching purity standards that then create new standards whose ostensible goal—makingyour partner the only outlet for not just sexual activity but any kind of positive attention at all—is impossible to maintain. These standards may seem current, as they’ve been created in response to rapidly evolving technology and communication, but they can also promote secrecy and shame over extremely human impulses.
Not all thinking about this kind of thing is oppressive. A memorable scene in Joachim Trier’s 2021 film The Worst Person in the World features a meet-cute between future lovers, protagonist Julie (Renate Reinsve) and Eivind (Herbert Nordrum). They’re both partnered, and so their flirtation consists of devising ways they can walk right up to the edge without actually cheating: smelling each other’s armpits, going in for (but not actually doing) a kiss, biting, peeing in front of each other. (Miranda July’s 2024 book All Fours features characters performing similar mental gymnastics.) “Where do you draw the line?” asks Julie, as she stands at the threshold she’s destined to clear. How long can one claim innocence when the writing is on the wall?
This can be a great thought exercise when it involves real people in the real world. But when it regards fictional characters who exist as binary data rendered on screens, we need to put our foot down. It seems pretty clear that a reasonable line to draw is one that separates animate and inanimate beings. There is, at least for the time being, a great difference between something happening in real life and onscreen. There is a great difference between a sex toy and actual human genitalia. It may feel that these distinctions are collapsing, and I suppose that in some ways they are, but they have not vanished. If we cut out video games, what’s next? Romance novels? Regular novels? Movies and TV? Daydreaming? Making eye contact with someone? Does anyone think that a world in which our partner consumes our every thought and desire is a good thing? No one can be everything to another person, and even if they could, who would want to live in such bondage?
Though Lehmiller stopped short of explaining exactly why people’s definition of cheating seems to be expanding, he did offer a potential theory, at least for some. “Some research finds that attachment anxiety (i.e., fear of abandonment) is linked to taking more expansive views of infidelity, which makes sense,” he wrote. “If you’re always afraid your partner is going to leave you, it might be tempting to filter any behavior in which your partner is directing their attention elsewhere, even to a fictional character, as a sign that they’re not satisfied.” That’s a cause to look within, instead of legislating what your partner can and cannot do with their free time. And if you’re so paranoid about their video game relationship, why not play it with them? That way you can make sure everything is “aboveboard” and you get to spend time together. If you can’t command their full attention, you can at least claim some of it. Nothing says compulsory monogamy like robbing an imaginary liquor store together.
