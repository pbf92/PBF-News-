TITLE: MonetizeMore says 10.8% of the traffic it checked was bots posing as people
OUTLET: PPC Land

Kean Graham, founder and chief executive of the publisher monetization company MonetizeMore, today argued in a LinkedIn post that the fraud filters Amazon Web Services began offering inside its ad auction network last week can only see the crudest kind of fake traffic, and published figures from his company's own detection tool to make the case. Over 12 months of data from MonetizeMore's Traffic Cop product, according to the post, general invalid traffic made up 0.4% of the traffic measured, while sophisticated invalid traffic - real browsers routed through home internet connections and "behaving like humans" - made up 10.8%.
The post is a vendor's argument as much as a data point. MonetizeMore sells Traffic Cop, which works on the publisher's page, and the page is exactly where Graham says fraud checks now need to go. The figures carry no outside audit, and the post does not say how many publishers, pages or ad impressions they cover.
In Short
The head of MonetizeMore, which sells an anti-fraud tool to publishers, says the add-on filters Amazon now offers inside its ad auction network can only look at the basic details of each ad request, which is enough to catch obvious bots but not the disguised ones that act like real people. His company's own figures put obvious bots at 0.4% of the traffic it checked over a year and disguised ones at 10.8%, so if that split holds more widely, most fake traffic is the harder kind. The open question is where checks run and how much each one misses: AWS's filters work before an ad is bought, while Graham says the hardest cases only show up on the page, a claim that comes from a company with a competing product to sell.
What Graham wrote
The post opens with its audience and its subject. "If you're an ad monetized publisher, this one is for you. AWS just made it far easier for bidders to filter fraud," Graham wrote. The product in question is AWS RTB Fabric Modules, which Amazon Web Services made generally available through AWS Marketplace on November 19, 2026. "Last week they launched RTB Fabric Modules: Demand-side platforms (DSPs) can attach third-party filters directly in the bid path, running inline at single-digit millisecond latency," the post states. AWS itself describes a broader set of users: its launch materials say demand-side platforms, supply-side platforms and publishers running on RTB Fabric can all attach modules.
Graham did not dismiss the launch. "That's genuinely good. But it matters what those filters can and can't see," he wrote. His case rests on one technical point. "A module in the bid path only sees the bid request. IP reputation, user agent, device signals and domain quality," according to the post. "That's the exact profile of general invalid traffic (GIVT): Datacenter IPs, known bots and malformed requests. Catchable before the auction. However, it never sees the page, the session or what happens after the ad loads."
Then came the numbers. "We just pulled 12 months of Traffic Cop data. GIVT made up 0.4%. Sophisticated invalid traffic (SIVT) via real browsers on residential proxies behaving like humans (Not including suspicious traffic), made up 10.8%."
The post concedes some ground. "To be fair, a strong pre-bid filter does catch some SIVT. Header parameters and IP intelligence can flag residential proxies before the auction," Graham wrote. "But with no visibility into browser and session behavior, the most sophisticated SIVT slips through any pre-bid filter."
From there the post becomes a pitch. "So as pre-bid filtering becomes table stakes, verification must move to the page to catch what slips through. It's why we built Traffic Cop at MonetizeMore," it states. Graham also took aim at unnamed competitors: "The legacy vendors that don't work with publishers and see monetization and compliance data tend to fail at detecting SIVT. A pre-bid module on RTB Fabric has even less to work with." The post closes with a four-option poll asking readers how they detect SIVT, ranging from "We detect it via our own tools" to "SIVT doesn't affect my business." No results were visible when the post was captured.
Two kinds of fake traffic
The split Graham relies on comes from the measurement standards of the Media Rating Council, the US body that audits ad measurement, which divides invalid traffic into two classes. In plain terms, general invalid traffic is the kind that routine filtering catches with lists and standard checks: requests from data centers rather than homes, crawlers that announce themselves as bots, and requests that are broken or malformed. Sophisticated invalid traffic is the kind that takes more work to find, through advanced analysis, several signals that confirm one another and sometimes human review.
Graham's description of the harder kind centers on residential proxies, services that route traffic through ordinary household internet connections so that automated visits carry the same kind of address as a real person at home. Combined with a real browser and human-like behavior, that traffic looks much like a genuine reader from the outside.

How a module sits in the auction
AWS's user guide for RTB Fabric describes where modules run. According to AWS, the process works in five steps:
- A seller, such as an exchange or a publisher, sends a bid request - the message offering one ad slot for sale - to a buyer over an RTB Fabric link, AWS's private network for ad auctions.
- The link carries one flow, and the flow holds the modules. Under AWS's default quota, a flow can hold two modules.
- Each module filters, enriches or observes the request before it reaches the bidder. A vendor module can write its verdict into the request as ALLOW or DROP, along with any data it adds, under a field named ext.rtbfabric.modules.
- In AWS's worked example, vendor modules are set to fail open, which means the request carries on without the module's verdict if the module's call fails or times out.
- The buyer's system receives the request and decides whether to bid. AWS says the whole path runs at single-digit millisecond latency.
Everything in those five steps happens before the ad is bought and before anything appears on a screen. That is the boundary Graham draws: what a module can judge is limited to what the request contains, plus whatever reference data the vendor holds about the addresses, devices and domains in it.
The launch vendors work inside that boundary. Pixalate's module, IVT Blocking, Bid Enrichment & CTV Show-Level Intelligence, screens invalid requests before they reach the bidder, and Pixalate's chief technology officer said in AWS's launch materials that this cuts wasted computing by up to 15%. On its own product page for pre-bid blocking, Pixalate lists categories including data centers and proxy gateways, but it does not say what share of sophisticated invalid traffic it stops. WURFL Device Signals, from ScientiaMobile, adds device details and bot flags to requests. When Pixalate released its pre-bid interface in September, it put the RTB Fabric version at about 5 milliseconds at the median and 12 milliseconds at the 99th percentile.

What the measurement rules already say
The industry's auditors drew a similar line well before AWS's launch. In a statement dated April 11, 2025, written in response to a blog post by the research firm Adalytics, the Media Rating Council said its standards do not require filtering or blocking invalid traffic before a bid, and that in several cases they discourage or caution against it. According to the council, filtering of general invalid traffic after the ad is served is required, and full filtering after serving is needed to inform any filtering before the bid. Detecting sophisticated invalid traffic is encouraged rather than required under the standard, the statement says, but where it is measured, detection after serving still has to cover every category even when pre-bid blocking is in place.
The council added a warning that bears directly on Graham's argument: blocking traffic before the bid can hide how much invalid traffic exists in later reports, because blocked requests never reach the stage where they would be counted. At the time, the council said DoubleVerify and HUMAN held accreditation covering pre-bid detection, while Integral Ad Science planned to submit its pre-bid detection for audit during 2025.
The company behind the numbers
MonetizeMore's interest in the argument is direct. Its Traffic Cop product page, last modified on November 11, 2026, describes a tool that scores traffic on every request in real time and stops bots, scrapers, click farms and emulated devices before they reach an ad call. That makes Traffic Cop a blocking tool that also acts before an ad is requested, with one difference from a bid-path module: it runs where it can watch the browser and the visit.
The page's mention of mobile apps differs from what Graham told publishers in October. At a webinar for Indonesian publishers, recorded and published on October 8, Graham warned that Google's invalid traffic enforcement can wipe out unpaid ad revenue and, asked about apps, said Traffic Cop was "currently" not available for them. The post does not say whether the 12 months of data include any app traffic, which suggests, but does not confirm, a mostly web sample.
Other numbers on the same problem
Graham supported his claim that the problem is growing with a figure from outside his company. "Imperva's latest Bad Bot Report found automated traffic surpassed human traffic and keeps climbing: 53% of all web traffic in 2025. SIVT is the sophisticated end of that wave," he wrote. Imperva's own post on the 2026 report, dated April 29, 2026, says automated traffic made up more than 53% of all web traffic in 2025, up from 51% the year before, with human activity at 47%. That figure covers all web traffic, including search engine crawlers and other declared bots that no one counts as fraud, so it is not an invalid traffic rate for advertising.
Figures from ad-specific sources vary widely, partly because they measure different things.
DoubleVerify put its global fraud and sophisticated invalid traffic violation rate at 0.5% in July 2026, and later that month reported that campaigns without protection saw 2.8% fraud violations against 0.6% for protected ones. Those are rates measured on campaigns, after a vendor's own filtering, and are not comparable with a publisher-side tool's view of all visits. At the other end of the range, the ad fraud researcher Augustine Fou argued in October that the widely cited $63 billion estimate undercounts fraud on ads paid by the impression, pointing to one campaign in which up to 80% of impressions were flagged as invalid.
The residential proxy route Graham describes has appeared in recent fraud cases. In July 2026, HUMAN Security disrupted a connected TV scheme it called NewsJunkie that at its peak produced up to nearly two billion invalid bid requests a day per seller, in part by routing traffic through residential IP addresses with forged device details. A year earlier, PPC Land reported on the BADBOX 2.0 operation, in which infected streaming devices were turned into residential proxies whose home connections were sold to other criminals.
