TITLE: How practices can use digital patient education to prepare patients before the consultation
OUTLET: Medical Economics

How practices can use digital patient education to prepare patients before the consultation
Key Takeaways
- Pre-consultation digital exposure can set unrealistic expectations; practices can reduce confusion by addressing common patient questions and explicitly separating general education from individualized clinical recommendations.
- Shared decision-making can be operationalized online by framing trade-offs, alternatives, and uncertainty; decision aids require defined choices, option consequences, and values clarification.
SHOW MORE
Patients now arrive having already researched their options online. FAQs, short videos and process guides built around their real questions — and brought into the visit itself — can correct misconceptions, set realistic expectations and support shared decision-making.
By the time a patient arrives for a consultation, they may have already watched procedure videos, compared treatment options, read recovery stories and drafted their questions on their phone. They may also have encountered conflicting advice or formed expectations that need a closer look.
For practices providing elective care, this makes the period before the appointment an important opportunity for education. Whether someone is considering joint replacement, dental implants, vision correction or cosmetic surgery, the information they encounter can shape what they expect and what they ask.
The clinical consultation still requires an individual assessment and a conversation with a qualified professional. But preparation for that conversation can begin much earlier. Practices that recognize this have an opportunity to make their digital content a useful part of the patient experience.
Start with the questions patients are trying to answer
A treatment page often begins with an explanation of the procedure. Patients may arrive with a more immediate concern: How will this affect my life?
Someone considering an orthopedic procedure may be thinking about returning to work or climbing the stairs at home. A dental patient may want to understand the number of visits involved. A person exploring vision correction may wonder what testing determines eligibility.
These questions deserve space alongside descriptions of techniques and potential benefits. Useful content explains what the evaluation involves, what factors may influence the recommendation and which questions require a personal discussion.
A practical starting point is to ask clinicians, nurses and front-office staff which questions come up repeatedly. Those conversations can reveal gaps that a carefully written FAQ, short video or appointment guide could address.
Give patients a framework for making decisions
Education should help patients consider their options and priorities.
The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s explanation of shared decision-making describes a collaborative process that combines clinical evidence and professional expertise with a patient’s goals, preferences and circumstances. Digital education can introduce the questions that make this conversation possible: What matters most to me? What trade-offs am I willing to accept? What do I still need to understand?
Evidence supports the value of structured decision support. A 2024 Cochrane review of 209 studies involving 107,698 adults found that patient decision aids improved knowledge, understanding of benefits and harms, and participation in decisions.
However, an educational webpage is not automatically a decision aid. Formal decision aids identify a specific choice, describe the options and their consequences, and help patients clarify their priorities. Practices should distinguish these tools from general information and promotional materials.
The lesson applies across specialties: Explain reasonable alternatives, acknowledge uncertainty and leave room for a patient to decide against treatment or request more time.
Make the process visible
Patients need to understand the steps surrounding care as well as the treatment itself. Who will they meet? What information should they bring? Will additional testing be necessary? How will follow-up work?
An online process guide can provide that orientation. For example, the South Florida Center for Cosmetic Surgery’s surgery-process page outlines stages including medical history collection, preoperative appointments, transportation arrangements and postoperative follow-up. It shows how a practice can share logistical information beyond a single conversation.
Other specialties can apply the same approach to their own workflow. A dental practice might explain the stages of an implant evaluation. An ophthalmology practice might describe the purpose of a surgical assessment. An orthopedic office might explain how imaging and treatment history inform a consultation.
General guides should make clear which details vary from patient to patient. Medication changes, testing requirements and preparation instructions require current clinical oversight and individualized direction.
Use different formats without creating different messages
A patient may discover a practice through a video, explore a treatment page and receive an appointment email weeks later. Each format should contribute to a consistent explanation.
A short video can introduce one question. A webpage can provide context and sources. An appointment email can direct patients to the most relevant material and explain how to raise concerns.
Problems arise when those messages disagree. A social media post describing an “easy recovery” can confuse patients if the written guide outlines meaningful restrictions. A brief overview should acknowledge that recovery and suitability vary, even when space is limited.
Clinicians and communication teams should review educational materials together, assign responsibility for updates and check older content when recommendations or office processes change.
Design for understanding and access
Digital information is useful only when patients can find, access and understand it.
Practices can use plain language, define unfamiliar terms, caption videos and organize pages around recognizable questions. Translated materials should receive appropriate review, particularly when they explain clinical concepts or preparation requirements.
Information should also be available through alternatives such as printed guides or staff conversations. Patients have different reading preferences, language needs, disabilities and levels of comfort with technology.
Sending a link does not establish understanding. At the appointment, a clinician can ask what the patient reviewed, what they took away and what remains unclear. This gives the patient permission to question the material and allows the care team to correct misunderstandings.
Bring digital education into the conversation
Pre-appointment education works best when the practice refers to it during the consultation.
A simple opening question — “What have you read or watched about this treatment?” — can reveal both useful preparation and misconceptions. Asking which concern matters most helps connect general information to the person in front of the clinician.
Teams can also invite patients to bring questions, clarify conflicting information and discuss whether online examples apply to their circumstances. The consultation remains the place for evaluating risks and making an informed decision.
Practices should assess educational content accordingly. Views and clicks show reach; patient feedback can reveal whether the information was understandable and useful. Staff can track recurring confusion and use it to improve materials.
Digital education gives practices more opportunities to prepare patients for meaningful conversations. Its value becomes clear when patients arrive able to explain their priorities, ask informed questions and recognize what still needs professional guidance. The appointment then builds on that preparation, with the patient and care team working through the decision together.
Rohan Policherla, MD, is a plastic surgeon at South Florida Center for Cosmetic Surgery in Fort Lauderdale, Florida. With specialized training in craniofacial and pediatric plastic surgery, his approach emphasizes patient safety, individualized treatment planning and thoughtful consideration of each patient’s goals.
