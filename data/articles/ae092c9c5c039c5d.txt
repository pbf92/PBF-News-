TITLE: COPYRIGHT NEWS—Meta should be held...
OUTLET: VitalLaw.com

By Matthew Hersh, J.D.
The individual lawsuit comes after a California court denied class certification over similar claims.
The company that runs Facebook is responsible for copyright infringement, trademark infringement, and design patent infringement for allowing the platform to become “the source and cause of some of the most detrimental and largest e-commerce scams,” a new federal complaint alleges. The lawsuit, brought by the company behind a popular animal-care product, comes months after a California court denied class certification in a lawsuit alleging similar conduct by the social media giant (Betty's Best, Inc. v. Meta Platforms, Inc., No. 5:26-cv-13646-NC (N.D. Cal. Nov. 12, 2026)).
The lawsuit was brought by Betty’s Best, the manufacturer of a horse and pet-grooming product called the StripHair Gentle Groomer. The product is sold through “hundreds of authorized retailers in the United States and other countries,” the complaint recites, including through the StripHair website, via Amazon, and through other authorized retailers. The product is also advertised on a wide range of social media outlets, including on Facebook. The company owns the federally registered trademarks to STRIPHAIR and THE GENTLE GROOMER, a design patent for a “Horse or Pet Grooming Tool” and the registered copyrights to its website and to related photographs and motion pictures.
The complaint asserts that Facebook has been awash in advertisements for knockoffs—and that Facebook has not done enough about it. The complaint identifies hundreds of discrete Facebook advertisers and thousands of counterfeit ads, along with more than 600 different Facebook pages promoting a counterfeit StripHair product. The counterfeit ads, the complaint asserts, also took videos and photographs owned by Betty’s Best and used them—sometimes changing their length, speed, text overlay, or music—to sell the knockoff products.
But even though the company reported those counterfeits to Facebook, the complaint asserts, Meta “took no action to stop the[se] [a]dvertisers from using Facebook ads to promote the sale of counterfeit products.” Many of the Facebook pages that the counterfeiters linked to remained active and were not removed, the complaint alleges. “Many pages went down briefly and became active again days later,” the company claims, “and many pages were reported four to six times and still were not removed.” As a result of Meta’s inaction, the complaint alleges, it suffered “a diversion of sales, a lessening of goodwill associated with the Betty's Best Marks and its products, and costs associated with policing and enforcement, including legal fees and investigative fees.”
The lawsuit brings copyright, design patent, and trademark claims. On the copyright side, the lawsuit contends that Facebook is liable for direct copyright infringement for displaying copies of the company’s registered works. The lawsuit also contends that Facebook contributed to these counterfeiters’ infringement because it failed to remove counterfeit ads after being specifically notified of them and, in addition, used an algorithm to serve up counterfeit ads to specific targeted customers. Facebook is also vicariously liable for the counterfeit ads, the lawsuit alleges, because it failed to exercise its right and ability to terminate advertisers and remove their products from its site while at the same time receiving a direct financial benefit from counterfeiters through their purchases of advertising.
On the trademark and patent side, the lawsuit accuses Meta of direct and secondary trademark infringement, false designation of origin, and unfair competition under both common law and California law. The lawsuit also brings a claim of design patent infringement on grounds that Facebook offered for sale, and also induced its advertisers to offer for sale, counterfeit products substantially similar to the patented design.
Betty’s Best has previously pursued the alleged counterfeiters in two federal lawsuits. In Florida, the company obtained a temporary restraining order and preliminary injunctions against some defendants. In California, Meta appeared as a nonparty to oppose a request for emergency relief and expedited discovery. The court denied the requested temporary restraining order and asset freeze, but the litigation ultimately resulted in consent judgments and permanent injunctions against some advertisers and dismissals of the others. The remaining claims were voluntarily dismissed without prejudice in July 2025.
The lawsuit is also not the first time that similar claims have been leveled against Meta. Several years ago, Florida sculptor Jennifer Cook filed a proposed class action accusing Facebook of copyright and trademark infringement for its role in the widespread distribution of “inferior knockoffs” of registered works. But earlier this year a California district court denied class certification in the case, finding that the claims were “not amenable to common proof” and would require individualized inquiry to resolve. Betty’s Best alleges that it was a member of the proposed class and invokes the earlier lawsuit to toll the limitations periods on its individual claims.
The Case is No. 5:26-cv-13646-NC.
Judge: Cousins, N.
Attorneys: Matthew Laurence Rollin (Sriplaw, P.A.) for Betty's Best, Inc.
