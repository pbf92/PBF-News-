TITLE: Corruption thrives where opportunity meets incentives
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Corruption in health systems may arise when governance failures offer opportunities, and systemic vulnerabilities provide incentives for private gain, according to an umbrella review published this week in the open access journal PLOS Global Public Health by Michele Coleman and Petra Varkonyi from the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, United Kingdom, and colleagues. Rather than treating corruption as individual wrongdoing, the authors examine it as a health systems issue, shaped by how systems are governed, funded, and held to account. The review was undertaken to inform the work of the Lancet Global Health Commission on Anti-Corruption in Health.
Corruption—the abuse of power for private gain—has real, tangible consequences for patients and their families. In the health sector, corruption can limit access to care and medication, affect nationwide and global goals such as pandemic preparedness, and cost lives. To better understand the impact and mechanisms underlying corruption in health systems, the authors of this study conducted an umbrella review of 12 systematic reviews and meta-analyses of corruption in healthcare. They focused on four types of corruption: substandard or falsified medications, informal payments or bribes, absenteeism, and pharmaceutical industry influence. Corruption within each of these issues might vary—absenteeism, for example might not be corrupt, but could be involuntary or officially sanctioned, while informal payments might also be part of health system dysfunction, so the authors classified absenteeism, informal payment, or interactions with pharmaceutical industries as corrupt only when the evidence indicated the systems were being misused for private benefit.
Of the 12 studies, three focused on high-income countries (the United States, Europe, and South Korea), five on work or low/middle income countries, three on sub-Saharan Africa, and one on Iran. The authors found strong impacts of corruption on healthcare outcomes. Infant and child mortality, for example, were estimated to be twice as high in Anglophone West African countries that had high corruption indices compared to low-corruption counterparts. Entanglements between physicians and pharmaceutical companies resulted in doctors marked preference for the pharmaceutical company’s drug. Informal payments resulted in doctors withholding treatment until they were paid, and absenteeism in the healthcare space prolonged wait times and delayed care, sometimes forcing patients to make more informal payments.
The authors also showed that the different types of corruption were likely when there was ample incentive for illegal profit and lack of oversight granted opportunity. Substandard medications were more likely in systems with high profit potential and weak regulations. Physicians were incentivized by gifts and material gain from pharmaceutical companies exploiting regulatory gaps. Informal payments arose when shortages incentivized patients to offer bribes. Absenteeism surfaced when clinicians were incentivized to seek extra employment, and systems were not available to track them.
The umbrella review considered only reviews—papers that did not directly measure impacts of specific kinds of corruption, though they did analytically summarize evidence—and the number of studies was limited, meaning that their findings cannot be taken universally. But the authors believe there is empirical evidence to suggest that corruption in healthcare is a systemic and global issue—formed by intersections of opportunity and incentive created by social norms, politics, economic pressure, power asymmetries, chronic underfunding and systemic inequality. Interventions, they conclude, should address both opportunity and incentive with enforceable codes of conduct, fair pay, transparency and accountability.
Senior author Dina Balanova adds: “Corruption in health systems is not a victimless administrative problem. Our review of 12 systematic reviews shows that its many forms, including falsified medicines, informal payments, absenteeism, and undue pharmaceutical influence, can cost lives, deepen poverty, and widen health inequalities by limiting access to care, reducing quality, and increasing out-of-pocket costs. Tackling corruption is therefore not optional: it is essential to protecting patients, strengthening trust, and improving health outcomes worldwide. These findings will inform the work of our Lancet Global Health Commission on anti-corruption in health, which argues that effective responses must go beyond isolated anti-fraud measures and address the incentives, opportunities, and accountability gaps that allow corruption to persist.”
First author Michele Coleman summarizes: “Our findings show the specific form of corruption varies but the pathway and conditions corruption survives in tend to operate through the same few channels. As a result, people can’t get care, the care they get is worse, or it costs them more than it should. Moreover, patients can face multiple forms of corruption during a single health care visit, compounding the effects. If a health worker is absent, or a patient may have to pay informally just to be seen, they may turn to an unregulated retailer, where the medicine itself is more likely to be substandard or falsified. Mapping these shared pathways matter because it tells is where an intervention might actually has a chance of working.”
She concludes: “We have enough systematic reviews to justify stepping back and asking what the evidence on corruption within health systems adds up to. What struck me in terms of the available data is we see a bias built into the evidence base itself. For example, a doctor taking a gift from a drug rep is far easier to document than a procurement system rigged at the top. What gets studied skews toward the corruption that’s visible, and not necessarily the corruption that’s costly or causing the most harm. It highlights a need for more research that captures the structural, harder to see forms--which the pathways component in our study begins to map--and beyond the collection of countries we currently have evidence for.”
