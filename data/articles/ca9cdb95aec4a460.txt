TITLE: Low Vitamin D linked to higher retinopathy risk, umbrella review finds
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Dietary choices may matter for the eyes of people with diabetes, but the evidence behind most of them is too weak to support firm advice, according to a review that pulled together the existing literature.
Diabetic retinopathy is a leading cause of vision impairment worldwide. Over the years, many research teams have asked whether what patients eat changes their risk, producing a thicket of systematic reviews and meta-analyses on individual nutrients and diets. Rather than add another review, Shi and colleagues at Peking University and collaborating institutions set out to take stock of the whole field.
An umbrella review synthesizes the findings of existing reviews rather than the original studies. The team searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library from inception through May 1, 2026, and included reviews of observational studies or randomized trials that examined dietary factors against retinopathy incidence or prevalence in people with diabetes. Of 2,162 records screened, 59 were assessed in full and 18 reviews were included.
The strongest signal concerned vitamin D. Low serum vitamin D was the only factor graded as convincing evidence for higher retinopathy risk. Omega-3 fatty acid intake and higher serum vitamin B12 earned a "highly suggestive" grade for lower risk. Several qualitative reviews converged on a similar pattern: higher serum vitamins C and E, vitamin D intake, plasma monounsaturated fatty acids and tea intake were each linked to lower risk, and most studies pointed in a protective direction for fish, fruits, vegetables and dietary fiber. The Mediterranean diet was the most consistently protective factor across the literature.
The caveat is substantial. Using the AMSTAR2 tool, the authors rated most included reviews as very low methodological quality. That means even the statistically robust findings rest on shaky foundations, and the different conclusions drawn for the same food should not be treated as settled. As the authors note, current clinical guidelines contain no retinopathy-specific dietary advice — a gap between what the evidence hints at and what doctors can recommend.
The review does not establish that any nutrient prevents or treats the disease; it grades associations reported in previous reviews, which themselves vary in quality. The authors call for rigorous prospective studies and targeted interventions before diet can move from suggestion to guideline. For now, the map they provide tells researchers where the evidence is promising and where it is thin.
