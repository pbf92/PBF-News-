TITLE: Mum's plea after newborn son died following ambulance delay and missed red flags
OUTLET: Manchester Evening News

Mum's plea after newborn son died following ambulance delay and missed red flags
Ezrah Pollard was just 19 days old when he started showing 'red flag markers' of infection — his parents called NHS 111 but faced a four-hour wait for a category two ambulance and had to drive him 20 miles to hospital
A mother whose newborn son died from catastrophic brain injuries caused by an infection has called for ambulances to prioritise babies under three months old, following a coroner's criticism of delays in reaching him.
Ezrah Pollard was just 19 days old when he began displaying "red flag markers" of infection.
His parents contacted NHS 111 and were assured help was on its way, but when they followed up with emergency services an hour later, an ambulance had still not been dispatched.
It subsequently came to light that there was a four-hour wait for category two calls – the second-highest priority – on that particular night.
This follows a new coroner's report which found there was an avoidable delay in administering antibiotics to Ezrah, with concerns raised that further deaths could occur if no action is taken.
Ezrah's parents Sarah Doolin and Craig Pollard, from Cornwall, drove their son 20 miles to the nearest hospital, where staff immediately recognised he was critically ill.
He was subsequently transferred to a hospital in Bristol, where a CT scan revealed devastating brain injuries.
Ms Doolin, 39, told the Press Association that Ezrah had been examined by a senior midwife the day before he fell ill in March 2022, who was "thrilled with his progress".
She had initially believed Ezrah had a cold, but he gradually lost interest in feeding as the day went on.
"I was still completely unaware of the idea that he could be very sick," Ms Doolin told PA.
"I thought maybe he needed a dirty nappy to clear his belly and I was thinking around things like a little cold.
"By early evening things were more alarming. Ezrah's breathing had changed, and I now know this is called a grunt and it's a sign of infection. But, to me, it sounded like he was coughing gently."
At approximately 8.30pm, Ezrah's parents contacted NHS 111 and were put through to the South West Ambulance Service.
They were informed a paramedic would evaluate their son at home and instructed to await its arrival.
The call was categorised as category two, which typically receives an average response within 18 minutes.
An hour later, the couple followed up on the ambulance but it remained undispatched "owing to significant operational pressures", according to a new prevention of future deaths report by coroner Andrew Cox.
The parents then drove Ezrah to the Royal Cornwall Hospital, approximately 20 miles from their St Martin home, before he was subsequently transferred to Bristol.
Ezrah succumbed to an infection triggered by group B streptococcus (GBS), a typically harmless bacteria present in between 20% to 40% of individuals.
It can occasionally transmit to the infant during birth, resulting in severe illness, with symptoms generally appearing within seven days.
Ezrah had late onset GBS, a rarer form that emerges in babies aged between seven days and three months.
An inquest heard there is presently no pathway for babies up to three months old to receive a category one ambulance response, which averages seven minutes.
Ezrah was classified as category two despite displaying "red flag markers" of infection, including "fever, grunting, breathlessness, 'deathly colour' and drowsiness", the report stated.
Ms Doolin told PA: "I would love to see that change. I don't understand why a baby is not considered category one.
"Newborn babies aren't ever category one unless they've stopped breathing. In which case, by that point, it's too late."
According to NHS England, a newborn would qualify for a category one response if they were unconscious, fitting, choking on solids, in cardiac arrest, or suffering major blood loss.
Neither NHS 111 nor the South West Ambulance Service were able to provide Ezrah's parents with an explanation as to why the ambulance was delayed, Mr Cox's report revealed.
It emphasised that "Ezrah required immediate antibiotics, yet their administration was avoidably delayed".
At the time, ambulances were taking four hours on average to respond to category two cases.
The report concluded that even where a category one response is not "considered appropriate" for young infants displaying signs of infection, parents ought to be advised to transport their child to hospital without delay.
Further concerns were raised regarding the absence of information about Ezrah upon his arrival at hospital, despite his parents having relayed his medical history to 111.
Ms Doolin called for improved communication between services, revealing she had to "repeat everything multiple times to different services".
Speaking to PA, she expressed feeling "hopeful" that Ezrah's inquest might trigger nationwide improvements.
"It would be amazing to see that taken more seriously," Ms Doolin said. "I remember thinking when I phoned 'he'll be priority', and obviously he didn't end up being.
"I understood from the inquest that they weren't even looking at the category two calls because they were too busy looking at category one and trying to make that work.
"To think I made that phone call; it's almost pointless, because no-one looked at it. And I'm sat thinking something's being done, and nothing's being done.
"I really do believe in 2026 we could do better. We could do better in terms of clinicians checking the call list right through from category one through onto the other two and three and beyond."
She proposes this approach could enable clinicians to inform parents about delays, allowing them to travel to hospital independently and ensuring babies receive treatment more swiftly.
According to NHS England, newborns would be classified as category one.
Ms Doolin, who works as an English teacher, and her husband Mr Pollard, 42, have subsequently raised over £100,000 in Ezrah's memory.
The pair, who also have a three-year-old daughter, are additionally campaigning to increase awareness of infection symptoms in newborn infants.
Ms Doolin explained: "Fundraising is an amazing way to put our energy into building a legacy for Ezrah that feels powerful, off the back of such a short life and such a painful and tragic story in terms of his illness.
"It's our way of trying to do something that's in the light rather than in the dark."
Dr Timothy Godfrey, medical director of enhanced and critical care at the South Western Ambulance Service NHS Foundation Trust, extended his "sincere condolences".
He stated that since 2022, "clinical oversight of waiting calls has significantly increased" at the trust.
