TITLE: Americans Rank Best Recent Presidents: Obama Compared to Trump
OUTLET: Newsweek

Barack Obama is Americans' choice for the best president of the past 25 years, and Donald Trump is the one they most often rank last, according to a new Politico poll.
The Politico Poll asked 2,000 adults to put the four men who have held the White House since 2001 in order from best to worst. Obama came first for 51 percent. Trump was a distant second at 28 percent, followed by George W. Bush at 16 percent and Joe Biden at 5 percent.
Trump also landed at the bottom of 52 percent of the rankings. Obama was last for only 8 percent.

Pollsters fielded the survey online from November 7 to 10, days after Democrats won both chambers of Congress in the midterm elections. The margin of error is plus or minus 2 percentage points.
Obama has led a string of recent polls on presidential popularity, nearly 10 years after leaving office. Trump keeps turning up at both ends of these lists. In a YouGov survey this summer, Americans named him the worst figure in U.S. history even as Republicans picked him as the greatest.
Obama vs. Trump
The gap between the 44th and 47th presidents has a lot to do with how evenly people judge them. Obama's support runs through the whole list. Besides the 51 percent who put him first, 18 percent ranked him second and 24 percent third.
Trump's numbers have almost no middle: 28 percent first, 11 percent second, 9 percent third and a majority last.
Todd Belt, director of the political management program at George Washington University, told Newsweek it was "not particularly surprising that Obama would be number one because he's associated with the recovery from the Great Recession."
"There was a lot of symbolism with Obama being the first Black president," Belt said. In his view, the other three "have pretty significant problems" that marked their time in office.
Obama also pulls some support out of Trump's own coalition. Sixteen percent of Americans who voted for Trump in 2024 ranked Obama as the best of the four, according to the poll. Among Trump voters who don't identify as MAGA, the figure was 23 percent.
Other surveys this fall found much the same. An AtlasIntel poll released November 1 showed 54 percent of Americans with a positive image of Obama, compared with 40 percent each for Trump and Biden. In a UMass Lowell/YouGov poll of likely voters in early October, 57 percent viewed Obama favorably.
Costas Panagopoulos, a political science professor at Northeastern University, looked at those numbers as both men went out on the midterm trail. "The overall findings are clear: Obama is way better positioned than Trump to be on the campaign trail," he told Newsweek in October.
Trump has his own ranking. In August he posted a tier list of presidents on Truth Social that put Obama and Biden among the "failures."
Inside the Parties: Bush Over Biden
For Democrats, the harshest number in the poll belongs to their most recent president. Biden got 5 percent of first-place votes, fewer than any of the others, and 34 percent of adults ranked him last.
Moderate Democrats show the problem most clearly. Seventy-six percent of them chose Obama as the best of the four. Bush, at 12 percent, drew more first-place votes from this group than Biden did, and most moderates parked Biden in the middle, with 46 percent ranking him second and 38 percent third.
Progressives were kinder to him. Obama was first for 83 percent of progressive Democrats and Biden second for 63 percent. Bush mostly landed third with them, at 62 percent.
Party lines decide the bottom of the list. Eighty-eight percent of progressive Democrats and 82 percent of moderates ranked Trump last. Trump voters saved fourth place for Biden: 70 percent of MAGA voters and 61 percent of non-MAGA voters put him there. Only 17 percent of MAGA voters ranked Obama last.
Belt tied Biden's slide to COVID and its long tail of inflation. "People don't like inflation," he said. "If the American people feel like the president isn't doing enough to handle their concerns or is dismissive of their concerns, then they will take it out on that president."
He said Biden also lost ground inside his own party because "he overstayed his time, and he was not ready to pass off leadership."
"I think a lot of Democrats are still angry at Joe Biden for not preparing the next generation of leadership and waiting too long to withdraw," Belt said. That anger, combined with the economy, "is what gave us Donald Trump," he added.
Bush sits in the comfortable middle. Sixteen percent of adults ranked him first, 40 percent second and 38 percent third. Just 7 percent put him last, the smallest share of the four.
He left office deeply unpopular amid the Iraq War and the financial crisis. A CNN/SSRS poll in June found 42 percent of Americans now view him favorably and 33 percent unfavorably.
Trump's Base Splits
MAGA Trump voters are close to unanimous. Eighty-one percent ranked Trump first and 65 percent placed Bush second.
The rest of Trump's 2024 coalition is harder to read. Among his voters who don't call themselves MAGA, 42 percent ranked Trump first, 32 percent picked Bush and 23 percent chose Obama. Nineteen percent of them put Trump last.
Other polls back up that loyalty. Republicans named Trump their most admired president ahead of Ronald Reagan and Abraham Lincoln, and an Elon University survey found 29 percent of Republicans called him the best president in U.S. history.
The same Politico Poll asked Americans which party they expect to win the White House in 2028. Fifty-two percent said Democrats. Among 2024 Trump voters, 22 percent said Democrats would win, while 60 percent expected a Republican and 18 percent were unsure.
Belt warned that the midterm results may not carry over. "This is a very backwards-facing election. It was a referendum on Donald Trump," he said. "Presidential elections, though, are more about the future, and Democrats need to figure out what they stand for."
With Congress likely gridlocked, he sees the party's best prospects in the states. "So I think that the path for Democrats probably runs through the governor's mansions," Belt said, since governors can enact policies and then point to results back home.
