TITLE: Custom deal name
OUTLET: PPC Land

Custom deal name is a dimension in Amazon Ads unified reporting that labels each row of a report with a name attached to a programmatic deal inside Amazon's buying platform. It sits beside a separate Deal name field, and the distinction is the reason it exists. A deal arrives in a demand-side platform (DSP) carrying whatever name the seller typed into its own system, which may be cryptic, generic or written for the seller's internal use. Buyers who run dozens of deals need labels they control and can read, and a reporting field that groups spend and results by those labels. Amazon lists custom deal name among four deal fields in the dimensions reference it updated on November 11, 2026, all of them available only for DVA+ campaigns, as PPC Land's count of the 156 unified report dimensions records.
Where the field sits
Unified reporting is the reporting system Amazon opened in beta on November 11, 2025 and made generally available in the Amazon Ads console on June 8, 2026. It replaced separate report builders for sponsored ads and Amazon DSP. Amazon retired the Amazon DSP name on September 29, 2026; programmatic display, video and audio buying is now sold as DVA, and DVA+ is its AI-driven campaign type. Reports combine dimensions, which split data into rows, with metrics such as impressions, cost and conversions.
The November 2026 reference lists 156 dimension attributes in 11 categories. Custom deal name belongs to the Supply category, which PPC Land counted at 35 attributes. The deal block inside it has four members: Deal ID, Deal name, Custom deal name and Deal type. PPC Land described deal type as the field that "distinguishes programmatic guaranteed deals, private auctions and preferred deals", but the published coverage gives no wording for custom deal name itself. None of the four applies to sponsored ads, so a mixed report returns null for those rows.
According to Slogan.website, which cited Amazon's Ads API release notes in a June 11, 2026 analysis, a custom deal name dimension was added to unified reporting on June 3, 2026, five days before general availability. Amazon's release notes page did not render outside a browser, so that date rests on the secondary report.
How a deal gets two names
The split makes sense once the life of a deal is traced from seller to report.
- The seller creates the deal. A publisher or a supply-side platform (SSP) such as Magnite, PubMatic or Index Exchange sets up the agreement in its own interface, fixing price, dates, inventory and the buyer seats allowed to bid. It assigns the deal ID and, usually, a name. The IAB Tech Lab Deals API, finalised in February 2026, carries that name in a recommended name field described as the "name of the deal as created in the origin system", and warns that "this name may be displayed to the buyer".
- The deal reaches Amazon. Partner deals are synced through an API or entered by hand. Amazon's buying guides for partners use a "Deal Creation ID" tied to each SSP seat. Equativ's Sharethrough guide says synced deals appear in Amazon's Inventory tab pre-populated under the exchange's name, and Microsoft's buying guide tells buyers to find a negotiated deal "using the deal name or ID".
- The buyer labels it. When a deal is set up manually in Amazon's DSP, the form asks for a name. Ogury's guide for Amazon buyers describes that name as "for your reference, won't impact delivery", while the deal ID, source SSP, type and floor price come from the publisher. Adobe's DSP works the same way: its deal name is "the name to identify your Deal ID", editable, with an auto-name option, separate from the external ID the SSP uses.
- The deal runs. In the bid stream only the ID matters. The OpenRTB 2.6 Deal object, inside the private marketplace (pmp) object of each bid request, carries id, bidfloor, bidfloorcur, at, wseat, wadomain, guar and duration-floor fields. It has no name field at all, so neither name affects which impressions are offered or won.
- The report joins the labels back. Unified reporting attaches the deal ID, the seller's name, the buyer-side name and the deal type to the impressions bought through each deal.
Read that way, Deal name answers "what did the seller call this?" and Custom deal name answers "what does this account call it?" Amazon's public documentation does not confirm that reading word for word, and a second interpretation exists.
A worked example
Consider a US streaming TV advertiser with three deals active in one DVA+ campaign. A preferred deal with a sports publisher arrives named "PUB_PD_Q4_CTV_US_1849" by the SSP; the buyer labels it "Sports CTV - fixed US$28". A private auction from a curator arrives as "Premium Video Package"; the buyer labels it "Curated auto intenders". A programmatic guaranteed deal with Prime Video carries Amazon's own label and is renamed "PV holiday PG".
Split by custom deal name, the report reads in the agency's vocabulary, with cost per thousand impressions (CPM) and conversions per row. Split by deal name, it reads in the sellers' vocabulary, useful for reconciling SSP invoices. The labels and prices are illustrative.
Origin and evolution
Deals predate the field by more than a decade. OpenRTB 2.2, published in 2013, introduced the pmp and Deal objects with only the ID required, and that minimalism persists in version 2.6. Names lived in each platform's interface, never in the protocol, which is why every DSP and SSP developed its own naming conventions.
Amazon's deal tooling grew in steps. On December 19, 2023 it added a deals widget to line item setup covering preferred deals, private auctions and programmatic guaranteed deals. On March 26, 2024 it launched Inventory hub, with Discovery, Inventory groups and Insights tabs; Amazon's announcement said buyers could find "off-the-shelf or custom deals" and name inventory groups "according to their internal naming convention". On June 10, 2024 the Supply Desk service introduced "pre-curated, off-the-shelf deals" alongside "bespoke" custom packaging arranged through an Amazon representative. Self-service buyers gained instant preferred deals with Prime Video, Twitch and Freevee on September 5, 2024, extended to programmatic guaranteed deals on February 18, 2025.
IAB Tech Lab's Deals API, released in December 2025, then gave the seller-side name a formal field, and Amazon's unified reporting exposed seller-side and buyer-side labels as separate dimensions.
Why it matters
Deals are central to how Amazon's DSP buys premium streaming inventory. Amazon has added Netflix inventory from the fourth quarter of 2025, Disney inventory and Guaranteed Deals for Prime Video Ads in August 2024. In August 2026 it extended publisher-hosted creatives to Google Ad Manager programmatic guaranteed deals in 33 markets. Each addition means more deal rows to read.
Naming discipline is also where deal problems show up. The Trade Desk said in June 2025 that roughly 90% of deal IDs fail to scale, an internal figure that has not been independently verified. A buyer-side label that records the publisher, price and purpose lets an analyst spot a dead deal quickly, without cross-referencing IDs against spreadsheets.
Limitations and disputes
The definition is not public in detail. PPC Land's reporting on the reference names the field without describing it, and Amazon's Support Center and API pages render empty outside a browser. Two readings fit the evidence. In the first, the field holds the buyer's own label for any deal, the reading supported by the manual deal form described by Ogury. In the second, it holds the name of a custom deal, Amazon's term for a deal negotiated specifically for one buyer, as opposed to an off-the-shelf package from Inventory hub or Supply Desk. In practice the two overlap, because custom deals are the ones buyers most often enter and label themselves.
Free text is fragile. A typed name can contain typos, change mid-flight or differ across accounts, so trend reporting by custom deal name is only as consistent as an agency's conventions. Deal ID remains the reliable join key.
Automation thins the role of human labels. Amazon's automatic deal selection, launched on April 15, 2026 for US streaming TV awareness campaigns, adds and drops deals by machine learning inside inventory groups. Deals chosen that way may never receive a buyer-side name.
Names reveal nothing about fees. A row labelled by deal shows what was bought, not how cost divided between Amazon, the SSP and the publisher, an issue sharpened when the FTC and 22 states sued Amazon over about US$20 billion in alleged hidden ad surcharges on August 31, 2026.
Custom deal name versus adjacent fields
- Deal name is the label the deal carries from its origin, normally the seller or SSP. Custom deal name is the label used on Amazon's buy side, or for custom deals specifically.
- Deal ID is the machine identifier generated by the seller's platform and passed in every bid request. PPC Land's deal ID explainer covers how it travels through OpenRTB. Names are for people; the ID is what systems match on.
- Deal type classifies the commercial terms: programmatic guaranteed, preferred deal or private auction.
- Inventory group name belongs to a container of several deals in Inventory hub. A buyer names the group, and the group can hold deals that each carry their own names.
Recent developments
Amazon opened DSP inventory management APIs in March 2026, with create and update endpoints for advertising deals listed as upcoming, which would let deal setup and labelling run through code. The November 11, 2026 dimensions reference fixed the four deal fields in the unified vocabulary, and PPC Land's guide to unified reporting sets out how the old order and line item terms became campaign and ad group. Legacy report pages become read-only on December 17, 2026 and shut down on January 31, 2027, after which deal breakdowns, including by custom deal name, exist only in unified reporting.
