TITLE: Disney's $20m Character.ai deal faces objection from US bankruptcy watchdog
OUTLET: PPC Land

The US Trustee, the Justice Department official who oversees bankruptcy cases in Delaware, asked the federal bankruptcy court on November 24 to deny Character Technologies' request to sell Disney a license to its technology for $20 million. In a 23-page objection, Docket No. 232 in case 26-11603 (BLS), the Trustee argues that the core of the deal - Disney's hiring of most of Character.ai's staff and the legal releases that came with it - was completed on September 18, before the company filed for bankruptcy, and that the court has no power to approve it after the fact. The filing also says the company "has provided no explanation as to where $2.7 billion received in August of 2024 has gone." Judge Brendan L. Shannon is scheduled to hear the sale on December 7.
In Short
The government's bankruptcy watchdog asked the judge on November 24 to reject the deal under which Disney pays bankrupt Character.ai $20 million for a license to its technology, after Disney had already hired most of the company's staff and secured promises that the company would never sue Disney or those employees. The watchdog says those promises were given before the bankruptcy, so the court cannot approve them now, and that the auction meant to show Disney paid a fair price cannot test what Disney actually got. If the judge agrees at the hearing on December 7, the company's creditors keep the right to look into what happened on September 18 and, possibly, to challenge it.
What the Trustee filed
The objection is signed by Linda J. Casey, a trial attorney in the office of Andrew R. Vara, the US Trustee for Regions 3 and 9. It reached the docket at 10:23 a.m. Eastern on November 24, according to CourtListener, ahead of the noon deadline for the Trustee shown on the filing's cover. The Trustee's job, in the words of a 1977 House report the filing cites, is to "serve as bankruptcy watch-dogs to prevent fraud, dishonesty, and overreaching in the bankruptcy arena."
The request itself is one sentence: "the U.S. Trustee respectfully requests that this Court issue an order denying the Sale Motion and granting such other relief as this Court deems just." The Trustee also reserves the right to amend the objection and to "conduct discovery," meaning to demand documents and sworn testimony before the hearing.
The Trustee has objected three times before in this case: to the bidding rules on October 27, to the fee of the company's investment bank, Houlihan Lokey, and to the second opening bidder. The November 24 filing is aimed at the sale itself. The fee objection was overruled on November 23. At that hearing the judge said the Trustee's doubts about how the Disney deal was marketed were preserved for the contested sale hearing, as PPC Land reported when Character.ai's banker won court approval for its $1.75m sale fee. The new filing sets that argument out in full. The objection to the second bidder concerned the unnamed financial backer of Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, as covered when Character.ai's $10m bidder faced objection over its unnamed backer. That dispute ended on November 24: the company named the backer as Octave Fund I LLC in a separate filing late in the morning and withdrew its request to keep the name confidential.
The Trustee is not the first to object to Disney's deal. On November 18, 39 states and DC asked the court to deny Disney's $20m Character.ai deal, on overlapping grounds. What the Trustee adds is a narrower legal claim about what a bankruptcy court is allowed to approve, a statement that nobody valued the released claims before they were given up, and the question about Google's money.
How the deal was put together
The objection reconstructs the Disney transaction from its documents. According to the Trustee, it happened in seven steps.
- September 18, 2026: the exclusivity agreement. Character Technologies and Disney Worldwide Services, Inc. signed an Exclusivity and Prepayment Agreement with a term sheet attached. The term sheet describes "two interrelated and integrated transactions": an "Initial Transaction," the hiring, and a "License Transaction." Disney paid $5.5 million that day, non-refundable, and a further $2 million as a refundable deposit. The releases took effect on signing.
- The same day: exclusivity. The agreement required the company to negotiate only with Disney and barred it from discussing with anyone else any deal involving any of its intellectual property outside the ordinary course of business, any sale of the company, or "any hiring, engagement or transfer of any employees."
- The same day: the services contract. The two companies signed a Transition Services Agreement under which Disney lends back the people it hired so the company can keep running. The original version is not filed with the court. The Trustee received a copy marked "confidential."
- Before the bankruptcy: the hiring. Sixty-six employees moved to Disney "shortly before the Petition Date," among them the chief executive.
- October 12, 2026: the bankruptcy filing. Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11.
- October 13, 2026: the paperwork catches up. The services contract was amended the day after the filing, and the amended version incorporates the exclusivity agreement. The stalking horse agreement, which grants Disney the license and makes its bid the one rivals must beat, "does not appear to be dated but appears to be effective as of September 18, 2026," according to the Trustee, and was signed no earlier than October 13 because it refers to the amendment. The sale motion, Docket No. 15, was filed the same day.
- The auction and the court order. Only the license goes to auction, with Disney's $20 million as the minimum. Closing requires a sale order "acceptable to Disney in its sole discretion."
The term sheet spells out what is and is not up for bids. The License Transaction "refers solely to the IP License and the Release, Waiver and Covenant Not to Sue Agreement" and "excludes all employee hiring, onboarding, transition, retention and related arrangements." Disney's opening bid was offered, in the term sheet's words, "as an inducement for Disney to be used as the floor bid for the License Transaction." Disney is, in other words, the stalking horse for the license, and only for the license.

Promises made before the bankruptcy
The first argument rests on two short sections of the Bankruptcy Code. Section 363(b) lets a company in bankruptcy sell "property of the estate" with court approval. The estate is the pool of everything the company owned when it filed, held for its creditors. Section 541 defines that pool as "all legal or equitable interests of the debtor in property as of the commencement of the case."
The Trustee's reasoning follows from the dates. The releases, legal promises by Character.ai not to pursue claims against Disney and the people it hired, were granted on September 18. Once given up, those claims stopped belonging to the company, so they were not in the estate on October 12. "Nothing in the Bankruptcy Code or the Bankruptcy Rules authorizes a debtor to seek ex post facto approval of a transaction consummated pre-petition," the objection says. The releases, it adds, "should not be vested with section 363 protections for Disney after the fact."
Who was released
The releases cover any claim the company "had, presently has or may hereafter have" arising from anything "that has occurred or existed at any time from the beginning of time up to and through" September 18, 2026. According to the Trustee, they run to four groups:
- Disney and its affiliates, divisions, representatives, successors and assigns;
- up to six Key Employees, chosen jointly by Disney and the company, one of them the chief executive, if hired by Disney;
- any other current employee Disney offered a job to, if hired; and
- any other current or former employee, consultant or service provider, if hired by Disney.
The scope is wide. It includes claims over Disney's recruiting of the company's staff and over the negotiation of the deal documents. It also reaches work the employees do at Disney that might draw on what they built at Character.ai, "including the use of any Technology and Intellectual Property Rights developed by such employees, consultants or other service providers during their employment with, or provision of services to, the Debtor." For the chief executive, the term sheet adds a release of claims for breach of fiduciary duty, the duty of loyalty and care that an executive owes the company.
Who, exactly, is covered is not in the record. "Other than the former CEO, the record does not inform parties which Debtor employees were employed by Disney," the Trustee writes. Because the releases reach any former employee Disney hires, possibly beyond the 66, "it is not even clear if the Debtor is aware of all former employees who they have released."
No price check before signing
How much were those claims worth? Nobody asked before giving them up, according to the filing. "Prior to the execution of the EPA, the independent directors that approved the EPA had not conducted any review of the value of claims being released. Rather, any review of the claims commenced after the execution of the EPA and, upon information and belief, has not yet concluded." The phrase "upon information and belief" signals that the Trustee is stating what it understands to be true rather than what it has proved.
A footnote doubts that such a review could produce an answer anyway. The value of releasing employees from their non-compete agreements and of promising not to sue them, the Trustee writes, "would appear to depend on the market's interest in hiring these employees free of restrictions from the Debtor, and not based on any litigation value of any potential claims."
What the sale order would say
Here the Trustee turns to the proposed sale order attached to Disney's agreement. A fraudulent transfer, in bankruptcy law, is a deal in which a struggling company gave away value for too little; a creditors' committee or a trustee can sue to undo it. The objection says the order would close that door. The findings would stop the creditors' committee, or a chapter 7 trustee appointed if the case were turned into a liquidation, from examining the September agreement and how it was negotiated. "The effect of these Sale Order provisions would be the equivalent of a release relating to the EPA," the Trustee writes.
The company's position, as the objection describes it, is that the three agreements form one integrated transaction, that the steps taken before the bankruptcy were needed to stop staff leaving, and that the whole arrangement lets it get the most for both Disney's license and the rest of the business, including the Character.ai app. The Trustee does not dispute that the early steps may have helped the sale. "The fact that pre-petition actions may have improved the ability of the Debtor to conduct a post-petition, Bankruptcy Code section 363 sale does not create jurisdiction to include such pre-petition actions as part of the 363 sale," it answers.
