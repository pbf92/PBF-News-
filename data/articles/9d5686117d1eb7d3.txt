TITLE: Conjunctival Autograft for Pterygium Yielded Lowest Recurrence in Randomized Trial
OUTLET: Review of Optometry

Pterygium often returns after excision, so surgeons use several strategies to prevent regrowth. Researchers in China compared three of them in a trial published in JAMA Ophthalmology: conjunctival autograft, amniotic membrane transplant and intraoperative mitomycin C.
The trial ran at five tertiary hospitals between January 2016 and December 2020. It randomized 750 adults with primary pterygium (mean age, 57; 65% women) to one of the three approaches after standardized excision. Surgeons used a free supertemporal conjunctival autograft, cryopreserved amniotic membrane, or a five-minute application of 0.02% mitomycin C to the bare sclera. About 91% completed 12-month follow-up. Surgeons and patients knew the assigned procedure, but the clinicians who judged recurrence from photographs did not.
At 12 months, recurrence was highest with amniotic membrane (23%), followed by mitomycin C (13.7%) and conjunctival autograft (5%). The autograft lowered recurrence by 17.9 percentage points compared with amniotic membrane and 8.7 points compared with mitomycin C. Mitomycin C was 9.2 points lower than amniotic membrane, though the authors said that comparison was less certain.
Best-corrected visual acuity stayed stable, and epithelial healing time did not clearly differ among groups. Moderate to severe conjunctival congestion was generally less common with the autograft.
Adverse events occurred in 18 of 249 eyes in the amniotic membrane group, 28 of 248 in the mitomycin C group and 16 of 253 in the autograft group. Most were epithelial defects. Mild scleral ulceration occurred in one amniotic membrane eye and four mitomycin C eyes. There were no serious intervention-related events, and all surgery-related events resolved without lasting effects.
“No established threshold defines a clinically important difference in pterygium recurrence,” the researchers wrote in their paper. They said the findings should guide technique selection by clinical context rather than replace one procedure with another across the board. Mitomycin C, they added, may be a practical alternative because of its lower cost and preservation of conjunctival tissue. Techniques not tested here, such as fibrin glue and limbal-conjunctival autograft, may suit some patients.
Limitations include the one-year follow-up, enrollment of only Chinese participants and the relatively small 4mm conjunctival defects.
