TITLE: Review: Is ‘GTA VI’ Actually Woke?
OUTLET: Vulture

Grand Theft Auto has been the big dog on the action-gaming block for decades now, and its conventions — intricately mapped-out cities and countrysides and deeply curated real-world music — have led the industry, while its bloodlust and irreverent humor got it pilloried as a bad influence. Each installment must be a little more engaging and eye-popping than the one before; vastness is GTA’s cross to bear. 2013’s GTA V covered a chunk of Los Angeles County and ultimately shipped over 230 million units, making it the third-best-selling video game ever. GTA VI already made 3.7 billion dollars in first-day sales for its parent company, Rockstar Games. It’s a monocultural meteor raising a tidal wave of opinions, from excitement about the game’s mechanics to apprehension about its politics. Conservative culture-war commentators like Steven Crowder and Zack “Asmongold” Hoyt have latched on to this latest iteration as an example of the entertainment industry pandering to a liberal agenda, but that’s a lofty read. GTA VI is actually pretty politically noncommittal in the interest of appealing to all the demographics it needs to reach to claim a crown as the obvious biggest video game of its time upon arrival. Its perspective, mechanics, and graphics have been carefully revamped to bring more players to the table, but GTA for the most part remains the anything-goes, attention-seeking Swiss Army knife of crime it always was.
Protagonists Jason Duval (Dylan Rourke) and Lucia Caminos (Manni L. Perez) are firsts for Grand Theft Auto, which has never had a woman as a lead or centered its story on two playable characters in love. The couple’s core motivation is also new for the series, which began as a road-rage simulator but grew into a mischievous open-world playground that is now returning to Vice City, GTA’s Miami. Jason and Lucia need money to gain custody of their infant son; VI starts with him rushing through home repairs to prepare for her return from prison. Their realistic concerns give a weight and maturity to the main story that dovetail with other advancements: Driving and shooting feel more finely tuned than ever, and evading the police requires consideration of the clothing, car, and company you were seen with. And if you want to have sex with a woman, you need to be nice to her. The romance mechanics drew claims that GTA VI is a “cuck simulator.” It got review-bombed on its Metacritic page, where it hovers at 5.7 from fans and 99 percent from critics understandably blown away by the intricacies of the interactions between players, objects, vehicles, weather, and non-playable characters.
Any notion that a series focused on murder and heists wants to coddle the player is culture-war histrionics fueled by lies. The chores around the house dry up quickly in the earliest hours; you’ll find Lucia loves crime so much that a two-person job helps the protagonists bond just as effectively as any of the mushier boyfriend behavior. You can create emotional distance between them by neglecting quality time, but you can’t force a breakup. The game is reactive but not so much so that elaborate duo missions could be soloed. Jason and Lucia need each other for the biggest schemes whether they like each other or not, so it’s smart gaming to play ball with the double-protagonist conceit. Lucia is a GTA dream girl — a valuable extra gun, a laugh riot, and a thinker sharp enough to wonder about the motivations of the many conspirators you meet around the Sunshine State. She’s a hothead who cools for Jason, a frosty but wickedly funny ex-merc more at ease with guns than words. She was locked up for robbing a check-cashing location to pay utility bills and got out to a parole officer who doesn’t want to see her succeed. These two new parents don’t have good enough credit to open bank accounts; the idea that crime can dissolve such restrictions, and that laws against thievery punish the underclass, is one of the more left-leaning thoughts GTA VI offers. (But Jason and Lucia’s structural obstacles match the tone of the gritty stories told in GTA’s HD years: 2008’s IV followed an Eastern European war veteran fighting for respect as a professional in New York City.)
Cooking up unique and bizarre ways to make money is VI’s main agenda. Graduating from smash and grabs and stickups to high-security bank and jewelry-store jobs, VI pulls inspiration from cinematic spree sagas like Natural Born Killers, True Romance, and Bonnie and Clyde, bringing fresh ideas to a series that had long taken after the brooding murderers in Scarface and The Professional. People who associate GTA with male surliness and solitude feel deprived, but stealth game freaks are challenging themselves to nonlethal, undetected thievery. You can play it as furiously or as peacefully as you like, except for consequential story sequences you must shoot your way out of. Brasher and sneakier play styles are equally rewarding and tap slightly different skill sets. It’s not “woke” that VI lets you decide whether to continue carjacking someone who has a panic attack when you rip the car door open or how fatal a shooter you would like to be. It just turns any exchange into a tree of options where you can take a more ultraviolent route for harsher blowback or leave a light footprint for a breezier escape.
Using a mother’s experience to highlight hardship at the bottom of the economic ladder in America does address historic criticisms of chauvinism in the series, from former Nightline anchor Ted Koppel campaigning against Grand Theft Auto III in the aughts to debates about women’s lack of agency in V. The killing in VI is equal opportunity, all of it achievable with Lucia, and carried out in the name of burning love and parental need, but this is the extent of the game’s desire to manipulate your thinking. What VI really wants — borne out in clips of absurd interactions with Rockstar physics and couples’ fan cams and players lobbing objects at NPCs’ heads — is to give us a platform that bends to our will and sensibilities. It’s a box of possibilities and contradictions. Jason and Lucia’s constructive, devoted relationship and earthier reasons for progressing into the Vice City underworld tug against the childish exploits players will draft them into. Rockstar’s commitment to realism is encroached on by just the right amount of jank to yield footage of unplanned death and disorder. If you hate influencers, you’ll delight at the ones being eaten by gators in the swamps; if you wanna be one, you get to help rappers shoot music videos. If you despise the so-called radical left, you’ll cackle at the politician who pays a dominatrix to tie him up and call him a globalist, or at the animal-rights protest to free a polar bear that instantly mauls and kills people. Billboards reveal a corporate landscape jostling to be seen as the most open-minded: a soul-patched male attorney can be reached at “1-999-FEMNIST” and the caffeinated-soda company Sprunk has a “STAY WOKE” ad campaign. If you see MAGA thought leaders as repressive snake-oil salesmen, you can sneer at an Alex Jones type selling supplements on a podcast and run a rescue operation for a surprisingly militarized furry underground. VI ruffles everyone’s feathers a bit while teasing out a synergy for its leads, who’ll follow any maniac a little ways for a payday. The game wants to scan as down for whatever, and tales of its softening point of view and dwindling appetite for violence are wildly overblown.
You need to commit hours upon hours to VI’s landscape to get a feel for what it is and isn’t saying, to catch Rockstar’s American Dream simulator insisting that everyone in the country may have something to hide. And you can tell many of its most vocal opponents haven’t seen much of the new game’s Vice City. All VI really did for a fiercely negative reaction is to lock sex behind a playable woman’s prerogative and begin with a tutorial on how to make her happy. (The earliest tasks in a GTA game rarely ask you to shoot anyone because they’re introducing geography and mechanics.) VI spices up as soon as this business is out of the way. A later chapter, titled “Tits, Ass, Drug Money,” delivers plenty in each category. The series is as raunchy as ever, and sexual high jinks surround Jason and Lucia, if they’re not always the most active participants. This is a couple that hits the strip clubs together, sometimes to throw money and sometimes to steal it. The cat-and-mouse routine of influencers who stake their reputation on demonizing critics and calling foul play on any consensus reflects a poisoned internet. Discourse about games is flooded with commentators who care less about informing the player of what actually happens in said games than how the medium can be used to wedge into age-old ideological disputes. GTA VI is a breathtaking waterfront felony sandbox that wants to be in on everyone’s jokes at once, to be legible to all parties, and not to pledge allegiance to any. It’s a parody straining to honor the multifaceted character of a region famous for Florida Man mayhem. It may be a bit rash to call it the greatest game ever. But Rockstar has once again set a hell of a bar for the present.
