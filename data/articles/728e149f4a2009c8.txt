TITLE: Sharp rise in key psychological risk factor may help explain growing anxiety among young adults
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Intolerance of uncertainty—a key psychological risk factor for anxiety—increased steadily among college-aged young adults in Canada and the United States between 1992 and 2023, with the sharpest rise beginning around 2012, finds a new study by Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health and Boston University School of Public Health. The increase was about three times faster from 2012 to 2023 than during the previous two decades. The study is published in JAMA Psychiatry.
The findings suggest that rising intolerance of uncertainty (IU)—the tendency to struggle with situations in which important information is unknown or outcomes are unpredictable—may be one mechanism underlying the growing prevalence of anxiety and mood disorders among young adults. Although IU is considered one of the central psychological processes involved in anxiety, little research has examined whether changes in IU over time parallel the well-documented increase in anxiety among young adults.
"Our results provide updated insights into one of the core building blocks of anxiety: intolerance of uncertainty," said Katherine M. Keyes, PhD, professor of Epidemiology at Columbia Mailman, and senior author. "IU is not itself a health outcome or a root cause of disease, but an intermediary cognitive process that contributes to the development of anxiety."
For example, someone with high intolerance of uncertainty may feel they need to know exactly what to expect before attending class or a social gathering and become anxious or distressed when they cannot predict what will happen. People with higher IU are at greater risk for multiple anxiety and mood disorders.
Using the database Web of Science – a gold standard platform for citation research-- the authors identified peer-reviewed studies of college students in the U.S. and Canada published between 2007 and 2025. After screening nearly 300 full-text articles, they analyzed data from 138 studies representing 35,296 participants who completed either the 27-item or 12-item Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale.
Across the study period, average IU scores increased by approximately 0.65 points per year from 1992 through 2023. The rate of increase accelerated markedly around 2012, with IU rising about three times faster during 2012-2023 than during 1992-2011.
"The important role of IU in the treatment of anxiety disorders raised our interest in studying trends in IU over time," said Jeroen M. Van Baar, PhD, postdoctoral associate, Epidemiology, Boston University School of Public Health, and first author. "There have been many reports of anxiety and depression symptoms becoming more common among young adults over the past two decades, but we knew little about how intolerance of uncertainty has changed since 2014. If IU plays an important role in anxiety and depression, we would expect it to have increased alongside these conditions."
The researchers say the findings also provide new context for understanding what may be driving today's youth mental health crisis. A number of societal changes—including increased smartphone and social media use, greater academic pressure, loneliness, economic instability, childhood adversity, and the COVID-19 pandemic—may all contribute to rising intolerance of uncertainty.
"It is likely that these factors are intertwined and mutually reinforcing, jointly contributing to population-level increases in intolerance of uncertainty and anxiety among young adults," said Van Baar. "Any explanation will be strongest if it accounts for both the long-term upward trend since the 1990s and the inflection point observed around 2012."
The findings may also help address an ongoing debate over whether rising rates of anxiety simply reflect greater awareness of mental health rather than true increases in underlying psychological distress.
"Some researchers have argued that increases in anxiety and mood disorder symptoms among young people may partly reflect greater mental health awareness," noted Van Baar. "Because young people are now more familiar with terms like 'anxiety’, they may be more likely to report symptoms in surveys even if their underlying feelings have not changed. On the other hand, intolerance of uncertainty remains a relatively unfamiliar concept outside psychology, making this study less vulnerable to that criticism.”
"The fact that we observed a consistent increase in intolerance of uncertainty that mirrored trends in anxiety and mood disorder symptoms suggests these patterns are unlikely to be explained solely by greater mental health awareness and may instead reflect genuine changes in underlying mental health,” said Keyes.
"The persistent increase in intolerance of uncertainty justifies continued research into the role of uncertainty in the epidemiology of anxiety and the development of interventions that effectively reduce IU,” added Keyes. “Our findings suggest that reducing intolerance of uncertainty may help curb the rise of anxiety and mood disorder symptoms among young adults. The encouraging news is that effective interventions to reduce IU already exist for people with anxiety and mood disorders."
Co-authors are Ava Aimable, Columbia Mailman School of Public Health; Taylor Teckchandani and R. Nicholas Carleton, Psychological Trauma and Stress Systems Lab, University of Regina, Canada; Marcia Pescador Jimenez, Boston University School of Public Health; and Ray Y. Hong, National University of Singapore.
The study was supported by the National Institute of Mental Health, grant 5T32MH013043.

Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health
Founded in 1922, the Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health pursues an agenda of research, education, and service to address the critical and complex public health issues affecting New Yorkers, the nation and the world. The Columbia Mailman School is the third largest recipient of NIH grants among schools of public health. Its nearly 300 multi-disciplinary faculty members work in more than 100 countries around the world, addressing such issues as preventing infectious and chronic diseases, environmental health, maternal and child health, health policy, climate change and health, and public health preparedness. It is a leader in public health education with more than 1,300 graduate students from 55 nations pursuing a variety of master’s and doctoral degree programs. The Columbia Mailman School is also home to numerous world-renowned research centers, including ICAP and the Center for Infection and Immunity.
