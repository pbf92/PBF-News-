TITLE: Your Riches Are Stored Up in Leonida
OUTLET: Christianity Today

Millions of real people waited in a real line to visit a fake place. It’s called Leonida—a parody depiction of the state of Florida—and it’s home to swamps, strip malls, beaches, penthouses, and a fake population of people with an unusually high tolerance for public disorder. Leonida exists within Grand Theft Auto VI, a buzzy new video game produced by Rockstar Games that made more than $1.8 billion in sales by its Thursday release.
I’ll spare you the doom-and-gloom messaging that normally accompanies any Christian critique of Grand Theft Auto VI and other games of its ilk. A Christian publication scolding a violent video game has all the suspense of a dog barking at the mailman: The dog barks—and the mail still comes.
For those unfamiliar with the Grand Theft Auto series, the premise is relatively simple. You’re placed in a fictional American city, usually in the character of a criminal, and given a staggering amount of freedom to carry out your most dubious ambitions. You can steal cars (as the name suggests), but you can also rob banks, shoot civilians, commit vehicular manslaughter, and flee arrest, all with few to no consequences. You are truly free in the world of crime.
What those who don’t play video games need to understand about a game like Grand Theft Auto VI is that while there is a story to follow and complete, it’s fundamentally “open world.” You are free to explore and interact with the world as you like, which means no two people will have the exact same experience. Unlike movies, which happen to you, in a game, you happen to it. You inhabit a fictional character’s body and live their life as you desire. The appeal of this extends beyond escapism; there’s also the joy of emergent storytelling, of having perfectly conventional conversations with friends where you can exclaim, “I stole a jet and crashed it into a mountain.”
Grand Theft Auto VI’s main story reportedly will take roughly 50 hours—longer than some multiseason TV series—but many players will find joy in spending hundreds, if not thousands of hours driving, shooting, and exploring their way through the open world.
Perhaps you see the appeal; perhaps you’re not sure why someone would find any of this appealing. But many do. Over 235 million copies were sold of Grand Theft Auto V, nearly double the number of Super Bowl viewers. In fact, so many people are excited to dive into Grand Theft Auto VI that employers worried its release day, November 19, would become “the least productive day of the year” as workers take time off to play.
Here lies the irony: The Grand Theft Auto series works as a satire of American consumer culture. Not one of its in-game advertisements is sincere. Take the social network, LifeInvader, a direct parody of Facebook. Everybody in Los Santos (the fictional setting of Grand Theft Auto V) knows LifeInvader mines their data, and nobody in Los Santos cares. Or consider the phones everyone carries, prominently marketed on a billboard: “iFruit 9iX: 3% thinner, 3% faster. 30 Hour Line.” The game makes fun of you for buying these things, and you still buy them anyway, in-game and in person.
The games invite you to consider whether sitting with these ironic depictions of American consumer life, while speeding around in a car so grotesquely expensive that even your fictitious bank account would shudder, makes you any less ambivalent about how you consume in the real world. The fact that so many have waited 13 years to put $80 of hard-earned money into a digital cloud makes that answer seem obvious. Grand Theft Auto VI will generate billions of dollars in gross consumer spending—all while making fun of the people spending the money.
The game laughs at us for buying, and we buy the game that laughs at us.
But while Rockstar mocks its audience’s consumerism, the games also nurture and encourage spending. Grand Theft Auto V didn’t become the second best-selling video game of all time just because of its story. Two weeks after its release, the game’s online component—GTA Online—went live. This added the ability for players to meet up with their friends (and strangers) on the virtual streets of Los Santos, for real people to commit fake crimes with each other—and sometimes, frustratingly, to each other. (Anyone who’s had their character shot in the head by a passing online stranger knows the feeling.)
Through GTA Online, Rockstar took the opportunity to make real money through a practice known as microtransactions. You bought the base game, sure, but now you can spend your real-world money on in-game currency known as Shark Cards—virtual credit cards in the shape of a shark fin.
Online, the good stuff is hard to come by. You want the fastest cars? That’s going to cost some serious fictitious bucks. And there’s two ways to get that kind of cash. One way is to spend many grueling hours doing missions and bank heists earning money. The other is easier: Fork over some real-world cash, convert it into fictional currency, and that car can be yours in seconds, rather than hours. (Microtransactions are not unique to the Grand Theft Auto series—plenty of modern video games feature them—but Rockstar’s rampant monetization of GTA Online players in this way adds a layer of irony to their anti-consumerism satire.)
Before those of you who have never touched a controller get too comfortable judging these players, you are not immune to consumerism either. You purchase things because of what they communicate about you. You want the expensive car, but you also want the person driving it to be someone worth noticing. You want the house, but you also want the recognition that comes with owning it. You want the followers, the wardrobe, the job title, and the expensive watch—or some other status symbol. You want to be seen as the sort of person who has these things.
The virtual economy is not particularly different from the real one. It has simply made these purchases more discreet to those around you. And Rockstar has made billions of real dollars selling fake dollars for people to buy fake cars and fake guns.
The dog barks, and the mail still comes.
There’s one more detail about the series I find notably interesting. When you die in Grand Theft Auto, you’re met with a slow-motion black-and-white screen, with large red letters reading: “Wasted.”
Not “You died” or “Game over.” Wasted. It’s in your face, mocking you. You wake up from the hospital, a couple hundred fictitious bucks shorter, and your completely totaled car restored to its full glory is waiting for you. And as you open those butterfly doors and get back into the driver’s seat, you forget all about the life you wasted.
Our real lives aren’t nearly so cheap.
I urge you not to waste your life. This isn’t about whether you play Grand Theft Auto VI, but about whether your life as a whole is about following the way of materialism or the way of Christ.
If living in Leonida is about moving fast, I implore you to move slow (Ps. 37:7; James 1:19).
If living in Leonida is robbing banks and purchasing penthouses, I ask you to stock food and invite friends over (Isa. 58:7; Heb. 10:24–25).
The life you want in Leonida is the mirror opposite of the life you should have in Atlanta, Birmingham, or wherever you happen to be while reading this.
In a game where you get to play God—buy whatever you want, kill whoever you’d like—maybe the real lesson isn’t to scold and rebuke those who buy it. Maybe we need to question why that’s so appealing.
Leonida will have millions of fictitious millionaires. They’ll continue to buy faster cars, bigger houses, and enough firepower to equip a small nation. The advertisements will keep running, and the billboards will keep promising that the next purchase is the one that finally makes everything worthwhile.
And eventually, somewhere on a screen will flash the word wasted.
The game will offer another life. But you won’t get one.
And the mail still comes.
Dakota Fiebranz is a writer and content creator from Madison, Wisconsin, whose work dissects Christian living.
