TITLE: Bombay High Court refuses Blink Digital’s plea to restore Meta accounts
OUTLET: Indian Television Dot Com

MUMBAI: Blink Digital’s attempt to get back into Meta’s advertising ecosystem has hit a judicial roadblock, with the Bombay High Court refusing to order the immediate restoration of four disabled Business Manager accounts. The court, however, has left the door open for the agency to make a fresh pitch to Meta, provided it gives an assurance that it will comply with the platform’s policies.
In an order pronounced on 20 November, Justice Sandeep V. Marne held that Blink Digital had not established grounds for an interim mandatory injunction directing Meta to restore the accounts and their associated advertising assets. The court noted that there was prima facie material suggesting policy-violating advertisements and business practices, which Meta was entitled to take into account while enforcing its rules.
The agency can submit a fresh reinstatement request, assuring compliance with Meta’s policies. Meta has been directed to consider the request and communicate its decision within two weeks of receiving it.
In the meantime, Meta must preserve all data, records, content, logs and communications associated with Blink Digital’s Business Manager accounts, advertising accounts, Facebook page and Instagram account. This protection, however, comes with a deadline: it will cease if Blink Digital fails to submit its request within four weeks or if Meta rejects it.
The ruling is a setback for the digital agency, whose business was heavily tied to Meta’s platforms. It also puts the spotlight on the responsibilities of agencies managing thousands of advertiser accounts, particularly when platform policy compliance becomes a point of dispute.
The dispute traces back to 4 August 2026, when Meta disabled four Business Manager accounts operated by Blink Digital: Hustle Neo, Hustle Digital, Hustle AI and Hustle New.
Together, these accounts administered approximately 9,000 advertising accounts, along with Facebook and Instagram pages and other business assets. Their disablement therefore affected more than the agency’s access to a digital dashboard; it disrupted the infrastructure through which it managed advertising campaigns for clients.
Blink Digital approached the court seeking immediate restoration of the four accounts, along with their associated permissions and functionalities. It also sought protection for its Managed Agency Partner status and the benefits attached to it.
Alternatively, the agency wanted Meta restrained from deleting or destroying its business data and records, and sought directions for those records to be shared with it.
The agency argued that Meta had disabled its accounts without adequate prior notice, specific reasons or a reasonable opportunity to challenge the decision. It also questioned the explanations provided by the platform, contending that Meta had offered different justifications at different stages, including in subsequent legal correspondence and its affidavit before the court.
Meta presented a different account of events. It maintained that the enforcement action followed repeated violations of its Advertising Standards, Community Standards and Account Integrity policies.
Its submissions included allegations that Blink Digital had facilitated advertisements involving misleading financial offers, sexually suggestive content, prohibited drugs and online gambling-related claims. Meta also questioned how the agency marketed its own advertising services.
A significant part of Meta’s case concerned Blink Digital’s promotion of advertising accounts as ‘ban-free’, ‘no-ban’ and without spending limits. The platform also raised concerns about claims relating to rental advertising accounts and access to Meta insiders who could allegedly overturn enforcement decisions.
The court examined this material and observed that there was prima facie basis to infer that Blink Digital had marketed its services by promising advertisers an unfair advantage over other businesses. It also noted indications that the agency had suggested workarounds to Meta’s enforcement systems.
The court referred to the agency’s earlier attempts to seek guidance from Meta on the policies and signals that could trigger account suspensions. Blink Digital had also offered to remove non-compliant clients from its network.
These circumstances, the court observed, weakened the agency’s contention that it possessed the requisite expertise in Meta’s advertising policies and compliance requirements.
The court also declined to accept Blink Digital’s argument that Meta’s reliance on older instances of alleged violations weakened the justification for the August 2026 action. It held that Meta was entitled to consider the agency’s cumulative conduct, including earlier account suspensions and alleged policy breaches, while deciding whether to take enforcement action.
The ruling also examined where an advertising agency’s responsibility ends and an advertiser’s begins when campaigns are run through Meta’s Business Manager infrastructure.
Blink Digital argued that its role involved managing campaigns and acting as an intermediary between advertisers and Meta. The court, however, observed that its responsibilities extended further. The agency managed advertising accounts, reviewed advertisements for apparent compliance, monitored campaigns and served as the interface between advertisers and the platform.
As a Managed Agency Partner, it was expected to understand Meta’s policies and ensure that advertisements appearing through accounts under its management complied with them.
The court’s observations underline the compliance burden on agencies that manage advertising infrastructure on behalf of multiple clients. Simply executing campaigns or passing advertisements through a platform does not, in itself, remove the responsibility to monitor what is being promoted through managed accounts.
Blink Digital had also challenged the process followed by Meta, relying on Rule 4(8) of the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021.
The provision requires significant social media intermediaries to notify users of the grounds for disabling access, provide a reasonable opportunity to dispute the action and ensure appropriate grievance redressal oversight.
The court held that Meta had substantially complied with these requirements, taking into account its communication of 4 August and the subsequent correspondence between the parties.
It further observed that even if there had been a procedural lapse in strict compliance with Rule 4(8), that alone would not establish a sufficiently strong case for an interim mandatory injunction. The material relating to the agency’s conduct also weighed against granting immediate restoration.
The court additionally noted that Meta, being a private entity, could not be compelled to provide its advertising infrastructure to an agency as a matter of right. Blink Digital was not prevented from conducting its advertising business on other platforms, including Google, TikTok, Snapchat and Yahoo.
The commercial stakes behind the dispute are substantial. In its submissions, Blink Digital said its revenue from operations for assessment years 2023–24 and 2024–25 stood at Rs 515 crore. It also claimed that approximately 80–90 per cent of its revenue came from advertising on Meta’s platforms.
The agency argued that losing access to its Business Manager accounts had affected its client relationships and employee base. It maintained that disabling the accounts was disproportionate, given the scale of the business and the thousands of advertiser accounts linked to its infrastructure.
In its main commercial suit, Blink Digital has sought damages of Rs 1,000 crore from Meta, apart from declaratory and injunctive relief challenging the account disablement.
Meta, meanwhile, argued that the agency’s conduct exposed the platform to potential policy and statutory risks, making the disabling of the accounts necessary to protect users and the wider advertising ecosystem.
The November 20 order does not finally decide the Rs 1,000 crore damages claim or the other substantive issues in the commercial suit. It deals with Blink Digital’s interim application, Interim Application (L) No. 31469 of 2026, filed against Facebook India Online Services Private Limited and Meta Platforms Inc.
Blink Digital was represented by senior advocate Kunal Dwarkadas, along with counsel from Tuli & Co. Meta Platforms Inc. was represented by senior advocate Janak Dwarkadas and a team from Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas, while Facebook India was represented by advocate Varun Pathak, also from Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas.
For now, Blink Digital has not secured the immediate restoration it sought. Its next move is to return to Meta with a compliance assurance, while the platform must weigh that request within the two-week window set by the court. The wider dispute, including the Rs 1,000 crore claim, remains to be decided.
