Musk's Antitrust Suit Against Apple and OpenAI Set for Texas Jury Trial in March
The legal battle between Elon Musk's companies and OpenAI will move to a jury trial in Texas in March, marking the second courtroom showdown between the two sides in less than a year. OpenAI has lined up Gary Bornstein, a partner at Cravath, Swaine & Moore, as its lead defense lawyer, while Musk's side is led by John Hughes of Bartlit Beck.
The case was filed last year by X Corp and SpaceX Super Intelligence, alleging that Apple and OpenAI conspired to monopolize the generative AI chatbot market. U.S. District Judge Mark Pittman is presiding over the case, docket number 4:25-cv-00914-P, with the trial set to be held in Fort Worth. Notably, Apple reached a settlement with Musk's companies in September, and neither side disclosed the terms, meaning OpenAI will be the focus as the substantive defendant at next year's trial.
According to Reuters, Bornstein is currently co-head of Cravath's litigation department and will personally argue the case as lead counsel. He and colleague Lauren Moskowitz are also handling "Fortnite" developer Epic Games' lawsuit against Apple over its App Store business, with Bornstein serving as lead lawyer in that case as well. Beyond Cravath, OpenAI's legal team also includes Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz, with members including Stephen DiPrima and Kevin Schwartz, in what amounts to a heavyweight lineup.
Musk's camp is equally formidable. Hughes's firm Bartlit Beck represented a client in 2018 in a case challenging Harvard University's admissions practices that went all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court, ultimately leading the justices to end the consideration of race in college admissions. In addition to Bartlit Beck, Musk's companies are also represented by lawyers from Axinn Veltrop and Stone Hilton.
This is not the first time the two sides have faced off in court. In May, a federal jury in Oakland, California, ruled against Musk in a case accusing OpenAI of abandoning its founding mission to benefit humanity. Now the battlefield has shifted to Texas, and less than a year later, the two sides are again squaring off before a jury.
By way of background, OpenAI was co-founded in 2015 by Sam Altman, Musk and others. Musk left the board in 2018, and OpenAI began establishing a for-profit business the following year, after which relations between the two sides soured and gradually evolved into a series of lawsuits spanning multiple states.
For investors, the significance of this trial lies not only in the continuation of the feud between the two parties but also in the possibility that the competitive rules of the generative AI market could be redefined. If the jury finds that an exclusive arrangement exists between Apple and OpenAI, it would put pressure on the practice of tying AI chatbots to default services on mobile devices; conversely, if OpenAI prevails, it would help consolidate its business model of deep integration with hardware platforms.
