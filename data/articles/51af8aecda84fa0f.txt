Cycling With Brain Games Preserves Alzheimer's-Linked Brain Structures
A study in JAMA Network Open found combining indoor cycling with brain-computer games helped preserve key brain structures tied to Alzheimer's disease in older adults.
Updated on Nov. 22, 2026 in Alzheimer’s

Researchers reported in JAMA Network Open that older adults with mild cognitive impairment who combined indoor cycling with computerized brain games preserved key brain structures linked to Alzheimer's disease. The study enrolled 146 participants who exercised three times a week for six months, with brain scans taken over the following year and a half.
Why it matters
Alzheimer's disease affects nearly 1 in 9 people, and no known cure exists, making lifestyle-based approaches a focus of ongoing research. The findings suggest that pairing aerobic exercise with cognitive training may help protect brain structures that are affected by the disease.
The study enrolled 146 older adults with mild cognitive impairment who exercised three times a week for six months, with brain scans following over a year and a half. The combined group's advantage over cycling alone was not statistically significant, and researchers have said more studies are needed.
The players
Northwell Institute for Healthy Aging
A research institute within Northwell Health focused on the study of aging and healthy longevity, with an affiliation to the expert source quoted in the study coverage.
The details
Participants used moderate to vigorous indoor cycling combined with five adaptive speed-of-processing brain games that become harder as players improve. The brain games train attention and the speed at which the brain processes visual information.
Timeline
- Participants completed three-times-weekly interventions over six months.
- Brain scans were taken over the following year and a half.
Health Landscape
The study builds on the American Heart Association's recommendation of 150 minutes of aerobic activity per week, extending that line of evidence into brain health by testing exercise paired with cognitive training. Its findings add to a growing body of research examining lifestyle interventions as a complement to treatment for a disease with no known cure.
For older adults with mild cognitive impairment, the findings point toward a practical routine of regular aerobic exercise paired with structured brain training, though the study did not show the combination clearly outperformed exercise alone. Readers considering these approaches should consult a healthcare provider before starting a new program.
The takeaway
Regular aerobic exercise remains a well-supported habit for brain health, and adding cognitive training may offer further benefit, though more research is needed to confirm its added value. Staying consistent with activity over months appears to be the key factor in these findings.
