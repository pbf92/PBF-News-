TechRadar published a long hands-on piece on November 25, 2026, in which writer Rob Dwiar says he played around 130 hours of Grand Theft Auto 6 while testing seven gaming headsets. The headline frames it as a verdict on each headset for Rockstar’s open-world action-adventure. For anyone shopping for a GTA 6 headset this week, that is the kind of long-session evidence a spec sheet cannot give you.

Our earlier look at what GTA 6 looks like after launch, at $79.99 with no roadmap showed how much attention the game still commands. Headset makers want a slice of it, and reviewers are now putting names to the products that survive a marathon.
This analysis sticks to what the available TechRadar extract confirms. It names five of the seven headsets, quotes three of Dwiar’s verdicts, and leaves the rest of the list, the prices, the battery figures and the testing platform unconfirmed. What TechRadar Published on November 25
The article carries Dwiar’s byline and a publication date of November 25, 2026. Its core claim is simple. He tested multiple headsets while playing Grand Theft Auto 6 for roughly 130 hours, then wrote up how each one handled Leonida, the game’s setting.
That structure matters. Most headset reviews rely on a week of mixed use, a few benchmark tracks and a handful of games. A single-title test across one long campaign and its aftermath gives a narrower but deeper view. It shows which headset a player still wants to wear in hour 90, and which one ends up back in the box.
The extract we can verify does not include a ranking, a score table or a pricing chart. It does include named products and short, quotable verdicts. So the honest reading is this: TechRadar has published a seven-headset roundup built around one game, and five of the headsets are identifiable today.
The 130-Hour Claim in Context
One hundred thirty hours is a lot of playtime for a review. For scale, our coverage of the 39 million PS5 players and 683 million logged hours implies an average of roughly 17.5 hours per player, if both figures cover the same audience. Dwiar’s total is about 7.4 times that crude average.
Why the hours matter for audio testing
Headsets fail slowly. Clamp pressure, ear-cup heat, a boom arm that drifts, a wireless link that drops once an hour. None of that shows up in a 20-minute demo. Hundreds of in-game minutes across city streets, highways, interiors and open country expose those flaws, and they are the flaws that decide whether a headset earns a recommendation.
What the claim does not tell us
Dwiar said he played around 130 hours. The extract does not say how those hours split across the seven headsets. A reader cannot tell whether each device got 18 hours or whether a favorite took most of the time. That split changes how much weight each verdict deserves, so treat the 130 hours as the test’s total, not as a per-headset figure.

Audeze Maxwell 2 and Maxwell2 ANC: Planar Magnetic for Leonida
The extract names the Audeze Maxwell 2 and a Maxwell2 ANC variant, and it confirms both use planar magnetic drivers. Dwiar described the Audeze headsets as “a huge joy for listening to the world and soundscape of Leonida.”
That is a sound-first endorsement. It does not mention voice chat, comfort or price. It focuses on how the game’s world comes through the drivers, which suits a title built around ambient detail. Planar magnetic designs spread the drive force across a thin diaphragm, and listeners often cite that for clean detail in layered scenes. That is a general description of the technology, not a measurement from TechRadar’s test.
The ANC label on the second variant points to active noise cancellation, a feature that matters for players in noisy rooms. TechRadar’s extract does not say how well it worked, so we will not rate it. We can say that a cancellation feature changes who the headset suits. A player on a quiet desk may never use it, while a player sharing a living room might use it every night.
What we cannot say is what the pair costs. The extract gives no price for either Audeze model, and planar magnetic headsets usually sit at the premium end of the market. Check current retail listings before you decide, and expect Black Friday promotions to move the numbers.

Why Audio Matters in an Open-World Game Like GTA 6
Open-world games lean on sound to sell their setting. Dwiar’s choice of the word soundscape in his Audeze verdict shows the reviewer thinks of Leonida as something you hear as well as see. That framing changes what a good headset looks like.
Ambient detail and spatial placement
A busy city street mixes traffic, crowds, music from passing cars and distant sirens. A headset that separates those layers helps the player place threats and landmarks by ear. Drivers, tuning and any spatial processing all play a role. TechRadar’s extract does not tell us which of the five headsets handled this best, so we can only say the criterion matters.
Comfort across long sessions
Weight, padding and clamp force decide whether a headset survives a three-hour night. A 130-hour test is exactly the kind of exercise that exposes this. None of the verified quotes address comfort directly, but a reviewer who wore these products for that long had to form a view, and it will likely show up in the full piece.
Voice, wireless links and battery
Voice pickup, wireless reliability and battery life round out the checklist. Wireless dropouts annoy more during a mission than during a menu. Battery life decides whether you charge nightly or weekly. We have no confirmed figures for any named model, so shoppers should pull those numbers from the makers’ own pages.
