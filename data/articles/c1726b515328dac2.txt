TITLE: Will we ever see another game like GTA VI?
OUTLET: Vox

Grand Theft Auto VI arrived last week, 13 years after the last installment in the series, to an enormous debut — nearly $3.8 billion in its first day, according to developer Rockstar Games. That’s more than Avengers: Endgame has earned at the global box office across its entire theatrical run, including rereleases.
The game took more than eight years to develop, and Rockstar grew to more than 4,700 employees worldwide during production. In the game, players are catapulted into Leonida, a fictional version of Florida that includes Vice City, the series’ take on Miami.
Unlike past games in the series, you play as a couple, Jason and Lucia, who are trying to make enough money to support their young family and make it out. Maintaining their relationship is just as important as robbing stores or completing missions.
For all that GTA VI is shaping up to be a critically acclaimed triumph, however, it arrives at a difficult moment for the video game industry. Studios have endured years of layoffs and rising development costs, while workers worry about how generative AI could affect their jobs and creative work. Can an industry under this much pressure afford to keep making games this big, or is GTA VI doomed to be the last of its kind?
To find out, Today, Explained co-host Sean Rameswaram spoke with Chris Plante, a video game journalist with about two decades of experience and host of the podcast Post Games. They discussed GTA VI’s long road to release, what could be next for the franchise and whether DLC is on the horizon, and the future of blockbuster gaming more broadly.
Below is an excerpt of their conversation, edited for length and clarity. There’s much more in the full podcast, so listen to Today, Explained wherever you get podcasts, including Apple Podcasts, Pandora, and Spotify.
Chris, people are saying that this game is the last of its kind. Why are they saying that?
It kind of is, but I think we should be really specific when we say that. The last of its kind means the last of this: thousands and thousands of people trying to make one game without artificial intelligence, and it being a historic, all-consuming moment.
The best shot that we have of another game like this happening is GTA VII. But what I would be wary of is saying there aren’t going to be big-budget video games anymore. If you look at another game that came out just this month, The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time is a remake of one of the most famous games ever, one of the most beloved games ever, and it looks stunning. It’s on the Nintendo Switch 2. It is great. And it cost a fraction of what Grand Theft Auto cost [to make]. It took a fraction of the time to create. And yet it still feels absolutely decadent.
You know what a fair comparison would be? We get those Avatar movies — they take forever because they cost a fortune to make, they require all this new technology. That’s your Grand Theft Auto. But you wouldn’t look at an Avatar and say, ‘Well, they’re never going to make expensive movies anymore.’ We still get Dune. We still get Marvel movies. Those things are still happening.
There is a whole separate question about the stability of the highest ends of the industry, the companies that tried to be the Grand Theft Autos: Electronic Arts, the company that makes Madden; companies like Xbox, which owns Activision, which makes Call of Duty.
You might notice I’m saying a lot of which does this but owns that. The consolidation in the industry is real, and it’s a real problem. So there’s going to be turmoil, but it is not the end of video games, and it’s not even the end of big video games.
It sounds like this is a great time for the industry, but it’s actually the opposite.
It’s a great time for people who play video games and a nightmare time for people who make video games. If you play video games right now, you can turn on Steam, which is a storefront on PC, and you can find literally hundreds of games that were released just this week. Over a thousand new games are released every month on Steam. And because of that, it targets not every niche, but close. There is probably something out there for you.
This is a nightmare for people who make games because even Grand Theft Auto, when it comes to PC, is going to live right alongside all those other games. So for the people who do not have the name recognition, who do not have the marketing, you can launch a game that you spent tens, maybe hundreds of millions of dollars on, and it is going to, the same day that it comes out, compete against a brand-new $5 game that was made by three or four people with a whole bunch of contractor support for a very low cost and has just as good of odds, minus the marketing, of being a hit.
Is AI going to make it less risky for big gaming companies to invest in developing something ambitious because maybe they can make it dramatically cheaper and compete with the games that only cost $10 to develop?
AI, as the investment class has it in their head: The answer is no, not whatsoever.
“Looking even beyond games, there’s a lot of reason to be cautious about things that seem like they’re going to make things faster actually, in the long term, making them much slower.”
AI as a tool, it gets more complicated. I think when a lot of people hear AI, they think, I’ve seen a video where I can go on Google and type a prompt, and I say, give me Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, but make it like a Black Eyed Peas concert. And people are like, wow, you seem really out of touch, but I’ll make it for you anyway. And then you have this great game, and you are getting the party started in here. And that is not how any of this works at all.
Games are literally a miracle. My former colleague, Frank Lantz, who’s an educator and game designer, would compare it to art, if you also had to build a skyscraper or a highway. You have to both make the architecture and make the art. It’s unhinged. We do not have this world where games just are going to materialize.
Now where it gets really complicated and it gets really messy inside the industry is using AI tools for things like coding. And this is very controversial amongst a certain corner of video games culture and video game developers. There are people who want a full ban, that we are not using AI on anything, that we need to protect jobs, and that it shouldn’t be used for generating images or art or music or code.
And then there are people who say, but I use AI for code. I think it is relatively common, and that is somewhat of a controversial take, but it is what I hear from so many people I talk to who are on that side of game development, to be using it when you are programming as a tool.
I think there’s a lot of promises, but I think that, again, looking even beyond games, there’s a lot of reason to be cautious about things that seem like they’re going to make things faster actually, in the long term, making them much slower.
And we still live in a world where it makes sense to start developing a GTA VII?
It does for Rockstar for sure, though I don’t think that they will be in a rush to do it anytime soon, for a few reasons.
One, they still need to get Grand Theft Auto Online out. That’s where the real money is. Grand Theft Auto Online drops people into the city of each new game. Grand Theft Auto V did it with LA. Now you’re going to have it in Miami. And that is where people can get lost for years and years and years, and they can start charging you for little incremental things like cool new outfits or fancy cars or spots to live. So that’s going to be their priority.
There has been word or rumor that they might just expand Grand Theft Auto VI, that we would see more cities. Who knows if that’s true? It would be a different approach, but the game began as a thing called Project Americas and was supposed to be a much larger game. So that could be one way that it grows rather than a GTA VII: We just see a GTA VI that gets bigger and bigger, not unlike Fortnite, but we’re going to get more of it.

Damn. Actually, I was going to say that I think it would be sooner than 13 years, and I’m not sure I actually believe that. I’m going to put it at, let’s say, 2037. I’ll see you in 11 years. Does that sound good?
If all goes well for our industries, yes.
Yeah. Better odds in the Grand Theft Auto business. I mean, hey, we can always turn into a romantic duo performing a life of crime.
