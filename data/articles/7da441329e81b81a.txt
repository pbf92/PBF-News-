TITLE: Pediatric mental health care utilization varied by climate vulnerability and historical redlining
OUTLET: 2 Minute Medicine

1. This cross sectional study analyzed the association of pediatric mental healthcare utilization in out-patient and emergency settings in relation to climate vulnerability and redlining.
2. The association of out-patient pediatric mental healthcare utilization and climate vulnerable communities were statistically significant and varied based on redlined versus non-redlined areas
Evidence Rating Level: 3 (Average)
Study Rundown: This cross sectional study analyzed the association between neighborhood level climate vulnerability and pediatric mental healthcare utilization in the setting of both outpatient care and the emergency department, and whether the associations varied based on redlining status. Redlining is the practice of denying or restricting financial services (ie. mortgages, loans, insurance or banking) to residents of specific neighborhoods based on race, ethnicity, or national origin rather than individual creditworthiness. Structures like these, in addition to environmental injustices, create varying risk levels for vulnerable populations. Associations between climate risk, structural racism and pediatric mental healthcare are not a heavily researched area, therefore, this study contributes much needed data for marginalized communities. Overall in this study, it was found that across all levels of climate vulnerability, mental healthcare utilization decreased from areas of low climate vulnerability to areas of high climate vulnerability in non-redlined areas. In redlined areas, mental healthcare utilization increased from areas of low climate vulnerability to areas of high climate vulnerability. Communities in areas of high climate vulnerability were associated with higher outpatient visit rates compared with low climate vulnerable areas. For children in non-redlined, high vulnerability areas there were higher ED visit rates, though this data was not statistically significant when compared to non-redlined low vulnerability areas. For children in redlined, high vulnerability areas there were higher rates of outpatient mental healthcare utilization and, to a lesser extent, a modest but statistically significant increase in ED utilization.
Relevant Reading: Five-Year Trends in US Children’s Health and Well-being, 2016-2020
In-Depth [cross-sectional study]: In this cross-sectional study, 553,134 mental health–related pediatric ED and outpatient visits across New York from 2017 to 2022 were analyzed. Among all the visits, 201,402 (36.4%) occurred in ED settings and 351,732(63.6%) occurred in out-patient settings. To assess redlining, data was gathered from children residing in 13 cities across NY state that were historically graded by Home Owners’ Loan Company (HOLC). HOLC produced residential security maps in the 1930’s and 1940’s which classified neighborhoods by “perceived lending risk”, these grades given by the HOLC are now widely used as a proxy for historical redlining. HOLC classes A and B were the best, most desirable areas, while classes C and D were declining, hazardous areas. In this study class A and B were considered non-redlined and C and D were considered redlined areas. The proportion of mental health related visits decreased progressively from areas of low climate vulnerability to areas of high climate vulnerability in non-redlined areas(44,676 of 137,669[32.5%] to 8,094 of 139,376 [5.8%]). The proportion of mental health related visits increased from areas of low climate vulnerability to areas of high climate vulnerability areas in redlined areas (92,993 of 137,669[67.5%] to 131,282 of 139,376 [94.2%]). This study does not account for other outside factors that could be contributing to the utilization of pediatric mental health services; other factors include bullying, accessibility of healthy food, discrimination, sexual identity, etc. This study also does not account for data on the utilization of tele-health mental health services, which have increased in usage post-COVID 19 pandemic. Furthermore, the usage of data only from hospitals neglects to account for data from Federally Qualified Health Centers, which may be more accessible in disadvantaged communities.
