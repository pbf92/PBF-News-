TITLE: Review: ‘Ebenezer’
OUTLET: Christianity Today

Charles Dickens’s A Christmas Carol (1843) has been adapted for the screen over 50 times, including versions populated by the Muppets and the Smurfs. For devotees of the novella, the question of whether Dickens’s most famous tale merits yet another appearance in movie theaters will hinge on whether the latest version captures something they think essential to the source material.
Some, however, will find nothing essential in a story they consider patently absurd.
Dismissing Dickens’s most famous work as sentimental dreck has proven far too easy for literary critics, myself included. Publishing on representations of disability in the writer’s fiction during my graduate studies, I briskly echoed the critique of those eager to reduce A Christmas Carol to its one-note characterization of crutch-wielding Tiny Tim.
Instead of awarding characters with physical differences the same complexity of motivation and temperament granted to others, Dickens’s early fiction tends to use disability to signal either unrepentant villainy or unwavering moral virtue. Though inspiring to many, Tim’s declaring “God bless us, every one!” in the face of premature death rings hollow with readers unreconciled to the sometimes fatal challenges accompanying disabling conditions.
Hitching Tim’s radically improved destiny to Scrooge’s philanthropy may illustrate love’s power to mitigate suffering—the boy’s new lease on life echoes the changed fortunes of the mobility-impaired Mephibosheth (2 Sam. 9:1–13). But to those dealing with unvarying, exhausting physical challenges, it can read as insensitive.
A similar skepticism reduces Scrooge’s own transformation to the preposterous. What are the chances that a bitter and grasping old man could become the town’s biggest philanthropist overnight?
What are the chances …
Christ obviously deals not in cold calculations but in the radically exceptional, in miracles that defy statistics and love that confounds expectation. Such inexplicable change is the essential element in this Christmas story, a shift in heart and soul predicated on what Flannery O’Connor called “possibility rather than … probability.” The best adaptations make Scrooge’s baffling metamorphosis spiritually plausible by reproducing the book’s stark contrast between despair and hope, then supplying a plausible bridge between the two. In Dickens’s hands, this bridge is a rickety, tremble-inducing route that so shakes Scrooge’s self-reliance that he must desperately grasp the rope lovingly thrown him.
Ti West, a director best known for a horror trilogy about a serial killer, is rather adept at unsettling his audiences, and in the new film Ebenezer (2026) he presents a redemption even more dramatic than Dickens’s original. As the ghost of deceased business partner Jacob Marley observes, altering the destiny of such a “man of the worldly mind” requires a serious scare.
At the opening, Ebenezer Scrooge (Johnny Depp) is a spiritually dead curmudgeon, the epitome of one whose unwillingness to risk loss has made his heart, in C. S. Lewis’s words, “unbreakable, impenetrable, irredeemable.” As closed off to the spiritual as he is to the philanthropic, he refuses to accept the earliest indications that something supernatural is afoot on this particular evening, throwing “Pooh, pooh!” at a door knocker that briefly resembles the dead Marley, “Humbug!” at the decorative tiles that take on that same, singular countenance, and “I won’t believe it” when Marley’s chain-wrapped ghost noisily ascends the stairs. In Dickens’s fiction, Scrooge refuses to accept what he sees until his partner’s ghost unwraps a head bandage, allowing his decayed lower jaw to drop onto his chest.
Ti West’s rendering of this moment is not as gruesome as, say, that in the version starring the late Patrick Stewart. But West makes up for it in other ways that underscore Scrooge’s stubbornness and dire need for phenomena terrifying enough to shatter his self-possession. And he needs to be shattered. “Sometimes in life,” observes West’s Ghost of Christmas Past, “it’s better to break than to harden.”
The slight crack in Scrooge’s composure created by Marley’s appearance widens with the appearance of this first of three Christmas ghosts, one Dickens describes as impossibly, simultaneously childlike and elderly, a strange figure “viewed through some supernatural medium, which gave him the appearance of having receded from the view.” Earlier films tend to simplify matters by swinging in one direction or the other, supplying us either an elderly man or ethereal young woman. West instead provides an unnerving ghost of indeterminate age with taut skin, uniformly white eyes, and raised spine who tilts her neck in creepy fashion and speaks in an echoing, ghoulish voice.
This specter pairs well with a visibly decaying Ghost of Christmas Present who lacks the mirth assigned him by Dickens and every other adaptation I’ve ever seen, one whose wheezing, dying corpse spawns the disturbing, child-sized pair Ignorance and Want. Dickens, ever attentive to the ways faulty education and poverty reinforce each other, wants readers to be as appalled as Scrooge by these shriveled, malformed “devils” and “monsters.” In the film’s most disconcerting moment, West allows these progeny of our collective indifference to break their way free of the now-dead ghost by bursting through his rib cage and scurrying after Scrooge, whose cruel business practices are clearly implicated in his current plight.
In other words, the disabling condition most indicted by Dickens’s novel and West’s film is actually spiritual in nature, a selfishness that breaks what charity seeks to heal. This film’s jubilant conclusion, made all the brighter by the frights that precede it, provides a perfect imaginative excursion for the Christ follower seeking pre-holiday recalibration.
