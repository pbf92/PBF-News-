TITLE: What does premature screen use take away from young children?
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Nearly half of Hungarian children are exposed to screens daily by the age of 18 months, yet what they need most for their development is play, physical activity, face-to-face interactions and active exploration of their surroundings. Early and intensive use of digital devices may pose risks not only because of the time spent in front of screens, but also because it may take time away from these essential experiences. A new study published in the journal Biologia Futura, with contributions from researchers at Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE), examines the developmental effects of early screen use and opportunities for prevention.
Although professional guidelines generally recommend avoiding screen use before the age of two, reality differs considerably from these recommendations. Based on representative data from the Cohort ’18 - Growing Up in Hungary birth cohort study, involving 2,569 young children, the research found that 49 per cent of 18-month-old children are exposed to screens daily, while 37.5 per cent spend more than an hour in front of screens on a typical day. Among children exposed to screens daily, average screen time exceeds two hours. Watching television is the most common activity, but the use of smartphones and tablets is already widespread at this age.
International studies have identified trends similar to those observed in the Hungarian data collected in 2019–2020. The Hungarian data were previously analysed by Krisztina Kopcsó and colleagues. Kopcsó, a researcher at the HCSO Institute for Quantitative Population and Economic Research, is the first author of the newly published study. The findings also indicate that longer screen time is more common among families with lower incomes and educational attainment, highlighting the importance of examining digital device use in the context of families’ living conditions.
But why might screen use at such an early age be problematic? Research in recent years has identified associations between early, intensive screen use and subsequent difficulties in language development, cognitive development and emotion regulation. One possible explanation is the displacement effect: screen use may take time away from activities such as physical activity, play and communication with parents. In the Hungarian study, for example, longer screen time was associated with less frequent shared play and reading.
“Young children learn primarily through actively exploring the world and interacting with others, rather than through screens. One of the greatest risks of introducing digital devices too early is that their use takes time away from precisely these essential experiences,” explains Ákos Pogány, a researcher at the Department of Ethology at ELTE and the corresponding author of the study.
It is not only children’s screen use that matters, but also that of their parents. Smartphone use can interrupt shared play and the face-to-face interactions that occur during feeding or comforting, which are important for language and emotional development. Moreover, children under the age of two generally find it more difficult to learn from screens than from real-life social interactions, meaning that even digital content marketed as educational cannot replace real-world experiences. Regularly using screens to soothe children may also reduce their opportunities to practise emotion regulation.
A growing body of scientific evidence, including findings from longitudinal studies tracking children’s development over time, suggests that
premature and excessive screen use may adversely affect development.
Although the precise mechanisms underlying these effects and the roles of different contributing factors are not yet fully understood, research findings increasingly point to the potential adverse effects of early screen use on children’s development, making prevention particularly important.
Parents play a crucial role in preventing adverse effects. By prioritising shared play, conversation and screen-free daily routines, and by being mindful of their own digital device use, parents can do much to support their children’s healthy development. However, they also need support: paediatricians, health visitors and other professionals have an important role to play, ideally even before the child is born. In addition, companies operating digital platforms and policymakers must take responsibility for ensuring that children’s developmental needs are not overlooked in a digital environment designed primarily to capture and retain users’ attention for as long as possible.
The study’s key message is that
what matters is not only how much time young children spend in front of screens, but also which activities are displaced by screen use and how digital devices affect the parent–child relationship.
