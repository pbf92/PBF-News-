TITLE: Chornobyl Radiation Only Partly Explains Thyroid Cancer Risk
OUTLET: Mirage News

Ignoring spatial patterns can lead to an overestimation of the association between radiation exposure and thyroid cancer
To the Point
- Ukraine thyroid cancer study: A study in BMJ Public Health examined thyroid cancer cases diagnosed across Ukraine in 2001- 15 years after the accident - using national cancer registry data and iodine-131 dose estimates for 627 districts and cities.
- Strong radiation-cancer association: Higher iodine-131 exposure in 1986 was linked to higher thyroid cancer incidence in 2001 among both men and women, with elevated rates not only around Chornobyl but also, to a lesser extent, in some central districts.
- Spatial modeling improves risk estimates: Accounting for geographic clustering, the study estimated that children aged 0-4 at exposure had about a 26-fold higher thyroid cancer risk at a dose of 1 Gy-about half the estimate from a model that ignored spatial factors-confirming the disaster's lasting health impact.
April 26 this year marked the 40th anniversary of the Chornobyl nuclear accident. To this day, this accident is considered the most severe of its kind and reached the highest level on the International Nuclear Event Scale (INES): Level 7- a catastrophic accident. At the V.I. Lenin Nuclear Power Plant's Unit 4 near Prypiat located in Ukraine, which was then a Soviet republic, an uncontrolled power surge occurred during a safety test due to design flaws and serious operator errors. Two violent explosions destroyed the reactor building and the roof of Unit 4. Over the course of several days, enormous quantities of radioactive substances-including cesium-137 and iodine-131-were released into the atmosphere and carried by the winds across large parts of Europe.
The ongoing war in Ukraine has highlighted the persistent risks associated with nuclear facilities in times of war. Russia's temporary occupation of the Chornobyl site in early 2022, an unprecedented event that brought a nuclear power plant under foreign military control, as well as the ongoing occupation of the Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant, have also raised serious concerns about the safety of nuclear facilities in war zones.
A recent study published in BMJ Public Health by Nataliia Levchuk, Jonas Schöley, and Domantas Jasilionis (all MPIDR), and Laura Cilek (Federal Institute for Population Research, BIB), presents the first nationwide district-level analysis of thyroid cancer incidence across all of Ukraine. The study examines how thyroid cancer incidence varied across Ukraine's districts 15 years after the Chornobyl accident and to what extent these differences were associated with radiation exposure.
Moving Beyond Earlier Geographic Limitations
"Previous research has largely focused on contaminated areas in northern Ukraine near the power plant or has used data aggregated (grouped) at the regional level. Although earlier studies found higher thyroid cancer rates in areas with greater radiation exposure, the problem is that these geographical patterns may also reflect other factors. For example, doctors may have looked more closely for cases of thyroid cancer in areas near Chornobyl. In our study, we accounted for these geographical patterns and unobserved factors. Even after taking these into account, we found a strong association between iodine-131 exposure and thyroid cancer incidence," says Nataliia Levchuk.
To analyze Ukraine as a whole, researchers used data from the National Cancer Registry of Ukraine on all thyroid cancer cases diagnosed in 2001 among people aged 15 and older, together with estimates of thyroid radiation doses from iodine-131 exposure in 1986. The radiation doses had originally been estimated for more than 22,000 localities by the National Research Center for Radiation Medicine (Kyiv) and integrated into Harvard University's MAPA Digital Atlas of Ukraine. The researchers combined these settlement-level estimates with population data to calculate average radiation doses for 627 spatial units (districts and cities) across Ukraine. They then examined the relationship between radiation exposure and thyroid cancer incidence.
Levchuk and her colleagues found a strong positive association between iodine-131 exposure in 1986 and thyroid cancer incidence in 2001. Higher levels of radiation exposure were associated with higher thyroid cancer incidence among both men and women.
Spatial Dependencies Matter
"Our analysis revealed significant geographic differences in thyroid cancer incidence across Ukraine. An elevated incidence was observed not only in the northern districts surrounding the Chornobyl Nuclear Power Plant, as previously reported in earlier studies, but also in several centrally located districts further south, albeit to a much lesser extent. By applying spatial smoothing, we were able to reduce random fluctuations in districts with a low number of cases and identify geographic patterns that might otherwise remain hidden in less detailed analyses," the researcher explains.
The spatial statistical model (ICAR-NB) used by the researchers takes into account geographic clustering in thyroid cancer incidence and the spatial distribution of radiation exposure. The estimated risk was lower than in the model that did not account for spatial factors. The absorbed radiation dose to the thyroid gland is measured in Grays (Gy). A dose of 1 Gy is a substantial level of exposure that is virtually never encountered in everyday life, but reached in the most heavily contaminated areas near Chornobyl. In the spatial model, among children aged 0-4 years at the time of exposure, the most vulnerable age group, a dose of 1 Gy was associated with an estimated thyroid cancer risk about 26 times higher than the baseline risk. The estimate was nearly twice as high in the non-spatial model.
"It is important to account for spatial dependencies when estimating radiation-related risks because neighboring districts may share characteristics that influence thyroid cancer incidence, like access to healthcare, screening practices, migration, and iodine nutrition. Our spatial model takes these geographical factors into account in an analysis covering all of Ukraine. Ignoring these spatial patterns can lead to a significant overestimation of the association between radiation exposure and thyroid cancer," added Jonas Schöley.
The findings provide important evidence of the lasting health impact of the Chornobyl disaster and show a strong association between radiation exposure and thyroid cancer incidence, even after accounting for unobserved spatial factors.
