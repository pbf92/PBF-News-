TITLE: Perfect employees and managers may not be the perfect match
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

A caring and supportive manager sounds like a dream for any employee, but could too much kindness turn ‘perfect’ staff members into exhausted workaholics?
New global research led by Adelaide University shows people with a strong desire to appear flawless to their manager are at risk of being overworked, due to their tendency to go above and beyond their duties to impress supportive leaders.
“We often assume that supportive and caring leaders always bring out the best in all employees. Our research challenges this assumption by exploring whether well-intentioned leadership can sometimes unintentionally encourage employees to work too hard and put their wellbeing at risk,” said first author Dr Lin Xiao Chu from Adelaide University’s School of Management.
“Employees that are particularly vulnerable are not the classic perfectionists – who want to be perfect –but those who want to be seen as perfect employees. In doing so, they will hide mistakes, conceal weaknesses and stay at work late to project an image of being flawless, reliable and highly competent to senior management.”
The collaborative research team undertook three studies, analysing data from more than 700 employees from various service organisations in China and the UK to see what impact supportive leadership had on employees who wanted to appear perfect, and whether it was connected to excessive work commitment and workaholism.
The findings consistently showed that employees who strongly wanted to appear perfect to their managers tended to become more overcommitted, which was associated with greater workaholism.
“Our findings reveal an interesting leadership paradox: even good leaders can unintentionally create pressure for some employees,” said Dr Chu.
“A manager who praises the productivity of an employee who is a workaholic, may unintendedly fuel the behaviour.
“The employee may interpret their manager’s support and encouragement as a reason to work even harder, struggle to say no, and become overly committed to meeting expectations in the false belief these behaviours will be beneficial for career advancement.”
The findings have been published in the journal Human Relations and come amid growing attention to workplace wellbeing, burnout and sustainable performance.
A recent poll found one in two Australians were facing workplace burnout, which can lead to feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion, negativity relating to work or reduced efficiency.
The report recommends further research into ways of identifying those employees at risk of workaholic tendencies under good leadership. Other recommendations include implementing leadership development programs and promoting better work life balance to reduce the risk of burnout.
“Good leadership is not just about encouraging employees to give their best. Leaders should make it clear that employees do not need to be perfect, that setting boundaries is acceptable, and that taking breaks or saying no does not mean they are less committed,” said Dr Chu.
Researchers from East China Normal University, The Australian National University, King’s College London, the University of Illinois Chicago and China Medical University were also involved in this study.
