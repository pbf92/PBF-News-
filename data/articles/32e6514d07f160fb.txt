TITLE: BACE1, an Alzheimer’s-linked enzyme, drives vascular injury in cerebral small vessel disease
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Cerebral small vessel disease is quietly common. It affects the tiny blood vessels that feed the brain, and in people over 60 it is more the rule than the exception. It is a leading cause of stroke and vascular dementia, yet it often announces itself only through subtle changes on brain scans, long before symptoms appear. Doctors can control blood pressure, but no drug directly targets the disease's core biology.
A study published in Science Bulletin now points to an unexpected culprit: an enzyme called beta-secretase 1, or BACE1, best known as a failed Alzheimer's drug target. The researchers found that BACE1 builds up in the cells lining cerebral blood vessels, where it cuts the insulin receptor — a key protein that keeps vessels healthy. Once the receptor is damaged, the vessel's ability to relax and maintain steady blood flow falters.
"Endothelial dysfunction is thought to be one of the earliest steps in cerebral small vessel disease, but what drives it has remained unclear," said Yong Shen, a professor at the University of Science and Technology of China who led the study. "Our data suggest that BACE1-mediated loss of the endothelial insulin receptor is an early and potentially reversible driver."
The team first looked at human brain tissue. In small arteries from patients with two common forms of the disease — hypertensive arteriopathy and cerebral amyloid angiopathy — the insulin receptor was diminished while BACE1 was elevated. The same pattern appeared in stroke-prone rats fed a high-salt diet, a widely used model of the condition. The increase in BACE1 was concentrated in the endothelial cells that line the vessels, rather than in the muscle cells around them.
Timing mattered. The receptor loss and BACE1 rise were detectable well before any stroke or bleeding occurred in the brain, suggesting the molecular damage begins early, while the disease is still silent.
The team then treated high-salt-fed rats with a BACE1 inhibitor that has been tested in people. The drug reduced BACE1 activity in the brain and blood, restored the insulin receptor in cerebral vessels, and revived the nitric oxide signaling pathway. Blood vessels relaxed better, resting blood flow returned toward normal, and markers of vessel constriction and blood-brain barrier leakage fell. Damage to the vessel wall — including loss of support cells and narrowing of the lumen — was also eased.
The functional payoff was substantial. Treated rats survived far more often, their brain scans showed much less stroke-related injury, and they performed better in memory and recognition tests.
But the protection depended on the insulin receptor. When the researchers blocked the receptor with a drug, or used a gene therapy approach to remove it from endothelial cells, the benefits of the BACE1 inhibitor largely vanished: vessels relaxed poorly again, stroke-related brain damage returned, and vessel walls deteriorated. Neither approach undid the drug's effect on the tight-junction protein occludin, which helps seal the blood-brain barrier, but both erased its vascular protection.
"BACE1 inhibition can act through more than one substrate, but our genetic and pharmacological experiments show that the insulin receptor pathway is essential for the benefit we see," said Feng Gao, a co-corresponding author of the study.
The authors caution that all results are preclinical. BACE1 inhibitors have a troubled history in Alzheimer's trials, where some worsened cognition. Elenbecestat was chosen partly because it had a more favorable profile than other drugs in its class, and the dose used in the rats was well below the clinically established safe human dose. Whether it, or a future drug designed to act specifically on brain blood vessels, can help patients with cerebral small vessel disease remains to be tested.
"The next step is to understand whether this pathway can be targeted safely and selectively in the cerebral vasculature," Shen said. "That is a clinical question, and it will take time."
