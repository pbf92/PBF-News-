TITLE: Spirituality and religiosity associated with better quality of life in older adults
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Researchers Alessandra Buja and Sohail Akhtar at the University of Padova in Italy conducted a meta-analysis of studies looking at aging adults’ quality of life in relation to spirituality and religiosity. Their findings, published on November 19th in the open access journal PLOS Aging and Health, indicated that either being religious or spiritual boosts one’s view of one’s life, and having faith generally confers a greater benefit than turning to it as a means of coping.
Though many studies have been conducted in this area, the findings have been inconsistent and have not always accounted for how the effects vary across cultures or between different religious practices. Understanding factors that contribute to quality of life among elderly people, however, is becoming increasingly important as the population ages. To this end, the researchers scoured the existing literature for studies that focused on adults above the age of 60 and recorded the participants’ experiences of faith as well as their level of well-being or satisfaction with their lives. The researchers identified 21 studies matching these criteria that also had available data, which the researcher extracted for meta-analysis.
Across this pool of over 5,600 elderly participants, the researchers found that both spirituality and religiosity increased life satisfaction. No difference in this benefit was observed between participants of different sexes, but location played a major role on the effect size. The majority of studies were conducted in countries in the Americas or in Asia. The positive effects of religion were far greater in Asian countries compared to American ones, perhaps reflecting local cultural and political climates. Studies in North and South America came from the US and Brazil, while studies in Asia were conducted in Iran, Pakistan, and India—all countries with high religious homogeneity in some cases codified by the national government.
The other most significant variable was how people engaged with their spiritual or religious practice: as a general part of their lives or as a means of coping with difficulty. Elderly people who viewed their faith as a regular component of their life received a much greater benefit in life satisfaction than those who only turned to their faith in trying times.
Dr Akhtar adds, “Across the studies we pooled, older adults who were more religious or spiritual reported higher life satisfaction and better quality of life. The link was moderate in size, but it was consistent enough to suggest that spiritual life deserves a place in how we support healthy ageing.”
