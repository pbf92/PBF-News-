TITLE: Belgian region halts Google data centre over unpermitted 3-hectare building
OUTLET: PPC Land

Inspectors sent by the Walloon Region ordered Google to stop construction on a data centre at the Ecopôle business park in Aiseau-Presles, in the Belgian province of Hainaut, on Thursday, November 26, 2026, after finding a building of 30,000 square metres almost finished even though the permit change it depended on had been refused in August. About 300 workers on site received a verbal order to down tools. A day later, on November 27, a regional government spokesperson said a report alleging "a major offence" was being sent to public prosecutors, according to The Guardian, which cited Agence France-Presse.
In Short
Belgian regional inspectors stopped work on a large Google data centre near Charleroi on November 26, 2026, because the building matched a version of the project that the Walloon government had refused in August. The refused version would have merged two planned buildings into one larger one and cooled it with water from the Sambre river, which the minister rejected over water needs during a drought. Google is challenging that refusal before Belgium's highest administrative court, with a public hearing set for January 7, 2027, and the region is now sending a report on the construction to prosecutors. The environmental group Greenpeace, which pushed for the halt, calls the site illegal, while the minister's office said the original 2024 permit for three smaller buildings remains valid.
What the inspectors found
Raphaël Stokis is the region's "fonctionnaire délégué", the civil servant who handles planning permits and their enforcement in the area. Speaking to the Belgian public broadcaster RTBF, he described the visit in detail. The following is a translation from French.
"Inspectors went to the site on Thursday," Stokis said, according to RTBF. "We found that a building of 30,000 square metres, 3 hectares, was almost finished, with the structural shell closed, even though the permit had been refused by the minister. There were some 300 workers on site, to whom we gave a verbal order to stop the work. This verbal order will be confirmed in writing to Google."
"Structural shell closed" is the French building term "gros oeuvre fermé": walls and roof up, interior fit-out not done. In other words, the building was not a foundation slab or a set of steel columns. It was a closed hall the size of roughly four football pitches, using the ratio RTBF applies to the whole site (PPC Land calculation).
The Guardian, in a report by Jon Henley published on November 27 at 18:21 CET, gave Stokis's quote in English: "Inspectors visited the site and observed that the building was almost finished, with the structural work completed, even though the permit had been refused." The same report said the verbal order "had been confirmed in writing to Google." RTBF's November 26 report, filed at 17:33, said the written confirmation was still to come. The articles do not show which version reflects the situation on November 27.
Nicolas Yernaux, a regional government spokesperson, went further on Friday, November 27. He told Agence France-Presse that a report was being submitted to public prosecutors alleging "a major offence: the illegal construction of a building covering 3 hectares", according to The Guardian.
Google's response was short. According to RTBF, which called it "very laconic", the company said: "we are committed to maintaining an open and collaborative dialogue with the regional government and experts regarding our investments in Wallonia." That sentence is translated from French. The Guardian quoted the English wording as "committed to maintaining an open and collaborative dialogue" with the regional authorities. Neither report carries a Google comment on the permit question itself. RTBF said on November 25 that it had contacted Google Belgium's spokesperson and received no answer at the time of publication.
Two permits, one site
The dispute turns on the difference between two versions of the same project. Google is building at the Ecopôle through its European subsidiary Crystal Computing, according to RTBF. The broadcaster describes the overall development as a complex of data centres spread over 53 hectares, "the equivalent of 74 football pitches", straddling three municipalities: Farciennes, Aiseau-Presles and Sambreville. The Guardian describes a 50-hectare business park on the site of a former coal mine, about 15 kilometres from Charleroi.
The 600-cubic-metre figure comes only from The Guardian. The daily figure above is a straight multiplication by 24 hours; none of the reports says whether the draw would run around the clock or only at peak load.
The original permit, granted in 2024, covered three "small" data centres cooled by air, according to RTBF and Greenpeace. In 2025, Google changed its request: it wanted to merge two of the three buildings into a single, much larger one and switch from air cooling to water taken from the Sambre. The Walloon administration approved that modification in April 2026. A month later, residents lodged an appeal against it, according to RTBF.
In August 2026, François Desquesnes, the Walloon minister for spatial planning, infrastructure and local authorities and a member of the centrist party Les Engagés, refused the modification. According to RTBF, he "pointed to the data centre's water needs to justify his decision." The decision came, as Greenpeace noted and RTBF relayed, while Belgium was going through a historic drought. The Guardian put it more briefly: the minister "revoked the modified permit over drought concerns."
What the minister's office said before the halt
The legal position was less clear-cut than the word "illegal" suggests, and RTBF set this out on November 25, the day before the inspection. The minister's office told the broadcaster that the initial permit delivered in 2024 "reste valable" - "remains valid". RTBF spelled out what that meant in practice: if Google follows the plans in the original permit, three "small" data centres, it is within the rules. If it builds the two "large" data centres of the 2025 modification, it is in breach.
The office then described the next step. "Regional officers will carry out checks to make sure that the work under way complies with the valid permit," the minister's office said, according to RTBF. It added: "If defects are found, an official report will be drawn up. The on-site checks will not drag on. If there is a report, it will be sent to the public prosecutor's office." Both statements are translated from French. The French term for the report is "PV", short for "procès-verbal", the formal record of an offence that Belgian officials pass to prosecutors.
Within roughly 24 hours, that is what happened. Stokis's finding on November 26 was that the 3-hectare building did not match the permit that remains valid. Yernaux's statement on November 27 confirmed the report was on its way to prosecutors.
How the stop order came about
The four reports describe a sequence of steps that runs from a citizens' appeal in spring 2026 to a prosecutors' file in late November. According to the sources, it unfolded in eight stages.
- Modification approved. The Walloon administration approved Google's 2025 permit modification in April 2026, according to Greenpeace and RTBF.
- Citizens' appeal. Residents appealed against the modification about a month later, according to RTBF.
- Ministerial refusal. In August 2026, minister Desquesnes refused the modification on appeal, citing the data centre's water needs, according to RTBF and The Guardian.
- Court challenge. On October 17, 2026, Google filed a request with the Conseil d'Etat, Belgium's highest administrative court, to annul the minister's decision and to suspend it in the meantime, according to Greenpeace.
- Interveners join. On November 16, several residents and Greenpeace filed a request to intervene in that case so they could argue their side, according to Greenpeace.
- Emergency request. Alerted by residents that work was continuing, Greenpeace investigated and, early in the week of November 23, wrote to the Walloon government asking for the work to be suspended immediately. RTBF dated this emergency action to Wednesday, November 25, and said it went to the minister's office.
- Inspection and verbal order. On Thursday, November 26, inspectors sent by the regional delegated official visited the site and verbally ordered about 300 workers to stop, according to Stokis via RTBF. The order was to be confirmed in writing to Google.
- Report to prosecutors. On Friday, November 27, a regional spokesperson said a report alleging the illegal construction of a 3-hectare building was being sent to prosecutors, according to The Guardian.
The Conseil d'Etat has scheduled a public hearing for January 7, 2027, according to Greenpeace and RTBF.
Greenpeace's case
Greenpeace Belgium is an environmental campaign group and a party to the court case, so its statements are advocacy, not neutral findings. Its press release, dated November 27, 2026, is titled "La Région wallonne suspend la construction du data center de Google à la suite d'une enquête de Greenpeace" ("The Walloon Region suspends construction of Google's data center following a Greenpeace investigation"). The release itself relies on media reports for the halt: the region ordered the work stopped, "as could be read in several media outlets last night," it says.
"Tech giants are not above the law," said Nadia Cornejo, a spokesperson for Greenpeace Belgium, according to the release. "We are pleased to learn that the site is now at a standstill. That should already have been the case in August, when the permit was annulled. This is a first victory for citizens and the environment. But we continue to watch closely what is happening in Aiseau-Presles. The construction in our country of hyperscale data centres, as hungry for water as they are for energy, concerns all of us and cannot go under the radar." The quote is translated from French.
On November 25, Cornejo had been sharper. "That Google continues to build while its permit is annulled gives an idea of the respect the multinational has for our regulations, our resources, the impact on our territories and on our citizens," she said, according to RTBF, in a translated quote. She added that the arrival of data centres "is a real debate for society and cannot remain confined to discreet agreements between industrial companies and our administrations."
The Guardian quoted Greenpeace in English: "The tech giant is continuing work, even though in August the latest version of its planning permission to build this datacentre was revoked." It also carried the group's warning: "The climate crisis will only intensify in our region. Against this backdrop, the construction of energy-guzzling behemoths cannot be allowed to go unnoticed."
How did Greenpeace know the work was continuing? RTBF said the group relied on satellite images. The Guardian said Greenpeace published a report "including drone footage". The two descriptions may refer to different parts of the same material; the reports do not say.
Greenpeace also pointed to Google's existing Belgian site. According to the group, as reported by RTBF, a similar Google project already built at Saint-Ghislain, near Mons, draws 1 billion litres of water a year to cool its data centres, and only a quarter of that water is returned, the rest evaporating in the cooling process. Those figures are Greenpeace's. Google's response to them is not in the reports.
Where the reports disagree
The four reports agree on the core facts: a halt on November 26, a 3-hectare building, about 300 workers, a refused permit change and a January 7 hearing. They differ on several details.
