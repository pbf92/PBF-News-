TITLE: Semaglutide and Alcohol Use Disorder: What the Trials Show
OUTLET: The Clinical Trial Vanguard

Semaglutide is the most talked-about drug in medicine, and alcohol use disorder is one of the conditions people most want it to treat. Enough of the evidence has now arrived in peer-reviewed form to say what it actually shows, which is more specific and more interesting than either the enthusiasm or the skepticism around it.
The Lancet Trial: A Real Effect in a Defined Group
The strongest evidence to date is the 26-week randomized trial published in The Lancet by Klausen and colleagues in Copenhagen (NCT05895643). It enrolled 108 treatment-seeking adults with moderate to severe alcohol use disorder and comorbid obesity, randomized 1:1 to once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide at 2.4 mg or placebo, with both arms receiving standard cognitive behavioral therapy. The primary endpoint was the reduction in heavy drinking days after 26 weeks.
Semaglutide met it. The semaglutide group had 13.7 percentage points fewer heavy-drinking days than placebo (95% CI, 5.4 to 22.0 points fewer; p=0.0015), with effects across multiple secondary alcohol-related and somatic outcomes. Adverse events were mostly transient gastrointestinal effects and were more frequent on semaglutide. Eighty-one percent of participants completed the full intervention.
Two features of that design matter for how far the result travels. Every participant was treatment-seeking and every participant had obesity. The trial tells us that semaglutide works, on top of therapy, in people who have both conditions and want help. It does not tell us what the drug does in people with alcohol use disorder and a normal body weight, and we should not read it as if it did.
The Oral Trial: A Missed Primary Endpoint
The contrast is the phase 2 trial of oral semaglutide published in the American Journal of Psychiatry by Schacht and colleagues at the University of Colorado (NCT05892432). Fifty treatment-seeking adults with moderate to severe alcohol use disorder received oral semaglutide, 3 mg daily for four weeks then 7 mg daily for four weeks, or placebo. The primary outcome was laboratory-based, cue-elicited alcohol craving at week 6.
The trial missed that primary endpoint. Semaglutide did not significantly reduce cue-elicited craving at week 6, and it did not significantly reduce drinks per day, a key secondary outcome. The encouraging signals were secondary: fewer heavy drinking days (b=-0.580, 95% CI -1.012 to -0.148), fewer drinks per drinking day, lower naturalistic craving, fewer alcohol-related consequences, fewer cannabis use days, and more participants moving down at least one World Health Organization risk drinking level.
Those secondary findings are consistent with the Lancet result and with the earlier nine-week JAMA Psychiatry trial of low-dose injectable semaglutide in 48 non-treatment-seeking adults, which met its primary laboratory self-administration endpoint and also reduced drinks per drinking day and weekly craving. But a trial that misses its primary endpoint is a trial that missed its primary endpoint. The honest reading of the oral study is that eight weeks at low oral doses did not move the craving measure it was designed to move, while the secondary drinking measures were encouraging signals pointing the same way as the larger trials. That is a reason to run the next study, not a result to build practice on.
What the Observational Data Can and Cannot Say
The patient-reported signal is real but it is a survey. A research letter in JAMA Psychiatry by Uscher-Pines and colleagues, using a nationally representative sample, found that people who started a GLP-1 receptor agonist commonly reported drinking less afterward. These are self-reports from people who were prescribed the drug for metabolic reasons, not an outcome measured in a trial, and the authors are careful about causal inference. They should be.
The cohort data is observational too, and should be labeled that way. A BMJ analysis of 606,434 US veterans from Washington University found that GLP-1 use among patients without a prior substance use disorder was associated with an 18% lower risk of developing alcohol use disorder, with 20% for cocaine and nicotine and 25% for opioids. A target trial emulation in BMJ Open of 40,703 adults who already had alcohol use disorder plus type 2 diabetes or obesity found that starting semaglutide or tirzepatide was associated with a lower hazard of alcohol-related emergency visits or hospitalization within a year, with hazard ratios from 0.74 against sulfonylureas to 0.35 against existing AUD medications in the obesity cohort. Associations of that size in well-controlled cohorts are worth taking seriously. They remain associations.
The population at stake is large. A cross-sectional analysis in JAMA Internal Medicine of 2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health data by Shuey and colleagues put overlapping heavy drinking and obesity at 8.6% of US adults, with 3.5% meeting criteria for both obesity and alcohol use disorder. The endocrinologist treats the weight and the psychiatrist treats the drinking, and until recently nobody asked whether one prescription was touching both.
What the Mouse Data Measured
The mechanistic story is often told as if it were settled. The Utrecht study behind it measured something narrower. In Pitx3-cre mice performing a cue-induced sucrose task, semaglutide at 1 mg/kg reduced the number of sucrose rewards collected and increased ventral tegmental area dopamine neuron activity during reward collection, but not during the cue. Lower doses reduced chow intake without changing sucrose intake or dopamine activity. That is a finding about food reward behavior in mice. It is consistent with a reward-pathway mechanism for alcohol, and it is the reason the hypothesis is plausible, but it did not measure human alcohol craving and we should not describe it as if it had.
Where This Sits Against Established Treatment
Alcohol use disorder has approved pharmacotherapy. The Kiefer trial randomized 160 detoxified patients to naltrexone, acamprosate, the two combined, or placebo for 12 weeks. All three active arms were significantly more effective than placebo on relapse, and the combination had the lowest relapse rate, significantly lower than placebo and acamprosate though not naltrexone. Those drugs, with disulfiram, are what we have, and they work for many patients when they are actually prescribed and taken.
Semaglutide is not FDA-approved for alcohol use disorder. It should not be presented, to patients or to clinicians, as a replacement for naltrexone, acamprosate or behavioral treatment; in the Lancet trial, semaglutide was given on top of cognitive behavioral therapy, not instead of it. What the evidence supports is narrower: a promising effect in treatment-seeking people with alcohol use disorder and obesity, where a drug they may already be indicated for appears to reduce heavy drinking.
What to Watch
The trial that will help answer the question in veterans is CRAVE, a phase 3, randomized, double-blind study in the Veterans Affairs system planning to enroll 622 veterans with moderate to severe alcohol use disorder, with a two-level reduction in WHO risk drinking level as its primary endpoint. It is recruiting. Until it reads out, it is a reason for patience rather than evidence that the effect is established beyond the Lancet population.
The question I would keep in front of the field is whether the benefit holds for people with co-occurring psychiatric conditions. The Lancet and Colorado trials enrolled people with alcohol use disorder; neither was powered to say anything about patients who also carry depression, bipolar disorder, PTSD or schizophrenia, who make up a large share of the people treated for alcohol use disorder. If semaglutide is going to matter in addiction psychiatry, that is the population in which it has to be tested, with psychiatric comorbidity stratified from the start rather than adjusted for at the end.
