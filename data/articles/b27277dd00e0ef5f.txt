TITLE: Is the future of work fractional?
OUTLET: Marketing Brew

Part-time executives called “fractionals” are popping up all over the corporate world, and depending on your role in a business, you either are one, may someday become one, or could eventually require one.
But what is a fractional worker, exactly?
“In this room, you don’t have to explain what you do to anybody, for once,” Meredith Dennis, the owner and chief strategy officer of FRAK, an annual conference for fractional workers, said in her keynote address last month to an audience of attendees, most of whom identified as full-time fractionals. “Show of hands: How many times do you get the line, ‘So you’re a consultant?’ Actually, no, I’m not.”
They’re also not advisors or freelancers or gig workers, or making ends meet between full-time jobs. Fractionals take on C-suite roles in growing companies to solve specific issues or achieve particular goals, and typically report to the CEO. They’re paid via monthly retainers or hourly rates, and don’t usually receive health insurance or other benefits.
“It’s not a fallback plan,” Dennis, who’s also a fractional chief marketing officer, told Morning Brew. “If you are looking for a bridge between full-time gigs, that’s probably freelancing or consulting or advising. Fractional really does need to be looked at as a career path and as a career trajectory.”
Though the community is small—just 75 fractionals gathered in Scottsdale, Arizona, for FRAK at the end of October—it’s grown rapidly in the last year: Fractional hiring demand grew by 149% between Q1 2025 and the same time this year, according to a report from Fractional Jobs. And as AI-induced layoffs affect job security and some industries face leadership shortages, fractional workers expect the style of employment to grow—meaning businesses should learn exactly what it means to hire a fractional.
“The momentum is real,” Dennis said. “This flywheel is taking off.”
Michael Meo, a founder and fractional chief product officer, told Morning Brew that fractional work has become attractive as the workforce becomes “increasingly distrustful of corporate environments” and experienced workers want to “build professional resiliency.”
“The 30-year Disney exec no longer expects her job to necessarily be there a month from now,” Meo said. “There’s job insecurity that has opened up a lot of very senior folks to the world of fractionals.”
Founders, he said, come at fractional work from both angles: They may engage in fractional work to help fund their own companies, then rely on fractionals when they “need expertise around the table, but they can’t necessarily afford a full-time” executive.
“On the founder side of things, it’s…having to work within more resource-scarce environments,” he said, “all the while understanding that having someone that doesn’t work full-time for you isn’t necessarily a bad thing.”
Startups that rely on fractional work are also pioneering a new type of compensation model that includes giving fractionals equity in the companies they helped build, Meo said.
In some industries, like IT and cybersecurity, fractionals can be used to fill in larger leadership gaps. But for the most part, fractionals aren’t hired to fill an open position.

The volume of business is too large, and that’s when permanent full-time leaders are required.”
Fractionals do their best work when their responsibilities are clearly scoped and communicated, which is up to the CEO, Schwinum said. Rather than looking for a “rock star” in a particular industry, CEOs should look for fractionals with specific experience solving the issues facing their business—that’s why a fractional’s performance should be measured on the impact they have on the company, rather than the number of hours they work.
“A fractional executive has to be able to look the founder in the eye and say, ‘I’ve solved this problem before,’ and then [they] are the right fit for the job,” Schwinum said. “Executives who solved very similar problems in very similar environments tend to be producing results faster and better.”
It’s also vital that fractionals are introduced to the company as executives and given the appropriate authority to do their jobs, Schwinum said.
“They’re responsible for a specific major function like sales or marketing or operations or finance,” Schwinum said. “They own KPIs.”
Taraneh Fultz, a fractional CMO who works in the healthcare industry, also told Morning Brew that it’s critical for a CEO to truly feel comfortable relying on a fractional.
“[Fractionals] need to take something off your plate,” Fultz said, “which means you need to trust them enough to allow them to take it off your plate.”
In some cases, fractionals can also be brought in to mentor current executives who need guidance. Todd Wilkowski, a fractional general counsel and partner at law firm FBT Gibbons, said in a FRAK panel that he sees such arrangements frequently with CFOs.
“You need to bring in a strategic CFO—a fractional—to come alongside this individual, mentor, develop, see what their abilities are,” Wilkowski said. “Ideally, you can turn them into a CFO because they didn’t have the development.”
But once a business has identified a need for a fractional worker, how does it find one? Full-time fractionals told Morning Brew that CEOs can turn to their own professional network, seek relevant thought leadership online, and browse LinkedIn, where many fractionals market themselves. That said, the onus isn’t necessarily on employers to find fractionals, according to Heather Sullivan, a FRAK conference attendee who works as a fractional CHRO.
“I need to be more visible in places that [companies are] playing, contributing thought leadership pieces around the problems that you’re trying to solve,” she said, “to make myself easier for you to find.”
At the FRAK conference last month, the in-group sentiment was strong. Attendees laughed (and groaned) as speakers shared anecdotes about the ups and downs of being a fractional trying to fit into a full-time world, illustrating a distance between how fractionals think of themselves and how employers interact with them. That tension is most evident in the realm of transparency in the fractional industry—something recruiters are grappling with, too.
Lemonaid, an AI-powered executive matching company, launched its Fractional Executive Marketplace in September to connect workplaces with “experienced fractional leaders across the C-suite and key executive functions.” Brian Shea, Lemonaid’s co-founder, told Morning Brew that he and his colleagues noticed an uptick in self-identified fractional executives and wanted to “support them”—both by helping them find their next gig and also encouraging transparency in the industry.
“There was no watermark that said, to be a fractional you must have or show competency in or experience [in a given field],” Shea said. “If we’re really going to support core fractionals, let’s get the posers out of the way.”
To do so, Lemonaid built a Global Fractional Readiness certification, which includes modules on “high-growth company dynamics” and “legal and compliance considerations,” among other topics.
“You can call yourself a fractional executive and say you’ve got any superpower you want,” Shea said. “That becomes really detrimental to smaller companies because every dollar matters, every hire matters.”
Verification is something that many fractionals already do on an ad hoc basis, by building informal relationships with other fractionals and potentially working together as a fractional executive team. But vetting was on FRAK speaker Schwinum’s mind, too.
He serves as the executive director of the Fractional Leadership Alliance (FLA), a nonprofit that works to educate the corporate world on what a fractional worker is and advocate for the fractional community, and feels that certification programs should be run by nonprofits like FLA, not “offered by companies with a commercial purpose.”
“The question then becomes, what is it that we would be certifying? Are we certifying the expertise that somebody has accumulated over a 20- or 30-year career? Is it how well they execute their fractional business?” he said. “Those are ongoing discussions.”
As fractional workers become a bigger part of the workforce, industry standards are one of many things they will have to confront and construct. That was part of the message Dennis shared with attendees during her keynote.
“There is a real opportunity over the next few years in establishing this as one of those fundamental options you can choose from,” she said. “We are the beginning of what this is going to become, and we’re getting to build it.”
