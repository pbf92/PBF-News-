TITLE: Self-Compassion Interventions Show Preliminary Mental Health Benefits in Young People
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A new systematic review synthesises current evidence on the effects of self-compassion interventions on psychological well-being in children and adolescents. The review notes that young people face increasing challenges that threaten their well-being, with self-criticism in response to failure serving as a key factor. Researchers investigated whether structured self-compassion practices could help reduce this self-criticism, improve coping mechanisms during setbacks, and ultimately support emotional health.
To evaluate the existing literature, investigators conducted systematic searches across six academic databases, comprising PubMed, CINAHL Ultimate, PsycINFO, MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science, from their inception through February 2026. The researchers extracted data in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions to maintain systematic reporting standards across all identified trials.
Methodological quality across the included trials was formally appraised using established assessment tools. The review team implemented the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 framework to evaluate randomised controlled trials, while quasi-experimental investigations were evaluated using the ROBINS-I V2 tool. These evaluations allowed the authors to systematically categorise experimental designs and identify structural vulnerabilities within the evidence base.
The overall review identified eight studies meeting inclusion criteria, representing a pooled total of 324 participants across child and adolescent age brackets. Notably, the literature focused on younger children was exceptionally sparse, consisting of only two studies that together encompassed 44 child participants, while the remaining studies examined adolescent populations.
Within the child cohort, self-compassion interventions were associated with statistically significant reductions in symptoms of anxiety and depression measured from pre-intervention to post-intervention. Furthermore, one of the child-focused investigations documented a statistically significant increase in positive affect among participants when compared directly against control participants.
Results among adolescent participants demonstrated more variable psychological outcomes across trials. While most adolescent studies reported significant reductions in anxiety symptoms, findings regarding depression and perceived stress remained mixed. In addition, trials involving adolescents reported no statistically significant changes in positive affect or broader emotional well-being measures.
The investigators determined that the overall risk of bias across the included body of research was rated high. This assessment was driven primarily by widespread methodological limitations in the existing literature, notably a consistent lack of participant and assessor blinding along with the complete absence of comparison control groups in several trials.
Given that the synthesis rests on eight studies and 324 total participants, the authors concluded that evidence supporting self-compassion interventions remains very limited and preliminary, especially for children. The review notes that future evaluations require adequately powered randomised controlled trials, qualified intervention facilitators, and child-tailored strategies to strengthen intervention delivery and home practice.
