Has the Riemann hypothesis finally been solved? Not officially—at least not yet.
On Friday, November 20, 2026, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician on the technical staff at artificial intelligence company Anthropic, posted a statement on his personal website claiming that he and Anthropic’s AI model, Claude, have found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis—a bombshell claim first reported by Scientific American.
"I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis," Alpöge wrote in a four-page note accompanying the release.

If independently verified, the breakthrough would settle a 167-year-old conjecture widely considered the most famous unsolved problem in pure mathematics—and mark a watershed moment in the rapid rise of AI-assisted scientific discovery. For now, however, outside mathematicians have not yet confirmed that the proof holds.
What Levent Alpöge and Claude are claiming
Alpöge’s announcement goes even further than Bernhard Riemann’s original 1859 conjecture. According to his note and the accompanying formal files, the proposed proof covers the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH)—a massive expansion of the original problem that applies to related mathematical objects known as Dirichlet L-functions.
- A massive formal archive: Instead of a conventional journal paper, Alpöge linked to 14 archive parts totaling roughly 239 megabytes and 175,187 source files written in Lean, a computer proof assistant that verifies mathematical logic step by step.
- The proposed method: In his four-page overview, Alpöge said he steered the research while crediting Claude with rapid late-stage progress, pointing to a strategy built around weighted prime sums (von Mangoldt sums) and chains of mathematical constructions called theta lifts.
- An emotional reflection: Recognizing the weight of the moment for human mathematicians, Alpöge struck an elegiac tone. "It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light," he wrote. "But what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater."
Why mathematicians aren’t calling it solved yet
Releasing a proof in Lean is a far stricter standard than publishing an unverified AI-generated essay, because Lean’s software kernel checks whether every logical step follows from established definitions. Even so, independent confirmation takes time.
Within hours of Friday's release, an independent verification project confirmed that the downloaded archive matched Alpöge’s mirror and that the top-level theorem statements compiled cleanly against Lean’s standard mathematics library (Mathlib). However, the full automated check replaying the entire proof was still running.
As mathematician and author Kit Yates and other experts noted, reviewers still need to confirm three critical things before calling the problem solved:
- Exact target: That the theorem proved in the Lean code is genuinely the standard Riemann hypothesis and not a subtly altered version.
- Faithful definitions: That all mathematical objects inside the code accurately reflect standard definitions.
- No hidden assumptions: That no unproven shortcuts or unintended axioms were slipped into the argument.
What is the Riemann hypothesis—and why does it matter?
Proposed by German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in 1859, the Riemann hypothesis is a deep conjecture about the hidden order behind prime numbers (numbers divisible only by 1 and themselves, like 2, 3, 5, 7, and 11).
The Riemann Hypothesis, Explained
While prime numbers appear scattered unpredictably along the number line, Riemann tied their distribution to the behavior of a complex function now called the Riemann zeta function:
- Trivial vs. non-trivial zeros: Mathematicians study the inputs where the zeta function outputs zero. "Trivial" zeros occur predictably at negative even integers (-2, -4, -6, and so on), while infinitely many "non-trivial" zeros lie inside a narrow vertical strip between real parts 0 and 1.
- The critical line: Riemann conjectured that every single non-trivial zero sits directly down the middle of that strip, on a vertical line where the real part is exactly 1/2.
- Why numerical checks aren't enough: Computers have already verified that the first 10 trillion non-trivial zeros lie squarely on that critical line. Yet a single exception anywhere beyond that range would disprove the hypothesis—making a universal proof essential.
- What a proof unlocks: Hundreds of major theorems across number theory and cryptography have been written under the assumption that the Riemann hypothesis is true. A verified proof would instantly elevate all of those conditional results into established mathematical facts.
Will Alpöge win the $1 million Millennium Prize?
Not anytime soon. The Riemann hypothesis is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a $1 million award.
Under Clay’s official rules, a personal website post and a code archive do not qualify for the prize. A proposed solution must first be published in a qualifying peer-reviewed outlet, survive at least two years after publication, and achieve general acceptance across the global mathematics community before the institute will consider awarding the money. As of Friday, Clay’s official page still listed the problem as unsolved.
Who is Levent Alpöge? Inside the AI math race
Alpöge is a well-credentialed number theorist who studied at Harvard and Cambridge, earned his PhD at Princeton in 2020 under Fields Medalist Manjul Bhargava, and served as a Junior Fellow at Harvard’s Society of Fellows before joining Anthropic.
Friday’s claim—arriving just one day after Anthropic’s stock debuted on public markets—is the latest salvo in an escalating mathematical race between top AI labs:
- July 2026: Alpöge drew headlines after posting a Claude-generated solution to the famous Jacobian conjecture.
- August 2026: Anthropic reported an earlier advance showing that at least 67.25% of the zeta function's non-trivial zeros lie on the critical line and are simple (up from roughly 41%).
- October 2026: Rival lab OpenAI released a massive dump of 722 AI-generated math manuscripts—including a claimed proof of a weaker "quasi-Riemann hypothesis"—drawing both intrigue and skepticism from mathematicians demanding human verification.
- Navier–Stokes claim: On the same day as the Riemann announcement, Alpöge and mathematician Tristan Buckmaster also posted a separate 79-page Claude-assisted paper claiming a breakthrough on another Millennium Prize Problem—the unforced Navier–Stokes equations—though its supporting numerical certificate has not yet been publicly released.
