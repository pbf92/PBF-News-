TITLE: Aquatic Exercise Shows Uncertain Effects on Depression and Sleep in Chronic Pain
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Psychiatry has determined that structured active aquatic exercise does not provide clear improvements in depressive symptoms or global sleep quality among adults suffering from chronic musculoskeletal pain. Conducted by researchers Luyi Shi, Qi Wang, and Qijing Deng, the rigorous investigation synthesized evidence from fourteen randomized controlled trials published between 1989 and 2026. Despite long-standing clinical interest in water-based physical therapy as a gentle, low-impact modality for individuals with persistent discomfort, statistical pooling revealed that pooled effect estimates remained statistically indistinguishable from zero across both psychological and sleep-related endpoints. The study authors concluded that current clinical data remain compatible with small benefits, no tangible differences, or even slight relative disadvantages, cautioning clinicians and patients against assuming that physical engagement in water inherently alleviates comorbid affective distress or sleep disturbances.
Chronic musculoskeletal disorders represent a monumental and expanding contributor to global years lived with disability, with persistent conditions such as chronic low back pain, fibromyalgia, and osteoarthritis imposing profound personal and socioeconomic burdens. In clinical environments, persistent pain rarely exists in biological isolation; it operates within an intricate biopsychosocial matrix where physical discomfort, affective disturbance, activity restriction, and chronic sleep architecture disruption continually reinforce one another. Depressive symptoms occur with elevated frequency among individuals experiencing unremitting pain, precipitating heightened healthcare utilization, more intense symptom perception, and greater functional impairment. Simultaneously, disrupted sleep patterns impair neurophysiological recovery and aggravate systemic pain sensitivity, establishing a debilitating reciprocal cycle. While physical exercise is widely endorsed as a cornerstone non-pharmacological management strategy for persistent musculoskeletal ailments, clinical research has historically prioritized primary mechanical indices, including localized pain severity and joint functionality, frequently presuming without dedicated empirical validation that functional gains automatically translate into psychological relief and restorative sleep.
To rigorously interrogate this critical clinical question, the investigators executed an exhaustive search across six international bibliographic databases—PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, and SPORTDiscus—from their respective inception dates through June 1, 2026. The systematic review adhered strictly to the PRISMA 2020 reporting standards alongside PRISMA-S literature search extensions and was registered prospectively with PROSPERO under the registration identifier CRD420261463810. Two reviewers performed study screening, eligibility appraisal, and numerical data extraction independently, resolving conflicting interpretations through consensus or formal adjudication by a designated third reviewer who verified all tabulated metrics against original trial reports. Random-effects meta-analytic models were constructed utilizing restricted maximum likelihood estimation paired with a modified Hartung-Knapp adjustment and a t-distribution reference framework to prevent artificial overprecision. Critical risk of bias evaluations were executed using the revised Cochrane RoB 2 instrument, and overall certainty across pooled bodies of evidence was graded systematically using the GRADE methodology.
Geographic representation spanned six countries, including Brazil, Türkiye, Spain, the United States, China, and Canada. Nine trials investigated fibromyalgia, two evaluated chronic low back pain, one enrolled individuals with ankylosing spondylitis, one focused on osteoarthritis, and one evaluated a combined cohort of rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis. Strikingly, nine of the fourteen trials enrolled female participants exclusively, including every single trial focused on fibromyalgia, while male participants were represented in only five trials. Crucially, none of the included studies required a diagnosed depressive disorder, clinical insomnia, or predetermined score thresholds on mental health instruments as eligibility criteria at baseline, meaning the synthesized cohorts reflected unselected chronic pain populations with variable baseline psychological distress rather than clinical psychiatric samples.
Intervention protocols evaluated across the trials incorporated diverse water-based modalities, including deep-water running performed at anaerobic thresholds, aquatic aerobic conditioning, multi-component mobility and strengthening routines, aquatic physiotherapy, respiratory aquatic gymnastics, aquatic Biodanza, and community-based swimming pool classes. Scheduled exercise durations ranged from 3 to 20 weeks, delivered at frequencies of one to five sessions per week, with individual sessions typically lasting 40 to 60 minutes in water maintained between 28 and 33 degrees Celsius. Total scheduled cumulative aquatic exercise exposure varied between 540 and 2,880 minutes among trials specifying fixed regimens. The systematic review established a vital methodological distinction by stratifying comparators into two mutually exclusive classes: Class A comprised non-exercise, usual-care, attention, passive balneotherapy, or passive physical modality controls, whereas Class B comprised land-based exercise, conventional physiotherapy, Mat Pilates, walking regimens, stretching, or structured educational programs coupled with active home exercise.
In the primary statistical synthesis of end-of-intervention depressive symptoms, structured aquatic exercise failed to demonstrate a measurable therapeutic benefit over either comparison category. When contrasted against Class A non-exercise or passive control conditions across two trials encompassing 327 participants, aquatic exercise produced an effect size of Hedges’ g of minus 0.24, with a wide 95 percent confidence interval spanning from minus 1.64 to positive 1.17 and a p-value of 0.279. When evaluated against Class B active land-based exercise comparators across five trials involving 317 participants, the pooled effect was nearly zero, yielding a Hedges’ g of minus 0.10, an associated 95 percent confidence interval from minus 0.55 to positive 0.34, a p-value of 0.566, and moderate statistical heterogeneity of 48.3 percent. The corresponding 95 percent prediction interval for land comparisons ranged broadly from minus 1.04 to positive 0.84, demonstrating that future clinical implementations could plausibly encounter substantial advantages or noticeable detriments.
Global sleep quality, measured uniformly across contributing trials using the standardized Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, mirrored the inconclusive psychological findings. For aquatic exercise compared against Class A passive or non-exercise controls across four studies comprising 246 participants, the pooled mean difference was minus 1.67 PSQI points, accompanied by an extensive 95 percent confidence interval extending from minus 4.93 to positive 1.59 points, a p-value of 0.201, substantial heterogeneity of 72.3 percent, and a prediction interval ranging from minus 9.46 to positive 6.12 points. When contrasted against Class B active land-based physical therapies across five trials with 241 participants, aquatic training yielded a mean difference of minus 1.51 PSQI points, with a 95 percent confidence interval of minus 5.47 to positive 2.44 points, a p-value of 0.347, and severe statistical heterogeneity of 94.6 percent. Because every pooled confidence interval crossed the line of no effect, the syntheses provide no empirical proof of superior nocturnal rest.
Exploratory sensitivity analyses revealed substantial structural instability and study dependency within the active comparative sleep evidence. Specifically, sensitivity diagnostics demonstrated that a single study conducted by Lopez-Rodriguez and colleagues in 2013 accounted for a disproportionate magnitude of apparent sleep benefit and statistical dispersion in the active comparator synthesis. Upon mathematical exclusion of that specific trial, the pooled mean difference for global sleep quality moved sharply toward nullity, shifting from minus 1.51 points to minus 0.12 PSQI points, while statistical heterogeneity plunged from 94.6 percent down to 43.2 percent. Similarly, an apparent subgroup interaction suggesting that programs lasting 12 weeks or longer outperformed shorter programs collapsed from borderline significance down to non-significance once this single influential study was omitted. Post hoc restricted-comparator analyses removing passive thermal balneotherapy or home education comparators failed to resolve underlying statistical imprecision, reinforcing that apparent gains could not be attributed specifically to the aquatic medium.
Methodological quality assessments performed with the Cochrane RoB 2 tool revealed pervasive limitations across the primary literature, resulting in all twenty-six outcome-level assessments being categorized at overall high risk of bias. Because physical rehabilitation precludes participant masking, and study outcomes relied entirely upon subjective, participant-reported questionnaires such as the Beck Depression Inventory, Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale, and PSQI, assessors judged that participant awareness of allocated treatment groups was inherently likely to distort reporting. Substantial attrition, unhandled missing outcome data, post-randomization exclusions, and absent prospectively registered outcome analysis protocols further compromised internal validity. In accordance with GRADE criteria, the certainty of evidence for all four primary comparisons was rated as very low, downgraded substantially due to serious risk of bias, striking between-study inconsistency, and wide statistical imprecision reflecting small total sample sizes.
The study authors emphasized that while structured aquatic exercise remains a well-tolerated, accessible therapeutic option for individuals who struggle with painful weight-bearing joint loading on land, current science does not justify prescribing water exercise specifically to treat depressive symptoms or insomnia in chronic pain patients. The heavy preponderance of female participants and fibromyalgia cohorts severely restricts generalizability to male patients and alternative chronic pain syndromes, while the absence of baseline diagnostic screening obscures potential efficacy in clinically depressed or insomniac individuals. The investigators concluded that future research requires prospectively registered, adequately powered randomized trials featuring verified concealed allocation, blinded outcome adjudicators, dose-matched active comparators, and validated domain-specific instruments before aquatic therapy can be credibly promoted as an affective or sleep intervention.
