TITLE: Exercise could offer an accessible treatment option for depression
OUTLET: Oxford Health NHS Foundation Trust

Exercise could play an important role in helping people manage depression, according to a recent commentary by researchers from the Trust, the NIHR Biomedical Research Centre (BRC): Oxford Health and the University of Oxford Department of Psychiatry.
The new commentary piece, “Exercise for depression: strong option with clinical benefits”, was published online recently in BJPsych Advances. It examines the findings of a major updated Cochrane review of exercise as a treatment for adults with depression.
The Cochrane review brought together 73 randomised controlled trials involving at least 4,985 adults with depression. Overall, the evidence suggested that exercise may moderately reduce symptoms of depression compared with receiving no treatment or a control intervention.
Exercise also appeared to have similar benefits to psychological therapies, such as talking treatments, while comparisons with antidepressant medication suggested a similar effect.
As an accessible and relatively low-cost option, exercise could help people with depression while also benefiting their physical health. It may also provide another option for patients who do not respond to established depression treatments, have difficulty accessing them or would prefer a non-medication approach.
Dr Daniela Almeida Borges, Clinical Researcher at the University of Oxford Department of Psychiatry and Honorary Consultant Psychiatrist at Oxford Health NHS Foundation Trust, said:
“Group exercise is already recommended in NICE guidelines for depression, and the Cochrane review’s findings reinforce the importance of clinicians considering exercise as a meaningful part of how we support people experiencing depression.”
Lena Beckers, Research Assistant at the University of Oxford Department of Psychiatry and lead author of the paper said:
“Exercise will not be the right option for everyone, but it is accessible for many people and brings wider physical health benefits. Importantly, treatment should be tailored to each person’s circumstances, preferences and ability to take part.”
The researchers also highlight important gaps in the evidence. Fewer studies have examined whether the benefits of exercise are sustained over time, and many did not adequately consider whether exercise programmes were equally accessible to people from different backgrounds.
Future research will therefore need to establish who benefits most, which approaches are easiest to maintain and how exercise can be offered equitably, including how exercise-based interventions can be integrated into routine mental healthcare.
Oxford researchers are already exploring different ways of using physical activity to support mental health. A recent study led by Brennan Delattre, doctoral researcher in the University of Oxford’s Department of Psychiatry, found that an eight-week salsa dancing programme reduced depressive symptoms in young adults, highlighting the potential of enjoyable, socially engaging forms of exercise.
