Shopify's Finkelstein: AI agents now drive real Black Friday sales, $2.3 million a minute
At the peak of Black Friday, Shopify's live checkout counter showed roughly $2.3 million in sales every minute. That's about 27,000 orders and 74,000 items a minute, with 26,000 merchants already having their best sales day of the year — numbers Shopify president Harley Finkelstein brought to CNBC's Squawk Pod in real time.
The number that matters more than any of those, though, is the one he says marks a turning point: this is "the first real Black Friday where Agentic is playing a real role."
Agentic shopping — software that searches, negotiates, and buys on a consumer's behalf — has been hyped for two years. Finkelstein's argument is that this is the season it started showing up in actual checkout data. Shopify sees about a 3.3x year-over-year increase in sales on its Shop app sourced through AI agents.
For anyone who owns retail stocks, that single figure is the loudest signal in this holiday season. The rest of the article is about what it means, who captures the money, and why the biggest fight in commerce is one nobody has won yet.
How AI agents change what people actually buy
The common fear is that letting an AI shop for you turns everything into a race to the bottom — a robot picking whatever is cheapest. Finkelstein argues the opposite is happening, and his reasoning is the most interesting idea in the interview.
"These agentic agents reward specificity," he said. "So what you're seeing is that consumers are not just saying I want to buy a phone for my kids. I want to buy a screenless phone for my kids."
That example isn't decorative. A search for "a screenless phone for my kids" surfaces Tin Can, a Wi-Fi-connected landline-style device, rather than a Best Buy shelf full of iPhones. Finkelstein said Tin Can has reported a 4x increase in agent-sourced sales. Because an agent will happily explain and compare instead of defaulting to the biggest brand on the shelf, niche products with a clear, specific pitch get pulled forward.
The result shifts the shape of the cart, not just the contents. "It's less about quantity of product. It's more about quality," he said. "They're not buying necessarily five different t-shirts. They're buying the one t-shirt that they really really love."
And far from being cold and robotic, he argues the experience gets more personal: "The AI era of shopping is actually making commerce more human because we are now having conversations with our agents that feel like personal shoppers."
The numbers behind a record Black Friday
Finkelstein's live dashboard and the broader industry data tell a consistent story: shoppers spent heavily, but they spent carefully. Average cart price on Shopify was about $115, up only slightly from roughly $113 last year — suggesting discounting isn't running hotter than prior years.
That last row is the one Finkelstein keeps coming back to. Shopify isn't just growing with the market — he says it captured about half of every new US e-commerce dollar created since January 2025. Independent industry forecasts support the scale of the season: the National Retail Federation projects $1.0132 trillion in November–December sales, up 4.2%, and Adobe Analytics put Thanksgiving Day online spending at a record $7.1 billion, with Black Friday projected at $12.9 billion and Cyber Monday at $15.1 billion.
Adobe's data also strips the "AI is a gimmick" argument of its legs. AI-referred visitors generated 83% more revenue per visit and converted at a 90% higher rate this holiday period — a sharp reversal from August 2025, when AI-referred traffic actually converted worse than ordinary traffic. That gap closing, and then flipping in AI's favor, is exactly the kind of inflection Finkelstein is describing.
The merchants trending on Shopify on Friday give a sense of who benefits: Lola Blankets, Manmade, Hexclad, Bombas, and elevated smart watches. Top categories were t-shirts, books, hoodies, and coats — everyday items, not doorbuster televisions. Finkelstein points to Comfort, a blanket-and-hoodie brand launched on Shopify in 2022, which he says hit $1 billion in sales in 2026, as proof that brands can now scale without traditional gatekeepers.
The trillion-dollar question: who pays whom?
Andrew Ross Sorkin pushed on the part of this story nobody has answered. When an AI agent — Meta's Muse, an Instinct-style assistant, or OpenAI's shopper — buys on a consumer's behalf, does the marketplace charge the agent for access? Or does the agent charge the merchant for delivering the customer? Whoever wins that negotiation captures the value.
Finkelstein's answer is aggregation. Because Shopify touches roughly half of every incremental e-commerce dollar, it can act as a bargaining agent for millions of small merchants and push their products "across every single surface area that exists" — online, offline, social media, and agentic surfaces.
He left the Amazon question open, which is telling. The answer will determine whether agentic commerce becomes a toll road owned by the big marketplaces or a referral business owned by whoever builds the best assistant. Either way, the ad-revenue and liability rules of retail get rewritten.
Leslie Picker asked whether agents might reduce returns — a real cost for every retailer. Finkelstein conceded "it might" but steered back to his quality-over-quantity thesis. The industry data is more direct: Adobe found roughly 69% of shoppers say they're less likely to return an item they bought with an AI assistant's help. If that holds, agents are a margin story, not just a sales story.
Retail's future isn't online versus offline — it's everywhere
Finkelstein's structural prediction is that the online-offline divide is the wrong frame entirely. "The future of retail will not be offline versus online but selling everywhere the consumer spends time," he said — and brands will get bigger faster than ever before, because the gatekeepers that used to slow them down are fading.
The physical side of the holiday backs him up. Dominic Chu reported from Westfield Garden State Plaza in Paramus, New Jersey, where lines formed before 7 a.m. at Aritzia, Abercrombie, Hollister, and Zara — a crowd skewing tween, teen, and young adult. Per ICSC survey data he cited, 84% of shoppers this Cyber Week plan to visit a physical store, rising to 90% when you include in-store pickup of online orders. If that plays out, it would be the highest physical mall traffic since 2019.
Chu's on-the-ground deals: Aritzia 20–50% off storewide, Hollister 30%, Urban Outfitters 40%, Timberland 30%, The North Face 30% (40% on $500+). He framed the shopper's gamble neatly — take 20% now, or risk a sellout waiting for 25–30% on Monday. Toys, TVs, apparel, appliances, and furniture are the best Black Friday buys; electronics and computers are better on Cyber Monday.
The draw for younger shoppers, Chu said, is experiential: touching the merchandise, socializing in line, and eating — Fogo de Chão and Shake Shack are both in the mall. That's the physical store's new job. It isn't where you buy the t-shirt; it's where you decide you want it.
The NFL is testing whether fans will follow games anywhere
The holiday's other big distribution story came from media analyst Rich Greenfield of LightShed Partners, who dismissed the complaint that spreading NFL games across streamers makes them "too hard to find." Local-market games still air on broadcast, he noted, and more households now have Netflix and Amazon Prime than have cable or satellite — so the marginal cost of access is low.
Wednesday's Netflix Thanksgiving Eve game and Thursday's three broadcast games on CBS, Fox, and NBC drew strong ratings, and Amazon carried the Steelers–Broncos game on Black Friday. On whether fans are overloaded: "I don't run into a whole lot of people unhappy that there's quote too much football."
His summary of the league's advertising power is blunt: "you can't be in this industry and sell ads at scale without the NFL."
Greenfield's structural prediction: at the next renewal — likely negotiated in 2029 for the 2030 season — the NFL may collapse one package and possibly create an international one, deliberately increasing bidder tension to push rates higher. The nearest hard date on the calendar is the change-of-control option at Paramount, which David Ellison acquired in August 2025; the NFL has until August 2027 to trigger renegotiation and hasn't yet. He also flagged noise that Netflix, with Peter Rice, wants a bigger slate of games than it currently has.
Meta wants the television, not just the phone
Greenfield argued the most underappreciated media story is Meta's ambition to get Instagram onto the TV. He pointed to Mark Zuckerberg demoing Muse on a $300 wirelessly connected camera-and-microphone device rather than an iPhone, and to horizontal video now appearing at the top of Instagram's Explore feed.
The precedent is YouTube, which used the NFL, Coachella, and soon the Oscars to force itself onto the big screen. Greenfield's open question is whether Meta cuts similar content deals to pull users onto Instagram on TV — and he suggested Muse could end up knowing more about viewing behavior than Netflix does. Sorkin endorsed the point ("do not sleep on what Meta is doing") while declining to recommend the stock.
The thread connecting Finkelstein and Greenfield is the same: the interface where the decision happens is being rebuilt, whether that's an AI agent choosing your t-shirt or a social feed choosing your next show. Value migrates to whoever owns that surface.
The rest of the morning: storms, a GDP spat, Nvidia, and tobacco
The news block covered four items worth noting. A Thanksgiving storm cancelled more than 450 flights and delayed over 2,500, with 56 mph gusts at LaGuardia and about 700,000 customers still without power, concentrated in Pennsylvania and New Jersey. The Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade was postponed for the first time in 100 years, to Saturday — hosts gamed out the cost of a second balloon inflation (Picker cited a figure above $1 million for helium) plus police overtime and hotel extensions.
President Trump called the Commerce Department's upward revision of third-quarter GDP — to 4.3% annualized from an initial 3.4% — "a record setting error" on Truth Social, suggesting the lower first estimate could have affected the midterms. Sorkin noted the revision is tied for the 16th largest first-to-second estimate change since 1990, while Picker argued affordability, not GDP, drove the election.
Nvidia has told partners including Dell and Super Micro to halt server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia, closing a route by which Chinese firms obtain compute through Southeast Asian data centers; the administration had called for closing that loophole earlier in November. Hosts questioned whether Nvidia's awareness of the loopholes creates legal exposure in coming congressional testimony, with the third session expected to be public.
And Altria is exploring an all-cash bid for UK-listed Imperial Brands, in talks with another party for part of its business. Bloomberg reported Japan Tobacco was weighing a bid, possibly with a partner. The deal could rank among the tobacco industry's largest and lift Altria to second among the most valuable listed tobacco companies.
What it means for investors
The through-line of the day is that discovery and distribution are being re-intermediated at both ends of the consumer economy: AI agents decide what gets bought, and streaming fragmentation decides where eyeballs land. Finkelstein's 3.3x agentic growth figure is early and self-reported by the platform selling the capability, so treat it as a directional signal rather than an industry statistic — Adobe's finding that AI-referred traffic now converts 90% better is the more independent confirmation that the shift is real.
