TITLE: How does hormonal contraception affect well-being?
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Millions of women use hormonal contraception – usually the contraceptive pill. In Germany, around one in four women between the ages of 18 and 49 take it. At the same time, there is considerable scientific debate about the possible risks to mental health. Researchers at the Universities of Göttingen, Leipzig and Witten/Herdecke used long-term data to investigate whether hormonal contraception affects well-being. They analysed data from over 5,200 women in Germany who were repeatedly surveyed over a period of up to 14 years. Their findings showed no evidence for effects on depression, life satisfaction or self-esteem. In addition, the psychological effects of contraception on individual women differed less than is often assumed. The results were published in Personality Science.
The researchers analysed data from women aged between 16 and 50 from the PAIRFAM survey, a longitudinal study investigating partnership and family life in Germany. In this study, participants provided information about their contraception and well-being over a period of several years. The design of this study enables researchers to observe psychological changes in the same person, for example when starting or stopping hormonal contraception. These effects can also be separated from other influences, such as a change of partner. Unlike experimental studies conducted under controlled conditions, the data reflect real decisions and life circumstances. This provides a realistic picture of the use of hormonal contraceptives in everyday life.
“Given the widespread use of hormonal contraception among women, the question of possible psychological side effects is highly relevant,” explains Dr Laura Botzet at Göttingen University’s Biological Personality Psychology research group. The results of the study contradict the notion that hormonal contraception reduces well-being, she says: “Overall, we found neither negative nor positive effects. Furthermore, our results challenge the widespread assumption that hormonal contraception has a strongly negative effect on some women and a strongly positive effect on others. We found no evidence of large differences in reactions between individual women.”
The researchers warn against presenting risks in a one-sided way: exaggerated reports of alleged psychological side effects could unsettle girls and women, according to the team. Waves of increased scepticism and avoidance resulting from “pill scares” in the past have shown that such discussions have real consequences. These include declining rates of usage and an increased number of unwanted pregnancies. At the same time, the researchers emphasise there are a variety of hormonal and non-hormonal alternatives available and that women should themselves decide on which method of contraception suits them best.
