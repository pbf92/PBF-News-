TITLE: Trump claims economy is booming and start-ups are at record highs, though data was released after midterms—too late to affect voting
OUTLET: voz.us

Several economic indicators that are favorable to the Trump administration were released after the midterms, including a sharp drop in gas prices and a jobs gain far exceeding analysts' forecasts. President Donald Trump has presented these figures as confirmation that the economy is improving, although the political attribution of price movements remains a subject of debate.
The drop in gasoline prices was particularly striking. According to the Energy Information Administration (EIA), the national average price of regular gasoline fell from approximately $3.995 per gallon in the week of Nov. 9 to $3.805 in the week of Nov. 16, a drop of nearly 19 cents in a single week. The AAA put the national average at around $3.75 per gallon in the middle of the month.
Trump: "Biggest drop in gasoline in 18 years"
The president celebrated the decline on Truth Social and stated that it was the "biggest drop in gasoline in 18 years." That characterization comes from an analysis by energy analyst Javier Blas, who on Nov. 11 identified a daily drop of about 3.6 cents as the largest in 18 years, based on AAA data at the time.
Trump also suggested that prices had remained high until after the election. There is no public evidence of coordination or deliberate price gouging for gasoline. Gas prices are primarily driven by the price of crude oil, global supply and demand, seasonal changes in gasoline blends, and geopolitical factors.
However, Blas stated on X that "If (yes, a big if) the wholesale market holds at current levels, American gasoline retail prices would be at a 8-month low by Thanksgiving weekend."
Employment exceeds forecasts
The Bureau of Labor Statistics' monthly employment report (BLS), released on Nov. 6, also came out after the midterms. Nonfarm payrolls increased by 165,000 jobs in October, well above the consensus estimate of about 65,000 jobs forecast by analysts.
The result was more than double what was expected and reinforced the image of a resilient labor market. The unemployment rate remained at 4.1%, while the BLS revised the August and September figures upward by a total of 84,000 jobs.
For the White House, the report provided a clear case. Job creation not only held steady but actually accelerated. The October increase was well above the monthly average of 48,000 jobs recorded over the previous 12 months.
Construction and manufacturing
Trump also posted on Truth Social a chart on job growth in nonresidential specialty construction, which showed a cumulative gain of about 119,800 jobs from January 2025 through October 2026.
This category does not exclusively measure "factory construction," although the label used by the White House presents it that way. It includes specialized contractors, such as electricians, plumbers, HVAC technicians and other technical trades, who work on all types of nonresidential projects, including factories, data centers, hospitals, offices and warehouses.
According to BLS data released on Nov. 6, employment in this sector reached 2,948,600 jobs in October 2026 and added 7,900 jobs in that month alone. The cumulative gain since the beginning of 2025 is around 120,000 jobs and reflects a broader expansion in nonresidential construction, not limited to manufacturing activity.
Manufacturing sector
Trump also shared a chart titled "Manufacturing Jobs Are Coming Back," which shows that manufacturing employment lost 179,000 jobs in 2024 and 113,000 in 2025, before recovering 101,000 jobs from December 2025 through October 2026. The figures match data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, which confirms the rebound and notes gains in computer and electronic products (+7,000), machinery (+4,000) and primary metals (+4,000).
The data supports the White House's argument that the manufacturing sector is regaining jobs after two years of losses. Even so, the figure of 101,000 represents the increase from the low in December 2025, not necessarily the full-year total for 2026, which has not yet concluded.
Favorable schedule
For critics, however, the timing does not prove manipulation. Energy prices and labor indicators are driven by economic and seasonal factors, and their release follows a preestablished technical schedule.
The political issue is not whether the data is real—it is—but whether its delayed release altered voters' perceptions. Falling gas prices and a rebound in employment provide the Trump administration with a solid economic argument, but the claim that someone "waited" until the election to allow for this improvement remains an unsubstantiated insinuation.
