TITLE: Scoping Review Finds Low Certainty for Light Therapies in HIV Symptom Relief
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Light-based therapies should be positioned strictly as adjunctive, symptom-directed interventions rather than primary treatments for people living with HIV, according to a scoping review published in AIDS Research and Therapy. Despite effective antiretroviral therapy, individuals living with HIV frequently experience multidomain symptom burden, including interacting mucocutaneous lesions, chronic pain, pruritus, oral infection, impaired healing, constitutional symptoms, and reduced quality of life. Although light interventions can modify local pathology and immune signaling, the authors determined that no modality is supported by high-certainty clinical evidence across any evaluated symptom domain.
Historically, clinical and mechanistic investigations into phototherapy for people living with HIV have been dispersed across dermatology, dentistry, infectious disease, oncology, and apheresis. To consolidate this fragmented evidence base, researchers conducted a scoping review structured under Joanna Briggs Institute guidance and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR standards. The team searched nine databases—PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed—from inception through August 9, 2026, with the protocol recorded on the Open Science Framework.
The search identified 55 evidence sources, comprising 21 Chinese and 34 English publications, representing 54 unique cohorts. Clinical data were charted by population, modality, optical parameters, symptom domain, efficacy, safety, immune or virological outcomes, and treatment burden. Methodological quality was evaluated using RoB 2 for randomized trials, ROBINS-I for non-randomized comparative studies, and JBI appraisal checklists for other eligible designs. Throughout the analysis, direct clinical observations in people living with HIV were synthesized separately from contextual photobiology and hypothesis-generating mechanistic links.
Across the reviewed literature, evidence clustered into five main domains: anogenital human papillomavirus-related disease, dermatologic disease and ultraviolet safety, oral conditions, Kaposi sarcoma, and systemic or psychological interventions. However, methodological limitations were widespread across all evaluated modalities. Randomized trials were mostly judged to have some concerns, non-randomized comparative studies commonly carried moderate-to-serious risk of bias, and uncontrolled or historical reports exhibited substantial methodological limitations, leaving overall clinical certainty low across the synthesized body of literature.
Among evaluated modalities, aminolevulinic acid-based photodynamic therapy (ALA-PDT) showed the most concentrated research support and the strongest relative signal for local lesion control in anogenital HPV-related conditions. Even so, confidence in ALA-PDT remains low due to risk of bias, study heterogeneity, and limited follow-up. Meanwhile, oral antimicrobial photodynamic therapy showed narrower, very-low-certainty support and may only be considered adjunctive. Photobiomodulation and ultraviolet B phototherapy similarly demonstrated narrow or very-low-certainty evidence in clinical cohorts.
The review established clear empirical boundaries regarding systemic outcomes and unproven therapies. Notably, a contemporary sham-controlled trial evaluating vascular photobiomodulation failed to demonstrate virological or immunological superiority over control groups. Consequently, current clinical evidence does not support vascular photobiomodulation for systemic immune restoration or virological control in people living with HIV. Furthermore, lesion-directed Kaposi sarcoma therapies, extracorporeal photopheresis, and bright-light therapy remain experimental or characterized by very low certainty within this population.
Safety boundaries represented another critical conclusion of the scoping synthesis, particularly regarding ultraviolet radiation. The authors emphasized that ultraviolet B therapy requires an explicit local HIV-transcription safety boundary due to potential risks of viral activation. Crucially, the review confirmed that no clinical or mechanistic evidence supports using light-based therapies for HIV eradication. Given these findings, the authors concluded that light-based interventions must remain adjunctive, symptom-directed options rather than replacements for standard antiretroviral care.
