TITLE: Natufian Figurines Reveal Early Artistic Traditions and Children’s Role in Prehistoric Israel
OUTLET: SSBCrack

Clay figurines discovered at Natufian archaeological sites in Israel suggest that people in Southwest Asia were making figurative art up to 3,000 years earlier than previously recognized, according to a new study by researchers from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.
The study examined 154 clay objects from Eynan-Mallaha in the Upper Jordan Valley, Hayonim Terrace in the Galilee and el-Wad Terrace on Mount Carmel. Fifteen are recognizable as figurines; the remaining 139 are cylindrical fragments that researchers believe may have come from larger figures.
The Natufians lived in the Levant from about 15,000 to 11,650 years ago and were among the region’s earliest communities to establish permanent or semi-permanent settlements. To assess the objects, researchers re-examined clay fragments that had previously received little attention. Microscopic analysis revealed fingerprints, tool marks and pigment traces, indicating that the clay had been deliberately shaped and decorated.
“These discoveries change how we understand the beginnings of artistic expression in this region,” said Dr. Laurent Davin, who led the study with Professors Leore Grosman and Anna Belfer-Cohen. “Thousands of years before agriculture emerged, people were already using clay to create figures, as part of their beliefs and knowledge of the natural world.”
The fingerprints also offer evidence about who took part in making the figurines. Researchers identified marks left by both adults and children, including some likely made by children younger than 10. On one human-shaped figurine, two overlapping prints appear to belong to a young adult or adult woman and a child who had not yet reached puberty.
The researchers suggest that children’s involvement was likely intentional. They may have helped shape or color the figures, providing an opportunity for adults to pass on practical skills and cultural knowledge. “What is particularly fascinating is the evidence that children were involved in making these objects,” Davin said, “suggesting that creativity and cultural knowledge were shared across generations.”
Of the 15 identifiable figurines, 11 depict birds. The others include a mammal, a gazelle and two human forms. Some bird figures are simply shaped, while others feature details such as wings, tails and feathers. Several may represent waterfowl, and others could depict birds of prey.
A number of the birds have their heads angled downward and turned to the left. Researchers say the posture may echo courtship displays seen in ducks during the breeding season. If so, the figures could have conveyed observations about animal behavior or seasonal change, and may have been used in storytelling or the transmission of traditions.
Several figurines also preserve traces of red coloring. The researchers identified evidence of engobe, a technique that involves applying a thin layer of colored clay. One figurine, dated to about 15,000 years ago, provides what the study describes as the earliest known evidence of the technique.
The research was conducted by a team from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem in collaboration with scholars from the University of Haifa, the Israel Antiquities Authority and French research institutions.
