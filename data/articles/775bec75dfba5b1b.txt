TITLE: Two mechanisms of amino acid transport in a rare disease-associated protein revealed
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

- A team led by IRB Barcelona and the University of Barcelona has identified how the same protein can transport certain amino acids with the help of sodium and others without needing it.
- The study, published in Nature Communications, combines cryo-electron microscopy, computer simulations, and functional experiments.
- The results provide new insights into a protein whose alterations cause lysinuric protein intolerance, a rare genetic disease.
Cells must constantly exchange amino acids with their environment to make proteins and support numerous bodily processes. To do this, they have specialized transporters located in their membrane.
A team led by Dr. Manuel Palacín, from IRB Barcelona, Dr. David Aparicio, Staff Scientist at IBMB-CSIC, and Dr. Raúl Estévez, from the University of Barcelona (UB), has explained how one of these transporters, called y⁺LAT1/CD98hc, uses two different mechanisms to exchange amino acids. The discovery, published in Nature Communications, resolves an issue that had remained open for decades.
"We've known for many years that this transporter could function in two different ways, but we didn't understand how it did that. Now we understand why sodium facilitates the transport of amino acids like glutamine and leucine, while others, like arginine, can be transported without it. The key is that sodium and the positive charge of arginine can occupy the same region of the protein and perform the same function," explains Dr. Manuel Palacín, head of the Amino Acid Transporters and Diseases laboratory at IRB Barcelona and professor at the UB.
A combination of techniques to unravel cellular transport
To reach these conclusions, the team obtained a three-dimensional image of the human glutamine-bound transporter using cryo-electron microscopy. Computer simulations based on this structure have allowed the researchers to identify the region where sodium is coordinated and to propose how amino acid transport occurs.
The researchers later introduced specific modifications to the transporter and examined how these affected the transport of various amino acids across the cell membrane. These experiments confirmed the proposed mechanism and showed that certain protein components are essential for sodium-dependent transport, though not for arginine transport.
New insights into a rare genetic disease
The transporter y⁺LAT1/CD98hc is linked to lysinuric protein intolerance (LPI), an inherited genetic disorder caused by mutations in the gene SLC7A7, which encodes for y+LAT1. These alterations hinder the transport of certain amino acids and can cause serious metabolic problems, as well as immunological and bone complications.
The association between this transporter and the disease was identified in the late 1990s, with Dr. Palacín leading the research. Since then, other studies have shown that the transport of glutamine by this protein also plays key roles in certain cells of the immune system and in those responsible for bone formation.
"Understanding how this transporter works can help us investigate whether some mutations affect amino acid transport differently and contribute to explaining the diversity of disease manifestations," notes Dr. Palacín.
The study was carried out in collaboration with the Bellvitge Biomedical Research Institute (IDIBELL), which conducted a substantial part of the functional experiments. Dr. Victor Guallar's team, from the Barcelona Supercomputing Center (BSC-CNS) and Nostrum Biodiscovery, a spin-off of IRB Barcelona, contributed the computational simulations, while Dr. Óscar Llorca's group, from the Spanish National Cancer Research Centre (CNIO), participated in the analysis of the structures obtained by cryo-electron microscopy. Other participating institutions included the Institute of Molecular Biology of Barcelona (IBMB-CSIC), the Autonomous University of Barcelona (UAB), and the Biomedical Research Networking Centre for Rare Diseases (CIBERER-ISCIII).
