TITLE: A Diving Club Stumbled on Nearly 2,000 Gold Coins on the Seabed, the Biggest Gold Coin Find Ever in Israel
OUTLET: The Daily Galaxy

A group of scuba divers came across nearly 2,000 ancient gold coins while exploring the Mediterranean seabed off Israel’s coast. The treasure, weighing around nine kilograms (20 pounds), had been hidden underwater for more than 1,000 years.
The coins were found in the ancient harbor of Caesarea, a Roman-era port along Israel’s Mediterranean coastline. Announced in February 2015, the discovery was described as the largest hoard of gold coins ever found off the country’s Mediterranean coast.
The treasure dates back to the Fatimid Caliphate, which ruled much of the Middle East and North Africa between 909 and 1171. But while experts were able to identify the coins, figuring out why thousands of them were lying on the seabed turned out to be a different story.
The Divers Thought They Had Found A Toy Coin
Members of a local diving club were exploring the waters around Caesarea when they noticed something unusual on the seabed. At first, they didn’t think much of it. The object looked like a toy coin, the kind you might find in a game. But after taking a closer look, they realized they were holding something far more interesting.
Rather than continuing to collect coins, the divers picked up a few pieces and headed back to shore to report their find to the diving club’s director. The Israel Antiquities Authority, as reported by the Guardian, later sent specialists to examine the site. Their search uncovered almost 2,000 gold coins in different denominations. The entire collection weighed approximately nine kilograms, making it the largest gold coin treasure discovered off Israel’s Mediterranean coast at the time.
The timing also played a part. Authorities explained that winter storms had exposed the coins, bringing them into view after centuries beneath the water. Without those storms, the divers might never have noticed anything.
A Fortune From A Lost Empire
The gold wasn’t just old, it belonged to a period when the Fatimid Caliphate controlled large parts of the Middle East and North Africa. The dynasty ruled from 909 to 1171, and the coins found near Caesarea were among the currencies circulated during that time.
Finding so many in one place immediately raised questions about where they came from and what they were doing in the harbor. Kobi Sharvit, director of the marine archaeology unit at the Israel Antiquities Authority, suggested that:
“There is probably a shipwreck there of an official treasury boat which was on its way to the central government in Egypt with taxes that had been collected.”
One possibility was that they were being transported aboard an official treasury ship carrying collected taxes to the Fatimid government in Egypt. However, there was another explanation.
“Perhaps the treasure of coins was meant to pay the salaries of the Fatimid military garrison which was stationed in Caesarea and protected the city,” Sharvit said.
The gold might have been money intended for soldiers rather than taxes heading to Egypt. At the time of the discovery, researchers had no way to confirm either theory.
A Shipwreck Full Of Gold?
There was also a third possibility: the coins might have belonged to a merchant ship that sank while trading between Mediterranean ports. Speaking to Agence France-Presse, Sharvit explained that further underwater excavations could help reveal where the treasure came from.
“There is probably a shipwreck there of an official treasury boat which was on its way to the central government in Egypt with taxes that had been collected,” he explained.
For now, the exact circumstances behind the gold’s disappearance remained unknown. And what about its value? Despite the nine kilograms of gold, the Israel Antiquities Authority refused to put a price on the collection.
Spokeswoman Yoli Schwartz stated that the discovery was so valuable that it was “priceless.” Finding these coins was pure luck. Keeping any reward for it is out of the question: authorities confirmed the divers will get no finder’s fee, and the mystery of where the coins came from is still unsolved.
