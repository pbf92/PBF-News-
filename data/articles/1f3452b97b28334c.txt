TITLE: Movement Screen Shows Limited Accuracy for Predicting Tactical Personnel Injuries
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

The Functional Movement Screen demonstrates limited prognostic accuracy when predicting subsequent musculoskeletal injuries in tactical personnel and should not be utilized alone for injury risk classification, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis published in BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation. Conducted by researchers including Fang Wu, Mingyang Wang, Qing Li, Chao Wu, Zhenxiang Wei, and Yunchen Meng, the study evaluated whether the movement screening tool could reliably identify incident training-related injuries across tactical populations.
The authors conducted their diagnostic systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with the PRISMA-DTA guidelines, registering the prospective review protocol on PROSPERO under the identifier CRD420261355876. To gather relevant evidence, the investigators systematically searched six electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, Scopus, and CNKI, covering literature indexed from January 2005 through July 2026. The search specifically targeted cohort studies that explored the utility of the Functional Movement Screen for predicting training-related musculoskeletal injuries among tactical personnel.
To evaluate the methodological quality of the retrieved evidence, the authors applied the QUAPAS tool to assess the risk of bias across all eligible cohort studies. Quantitative data synthesis relied on a bivariate random-effects model to pool diagnostic performance metrics across the cohorts. Furthermore, the team constructed hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic curves alongside Fagan nomograms to determine the overall discriminative capacity and practical clinical utility of the screening assessment.
Across the comprehensive literature search, seventeen separate publications meeting the eligibility criteria contributed nineteen independent cohorts involving a cumulative total of 8,970 tactical personnel. The quantitative synthesis revealed a summary sensitivity of 0.513, with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 0.405 to 0.620, paired with a summary specificity of 0.746, with a 95 percent confidence interval ranging from 0.665 to 0.813. The area under the hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.688, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.611 to 0.755, accompanied by wide prediction intervals.
The researchers conducted exploratory meta-regression and subgroup analyses to examine potential factors contributing to between-study heterogeneity. The method chosen to determine screening thresholds demonstrated a significant joint effect (P = 0.004) and was directly associated with screening sensitivity, whereas the broader occupational group did not exhibit a significant joint effect (P = 0.086). Complementing these metrics, Fagan nomogram analyses showed only modest shifts in post-test probability, indicating that screening results provide limited adjustments to an individual’s likelihood of sustaining an injury.
To verify the stability of the pooled findings, the authors performed leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, which demonstrated consistent and stable parameter estimates across iterations. An evaluation for potential publication bias using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test yielded a non-significant result (P = 0.135), indicating no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry, though the investigators noted that statistical power for this test was limited.
Based on the synthesis, the authors concluded that the Functional Movement Screen should not serve as an isolated tool for screening and categorizing injury risk in tactical personnel. While complementary evaluations such as the Y-Balance Test, isometric strength assessments, and loaded-march measures represent potential candidate tools, the researchers emphasized that any incremental prognostic value they might offer alongside movement screening still requires prospective validation.
