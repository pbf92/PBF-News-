TITLE: Uncertainties Cloud Evidence for Closed-Loop Vasopressor Systems in Critical Care
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic review and meta-analysis of closed-loop vasopressor (CLV) systems confirms improved short-term target adherence in hemodynamic management, yet a critique by Mistry and Nair in the Journal of Clinical Monitoring and Computing suggests the findings warrant cautious interpretation. The original review by Alvarado-Sánchez et al., which serves as a meta-analysis of this emerging technological field, provided a useful synthesis of automated hemodynamic control. However, the critique highlights significant methodological inconsistencies regarding patient classification, eligibility criteria, and statistical reporting that impact the generalisability of the reported results.
The review characterized its scope as including five perioperative trials and one intensive care unit (ICU) study. However, Mistry and Nair note that the COMAT trial, which enrolled patients with severe brain injury in a nonoperative neurocritical care setting, contributed data to all three forest plots assessing hemodynamic control. Furthermore, two other trials focused on postoperative ICU care following complex surgery. The critics argue that grouping nonoperative neurocritical care patients with perioperative surgical patients complicates the assessment of the clinical populations where CLV systems are intended for use. They suggest that separating intraoperative, postoperative ICU, and nonoperative ICU evidence would more accurately describe the populations represented and the limits of generalisability.
The critique also questions the consistency of the study eligibility criteria. The reviewers’ prespecified methodology required that trials report at least one hemodynamic-control outcome: the percentage of time within a target band, or time spent in hypotensive or hypertensive states. Despite this, a trial by Merouani et al. was included in the quantitative analysis of total norepinephrine dose, even though it did not report any of the required hemodynamic-control outcomes. Mistry and Nair note this creates confusion regarding how secondary outcomes influenced study inclusion. They advocate for greater transparency in distinguishing which studies met the primary criteria, as this lack of clarity affects the overall strength of the evidence.
Variations in how studies defined hemodynamic-control outcomes create further complications. The review pooled results for ‘percentage time within target,’ but definitions varied significantly across trials: two intraoperative trials used a 20-mm Hg band, two postoperative ICU trials used a 10-mm Hg band, and the COMAT trial used a 10-mm Hg band around a clinician-selected target. Because the percentage of time within a target is intrinsically sensitive to the chosen bandwidth and the placement of that band, the critics observe that the pooled mean difference of 33.94 percentage points may partly reflect these structural differences in outcome construction rather than a transportable effect of the CLV therapy itself. They suggest that future sensitivity analyses should account for these diverse target definitions.
Concerns regarding the statistical interpretation of adverse events also emerge from the critique. The review’s protocol specified a fixed-effects model when I² heterogeneity was below 50 percent, and a random-effects model when I² was 50 percent or higher. However, Mistry and Nair observe that Figure 4 reported random-effects estimates for both major and minor adverse events despite I² values of 30 percent and 10 percent, respectively. Furthermore, safety outcomes were markedly imprecise. Major adverse events were based on data from only three trials, resulting in an RR of 0.73 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.15 to 3.44 and a prediction interval of 0.00 to 1606.87. For minor events, the RR was 1.08 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.46 to 2.53 and a prediction interval of 0.04 to 28.23. Because these intervals span both potential benefit and harm, the critics question the moderate and high certainty assigned to these outcomes, as GRADE typically suggests downgrading evidence when confidence intervals include clinically important effects in both directions.
The critique also addresses the study’s search methodology. The authors searched MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). Mistry and Nair note that the Cochrane Library is a collection of multiple databases, and CENTRAL is a database specifically focused on trials that is already housed within the Cochrane Library. They describe searching both simultaneously as methodologically redundant. While this does not fundamentally invalidate the study’s conclusions, the critique emphasizes that strict adherence to standardized systematic review protocols is necessary for robust evidence synthesis.
Concluding their assessment, Mistry and Nair maintain that these points do not invalidate the original review’s central observation that closed-loop vasopressor systems show promise in improving short-term target adherence. However, the range of valid inferences that can be drawn from the pooled estimates is narrower than previously suggested. They emphasize that correcting study classification, clarifying eligibility decisions, implementing sensitivity analyses that account for non-equivalent target definitions, and reevaluating the certainty assigned to safety outcomes are necessary steps to strengthen the interpretation of future research. This exchange underscores the necessity for transparent methodology as these automated technologies move toward broader clinical implementation in high-acuity environments.
