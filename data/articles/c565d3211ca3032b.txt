TITLE: Amazon blocks 20 of 24 AI agents by name, the most of 79 top US retailers
OUTLET: PPC Land

Thrad, a San Francisco company that sells advertising inside AI chatbot conversations, today published an analysis of the website rules of 79 of the largest US retailers, finding that 16 of them, or 20%, now name at least one AI agent and explicitly let it in, up from a single retailer in 2024. Across the same 79 companies, individual invitations to AI agents rose from 1 in 2024 to 22 in 2025 and 63 in 2026, with Ulta Beauty alone inviting 11. Amazon went the other way. It blocks 20 of the 24 AI agents Thrad tracked, by name, more than any other retailer in the study.
In Short
A company that sells ads inside AI chatbots checked the public rule files that 79 big US retailers use to tell bots which pages they may visit, and found that one in five now welcome specific AI agents by name, while Amazon turns away 20 of the 24 it checked. This matters to brands and retailers because AI assistants increasingly read product pages on a shopper's behalf, and these rules tell them which stores' pages they may read. The catch is that these files are polite requests rather than locks, so a written welcome does not guarantee that an agent gets in, and separate security software can still turn it away.
What Thrad counted
The study rests on one file. A website can publish a robots.txt file at a fixed address, a plain list of instructions addressed to automated visitors by name. "These files tell automated programs, including AI agents, which parts of a website they can access," according to Thrad. A line can address a specific program, for instance the crawler behind an AI search product, and say whether it may enter. If a program is not mentioned at all, it falls under whatever general rule the file sets for everyone.
Thrad read those files for 79 retailers in 2024, 2025 and 2026 and tracked how they treated 24 AI agents. Each retailer was placed in one of three groups. Retailers that invite agents name at least one of them and grant it access. Retailers that are open by default say nothing specific about AI agents. Retailers that block agents name them and deny them access.
The study's definition of an invitation is precise: "An invitation means a retailer specifically names an AI agent in its website rules and gives it permission to access the site." Open by default, in Thrad's words, means "their website rules don't specifically invite or block AI agents."
Source: Thrad, November 25, 2026, except where marked.
The largest group is still the silent one. In 2026, 72 of the 79 retailers, or 91%, either invited AI agents or left access open by default, according to Thrad. Walmart, Costco, Target and Home Depot are all in the open-by-default group. The remaining seven, about 9% by PPC Land's arithmetic, fall outside both groups. The only retailer Thrad names as blocking agents is Amazon.
The shift toward invitations is recent. Ten of the 16 retailers inviting agents in 2026 started this year, according to the study, among them Ulta Beauty, Harbor Freight and True Value. The average number of agents invited by each inviting retailer barely moved between 2025 and 2026, at 3.7 and 3.9 by PPC Land's calculation, so the growth in total invitations comes mostly from more retailers joining rather than from existing ones inviting many more agents.
Shopping agents are a narrower slice. Thrad separately counted retailers that invite at least one agent "used for AI shopping, assistance, or search," a group that grew from none in 2024 to five in 2025 and 12 in 2026. According to Thrad, "These agents help power AI experiences where consumers can ask questions, discover products, compare options and get recommendations through natural conversations."
Change itself was not universal. Of the 79 retailers, 23 changed their AI agent rules at some point between 2024 and 2026, and 15 of those began inviting agents or expanded the number they invite, according to Thrad. The release does not describe what the other eight did.

Together, the top five hold 40 of the 63 invitations, or 63%, by PPC Land's calculation. The other 23 invitations are spread across the remaining 11 inviting retailers. In 2025 the concentration was sharper still: Gap's eight and Coach's six made up 14 of that year's 22 invitations.
The leaders are specialist chains and brands: a beauty retailer, an apparel company, a tool retailer, a regional grocer and a leather goods label. Walmart, Costco, Target and Home Depot sit in the silent middle.
How the study worked
Thrad's methodology note runs to two sentences. Put together with the definitions in the announcement, it describes five steps.
- Sample. Thrad picked 79 of the largest US retailers, "drawn from the National Retail Federation's Top 100 list." The announcement does not say which year's list was used, or why 21 companies on it were left out.
- Source. For each retailer, Thrad read the public robots.txt file for 2024, 2025 and 2026. No dates are given for when each year's version was captured.
- Agents. The study followed 24 AI agents "used for search, shopping, assistance and AI training." The 24 are not named.
- Classification. "The study classified retailers based on whether they explicitly invited AI agents, left access open by default, or blocked them," according to the methodology.
- Counting. Each AI agent a retailer named and granted access counted as one invitation. Adding those across all 79 retailers produced the totals of 1, 22 and 63.
What a written welcome does and does not do
Robots.txt is a request. The convention dates to 1994 and was written up by the Internet Engineering Task Force as RFC 9309 in September 2022, but nothing in the file stops a program that chooses to ignore it. Google made the same point from the other side in October, when its Search Central team described robots.txt as a protocol that governs whether a crawler enters a page, not what an AI system does with content it has already collected. Google's parser, its hosts said, reads only a few kinds of lines - which program is addressed, what it may and may not visit, and where the sitemap is - and drops anything else.
Compliance, then, is a matter of behaviour, and companies have started grading it. Cloudflare on October 16 outlined a plan to give its Verified bots trust scores for ignoring site rules, with robots.txt compliance as one of the first two signals.
The bigger complication runs in the opposite direction. A retailer can welcome AI agents in its robots.txt file and still turn them away at the door. Blend Commerce, a conversion rate optimisation agency, said on November 4 that bot protection blocked its AI shopping agent at 29 of 200 UK and US retailers. Blend could read robots.txt for 24 of the 29, and every one of them allowed the search crawlers used by ChatGPT, Perplexity and Claude. Home Depot was among the stores that refused Blend's agent. In Thrad's study, Home Depot is open by default.
Those two findings do not contradict each other. Blend tested its own automated browser, not a named agent from a major AI company, and bot management software can keep lists of approved visitors while challenging everything else. What the comparison shows is the limit of Thrad's method: robots.txt records what a retailer says to bots, while the security layer in front of the site decides who actually gets through. A study of one cannot speak for the other.
Amazon's closed door
Amazon is the outlier Thrad names. "The company blocks access to 20 of the 24 AI agents studied by name, more than any other retailer in Thrad's analysis," according to the study.
That position has been building for more than a year. On August 21, 2025, Amazon's robots.txt added six more AI-related crawlers, including bots operated by Meta, Google, Huawei and Mistral. On November 4, 2025, Amazon sued Perplexity in the Northern District of California, alleging that the Comet browser's shopping agent identified itself to Amazon's servers as an ordinary Google Chrome browser. And on September 20, 2026, Amazon said that Meta's Muse agent could no longer place orders on Amazon.com for customers.
The Perplexity case points to the weakness in any count of named agents. A robots.txt rule addresses a program by the name it announces. An agent that presents itself as a regular browser is not addressed by name at all.
Amazon is not alone among large marketplaces. eBay's user agreement, in force for all users since February 20, 2026, prohibits buy-for-me agents and LLM-driven bots unless eBay gives prior express permission. eBay is not one of the retailers Thrad names, and its rule sits in a contract rather than in a robots.txt file, which is another kind of door the study does not measure.
The commercial logic behind Amazon's stance is not discussed in the study. Amazon sells sponsored placements on its own search and product pages, a retail media network whose value depends on shoppers seeing those pages. An outside agent that reads the page on a shopper's behalf and reports back inside another company's app sits outside that arrangement.
Open by default is not the same as no policy
Walmart, Costco, Target and Home Depot say nothing to AI agents in robots.txt, according to Thrad. For at least two of them, that silence sits alongside detailed agent policies set elsewhere.
On January 11, 2026, Target and Walmart brought checkout into Google's Gemini app and AI Mode, through the Universal Commerce Protocol both retailers developed with Google. On October 21, 2026, Walmart set out terms for AI agents shopping on its site: customer profiles with spending limits, an invitation-only gateway for agents, and a warning that agents breaking the terms would be blocked. Walmart had already tested ad formats inside its own Sparky shopping assistant in the fall of 2025.
None of that appears in a robots.txt count. The largest retailers increasingly decide which agents get in through partnerships, protocols and contract terms, so the open-by-default label describes a file, not a strategy.
Thrad's stake in the answer
The company behind the numbers has a direct interest in the result. "Thrad enables advertisers to reach high-intent users inside AI conversations with contextual, in-chat ads," according to the company's own description. Those ads are matched to the subject of a conversation, a form of contextual advertising, and shopping conversations depend on AI assistants being able to read retailers' product information. Thrad says it reaches more than one billion users each month across Europe, the Middle East and North Africa, Asia-Pacific and the Americas. It gives no method for that figure.
The announcement makes the link itself. According to Thrad, the findings come as more consumers use conversational AI to shop, "creating new opportunities for brands to reach them through ads inside AI apps." Thrad's chief executive made the same argument.
"More retailers are recognizing that AI is playing a bigger role in how people shop, from researching and comparing products to deciding what to buy," said Andrea Tortella, co-founder and CEO of Thrad. "They're responding by making their websites and product information more accessible to AI agents."
Tortella then turned to advertisers. "For advertisers, the opportunity is to reach consumers while they're actively making decisions," Tortella said. "When someone asks an AI assistant for product recommendations or help comparing options, they're telling you exactly what they're interested in. That makes conversational AI a valuable place for brands to reach consumers with relevant ads."
The study itself measures permissions, not shoppers. It contains no figures on how many people use AI agents to shop at these retailers, how many agent visits turn into sales, or how ads inside AI apps perform.
