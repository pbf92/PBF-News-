TITLE: Brazil's ANPD names 8 platform types presumed to face child safety law
OUTLET: PPC Land

Brazil's National Data Protection Agency (ANPD) has issued an English edition of its guidelines on who must comply with Law No. 15,211, the Digital Statute for Children and Adolescents known as the Digital ECA, and on what the law's general "duty of prevention" requires of those providers. The 69-page document is marked "Version 1.0" and "Digital publication (November 2026)", with no day given. The Portuguese original, which the ANPD states is the official version and prevails over the translation, was adopted on October 28, 2026, according to Digital Policy Alert and the Brazilian trade publication Mobile Time.
In Short
Brazil's data protection agency has published, in English, its official reading of who must follow the country's child online safety law and what the law's core prevention duty demands. It matters to any app, platform, game, AI chatbot or ad-funded service that children in Brazil can reach, including companies with no office in the country. Running age checks or other compliance tools does not take a service outside the law, and the agency, not the company, has the final word on whether a service is covered.
Two questions, one document
The guidelines set out to answer two questions, according to the introduction: "(i) to whom does the Digital ECA apply; and (ii) what are the meaning and implications of the duties of prevention, protection, information, and safety ('duty of prevention') for regulated entities." The document does not deal in detail with age assurance, parental supervision or content moderation. It says so plainly, while describing the duty of prevention as "the central foundation for the interpretation and application of these and other obligations provided for in the Digital ECA."
A 13-person drafting team wrote the text. Camila Leite Contri and Lucas Borges de Carvalho reviewed it, and the same two people oversaw the English translation, which, according to the document, "was generated using artificial intelligence." For direct citations of the statute, the ANPD used an English translation prepared by Brazil's Ministry of Human Rights and Citizenship, "with minor adaptations." The stated aim is "greater predictability and legal certainty in the application of the Law."
The law itself dates from September 17, 2025. It has been in force since March 17, 2026, and its implementing regulation, Decree No. 12,880, is dated March 18, 2026, according to the ANPD's English-language Digital ECA page. A draft of these guidelines went to public consultation on the Brasil Participativo platform from April 30 to June 15, 2026, according to a legal alert from the law firm Trench Rossi Watanabe and Digital Policy Alert's event record.
Location does not matter, and neither does price
The first finding is territorial. The Digital ECA applies "irrespective of its location, development, manufacture, provision, marketing, and operation" (Art. 1). The guidelines compare this with Article 11 of the Brazilian Civil Rights Framework for the Internet and Article 3 of the General Personal Data Protection Law (LGPD). A video game or a social media platform that is "available on the internet and likely to be accessed by children or adolescents in Brazil" falls under the law "even if, for example, the provider is a foreign company or is headquartered in another country."
The second finding concerns money. "The user's lack of direct payment, when analyzed in isolation, does not preclude the application of the Digital ECA's provisions," the ANPD writes. Users who do not pay in cash are often "part of a business model sustained by their data, advertising, or other indirect monetization mechanisms." The conclusion is explicit: "digital services based on advertising or other forms of indirect remuneration are subject to the Digital ECA, provided they are supplied remotely, electronically, and upon individual request."
The definition is also functional rather than tied to particular technologies. According to the guidelines, it can capture "marketplaces, messaging services, connected devices, and similar services" even though the law does not name them, and it is written to cover "both current and future technologies, even if they are not yet available to the public."
The three-part test for an information technology product or service
The statute defines an information technology product or service as one "provided remotely, by electronic means, and supplied in response to an individual request" (Art. 2, i). The guidelines break that into three elements, all of which must be present.
Shared use does not break the chain. Subscriptions to educational or streaming platforms used by several people in a household still involve an individual request, and "the Digital ECA remains applicable."
Proportionality, with limits
The law adjusts some obligations to the provider. Article 39 says the duties in Articles 6, 17, 18, 19, 20, 27, 28, 29, 31, 32 and 40 apply "according to the characteristics and functionalities of the information technology product or service, modulated according to the degree of interference of the provider of the product or service over the content made available, the number of users, and the size of the provider." Article 34, § 2 tells the ANPD to ensure "differentiated and proportionate treatment for services of different nature, risk, and business model."
According to the guidelines, strictly educational, professional or public-interest platforms may receive different treatment. Other relevant aspects include whether a provider is for-profit or nonprofit, the type of content offered, the design standards adopted, "and the presence or absence of targeted advertising." The ANPD adds a warning in a boxed note: "Proportionality does not reduce the provider's responsibility; rather, it guides the form and scope of the expected measures." Every assessment must be done case by case.
Five named categories of provider
The statute and the decree single out several types of provider for specific duties on top of the general ones. The guidelines take each in turn.
Social networks
The statute defines a social network as "an internet application whose primary purpose is the sharing and dissemination, by users, of opinions and information conveyed through text or image, audio, or audiovisual files on a single platform, via accounts that are connected or articulately accessible, allowing for connections between users." The guidelines read this through its effects: such services are "typically characterized by direct and widespread positive network externalities," meaning they become more valuable as more people join. "As a general rule," social media platforms are to be treated as directed at or likely to be accessed by children and adolescents.
Labels carry no weight. Classification "is based on the activity actually carried out by the provider, and not from its self-declaration or the trade name it uses." Article 19, § 2 of Decree No. 12,880/2026 makes the provider's own assessment non-binding on the ANPD, which "may, within the scope of its authority, determine a different classification at any time."
The document illustrates the line with a school example. A 15-year-old student uses a platform run by her school, available in app stores, with a login tied to her student number and a forum for real-time discussion. According to the ANPD, it is not a social network: sharing opinions is not the platform's primary purpose, the forum is "merely one of the platform's incidental features", and access is restricted to students and faculty.
App stores and operating systems
App stores are defined as "an internet application that distributes and facilitates the download, for terminal users, of internet applications made available or accessible through its platform." Payment model does not matter: a store "may operate under free, paid, or hybrid models." The guidelines describe stores as "a first layer of agents with responsibilities" and add that they must provide "effective safeguards to prevent children and adolescents from entering into contractual or financial transactions without the proper supervision" of guardians.
Operating systems are "system software that controls the basic functions of hardware or software and allows internet applications, computer programs, apps, or other software to run through it." Because they come pre-installed and shape the device from first set-up, "a significant portion of the obligations under the Digital ECA falls on them," according to the ANPD. Together with app stores, they enable protection at scale, because "the same age assurance mechanism - in particular, the age signal - can be used by various apps accessed by children or adolescents."
Not every operating system is treated alike. Systems built on collaborative models and open source software, "which are primarily used in technical communities and have a low likelihood of being accessed by children and adolescents", are "not subject to the same obligations as proprietary operating systems used on a large scale by end users, especially children." The same modulation applies to systems on internet-connected home appliances. The guidelines also point to Article 2, § 2, which excludes from the definition "the functionalities essential to the operation of the Internet, such as the open and common technical protocols and standards."
Editorial control and licensed content
A service with editorial control is "an internet application whose primary purpose is to make previously selected content available, without the use of automated selection methods, by a responsible economic agent." The exclusion of automated selection, the guidelines say, "presupposes some degree of human evaluation and curation." Video streaming, music streaming and digital libraries are the examples given. "The mere hosting or recommendation of content submitted by end users does not, in and of itself, fall under either of these scenarios."
These providers can skip age assurance under Article 22 of Decree No. 12,880/2026, provided that:
"i - they provide children's accounts or profiles with content appropriate for the age group of the child or adolescent, in accordance with the age rating system, when applicable; and ii - they implement parental supervision, blocking systems, or restrictions on children's and adolescents' access to content, while respecting progressive autonomy and age ratings, where applicable."
Journalistic and sports content not subject to ratings and under editorial control is exempt from age assurance outright. They are also exempt from the Article 29 duty to remove content that violates children's rights upon notification, which the guidelines say applies "particularly" to services "such as social media platforms" where users share content "without any prior human curation or licensing." The exemption has a ceiling, though: it "does not exempt the provider from the duty to adopt the most protective settings by default (Article 7 of the Digital ECA) or from the other obligations under the Digital ECA."
"Likely access" and the eight presumed categories
The phrase that stretches the law beyond services built for children is "likely access". Under the sole paragraph of Article 1, a product or service is considered likely to be accessed by children and adolescents when three conditions are met:
"i - sufficient likelihood of use and attractiveness of the information technology product or service by children and adolescents; ii - considerable ease of access to and use of the information technology product or service by children and adolescents; and iii - a significant degree of risk to the privacy, safety, or biopsychosocial development of children and adolescents, especially in the case of products or services intended to enable social interaction and large-scale information sharing among users in a digital environment."
