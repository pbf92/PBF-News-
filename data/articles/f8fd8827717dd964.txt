TITLE: Underwater Resection Achieves High Complete Removal Rates for Precancerous Colorectal Lesions
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis evaluating underwater endoscopic mucosal resection for sessile serrated lesions of the colon has demonstrated a pooled complete resection rate of 90.5 percent and an overall adverse event incidence of 0.56 percent across seven observational studies. Colorectal cancer represents the second most common cause of cancer-related mortality across the globe, having accounted for approximately 1.9 million newly diagnosed cases and more than 900,000 deaths in 2022. Between 15 percent and 30 percent of colorectal malignancies arise through the serrated neoplasia pathway, where sessile serrated lesions advance through an intermediate dysplastic stage toward invasive carcinoma. Although precursor serrated lesions may remain indolent for more than ten years, lesions that acquire dysplasia can progress rapidly to microsatellite instability-high or microsatellite stable colorectal cancer. Because these flat or slightly elevated mucosal growths frequently exhibit indistinct borders, adherent mucus caps, and right-sided colonic predominance, endoscopic detection and complete removal have historically presented formidable clinical challenges that contribute directly to post-colonoscopy interval cancers.
Endoscopic management strategies for serrated colonic lesions have historically incorporated cold snare polypectomy, conventional endoscopic mucosal resection with submucosal lifting, and endoscopic submucosal dissection, each presenting distinct procedural trade-offs. Cold snare resection has gained broad popularity due to its favorable safety profile and complete avoidance of electrocautery-induced thermal injury, but its practical utility decreases significantly when confronting large or fibrotic lesions where piecemeal removal is routinely required and recurrence risks may be higher. Conversely, conventional endoscopic mucosal resection relies on the submucosal injection of fluid cushions to separate tissue layers, yet this elevation can paradoxically flatten mucosal borders and hinder snare capture of flat serrated tissue, leaving patients vulnerable to residual disease. Endoscopic submucosal dissection achieves histological complete removal rates exceeding 90 percent with near-zero recurrence, but requires extensive procedural time, advanced technical specialization, and carries a non-negligible hazard of severe adverse events. To mitigate these specific drawbacks, underwater endoscopic mucosal resection was introduced in 2012 by Binmoeller and colleagues to resect lesions without submucosal injection.
Sessile serrated lesions differ biomechanically from conventional adenomatous polyps and neuroendocrine tumors through lower cellular density, marked crypt dilation, and abundant mucin content, structural features that confer higher buoyancy in an aqueous environment and theoretically facilitate endoscopic snare capture. However, the soft consistency and poorly defined margins of serrated tissue can also blend seamlessly into adjacent mucosal folds under water immersion, complicating precise margin delineation. Although prior meta-analyses established the general safety of underwater resection for mixed colorectal lesions, histological data dedicated specifically to sessile serrated lesions remained sparse, prompting investigators to conduct this registered investigation to synthesize dedicated efficacy and safety evidence.
To rigorously aggregate available clinical evidence, investigators registered the systematic review protocol with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews under registration CRD420252083459 and followed the methodological guidelines established by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions alongside the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement. The study protocol received institutional review board and research ethics committee approval under identifier CCEP 5430/25. Independent reviewers Ricardo Almeida and Paula Santo conducted comprehensive electronic searches across the PubMed, Cochrane Library, and EMBASE databases through June 28, 2025, using structured search algorithms coupling underwater resection with serrated lesion terms without applying geographic or language limitations. The predefined eligibility criteria mandated prospective or retrospective observational studies or randomized clinical trials enrolling adult patients aged eighteen years or older undergoing underwater endoscopic mucosal resection for sessile serrated lesions and reporting at least one target outcome. Historical terminology equating sessile serrated adenomas with sessile serrated lesions was accepted unless distinct criteria were specified.
The primary outcome of the meta-analysis was histologically confirmed R0 resection, strictly defined as en bloc specimen removal with both lateral horizontal and vertical deep margins completely free of serrated tissue upon pathological examination. Key secondary clinical endpoints included macroscopic complete resection achieved during the index endoscopic procedure, en bloc resection defined as removal of the entire target lesion in a single contiguous specimen piece, recurrence established by histological verification of serrated tissue during surveillance colonoscopy, and safety profiles encompassing overall adverse events, post-discharge delayed bleeding, and colonic perforation. Methodological quality and risk of bias across the included non-randomized studies were systematically appraised using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool across seven key domains. Statistical pooling was performed in R software version 4.5.1 utilizing a restricted maximum likelihood random-effects model, applying arcsine transformations for event proportions falling outside the 0.2 to 0.8 range and logit transformations with generalized linear mixed models for remaining endpoints.
From an initial yield of 114 published records identified across database queries, 32 full-text articles underwent detailed eligibility appraisal, resulting in seven observational studies that fulfilled all formal criteria, comprising five prospective cohort investigations and two retrospective series encompassing 581 patients with 581 lesions. Across these investigations, median patient age ranged between 54.5 and 68.5 years, and median polyp size varied from 12 millimeters to 24.6 millimeters.
Quantitative synthesis of primary histological outcomes revealed that underwater endoscopic mucosal resection achieved a pooled R0 resection rate of 72.53 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 58.44 percent to 83.22 percent across five studies encompassing 252 evaluated lesions, accompanied by substantial between-study statistical heterogeneity reflected in an I-squared value of 64 percent. When researchers conducted a stratified subgroup analysis restricted exclusively to lesions measuring 10 millimeters or greater in diameter, the pooled R0 resection rate dropped to 63.64 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 53.13 percent to 72.99 percent, while between-study heterogeneity completely resolved to an I-squared value of zero percent. Endoscopic en bloc resection in a single piece was reported across six studies comprising 536 lesions, yielding a pooled proportion of 80.4 percent with a 95 percent confidence interval of 65.04 percent to 90.05 percent and an I-squared value of 86.7 percent, which decreased to 74.29 percent among lesions measuring 10 millimeters or larger.
Macroscopic complete resection was documented across five investigations evaluating 520 sessile serrated lesions, producing a pooled success rate of 90.5 percent with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 69.57 percent to 100 percent, alongside high statistical heterogeneity of 96.1 percent. Regarding safety outcomes evaluated across all seven studies comprising the full cohort of 581 lesions, the pooled incidence of procedure-related adverse events was 0.56 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.00 percent to 1.74 percent and an I-squared value of zero percent. Specific complication reporting identified six delayed post-procedural hemorrhages occurring after discharge from the endoscopy unit, corresponding to a pooled rate of 0.04 percent. Colonic perforations were documented in five total cases, accounting for a pooled rate of 0.86 percent across two studies, consisting of four cases reported in the large cohort by Nett and colleagues and a single case identified in the prospective investigation by Tanaka and colleagues.
Assessment of post-resection recurrence was available from three studies tracking surveillance colonoscopies across 380 lesions, demonstrating an exceptionally low pooled local recurrence rate of 2.37 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval between 1.24 percent and 4.49 percent and zero percent statistical heterogeneity. Within these follow-up cohorts, Toyosawa and colleagues observed zero residual lesions, Tanaka and colleagues identified three recurrences without describing specific histological details, and Nett and colleagues reported a single tubular adenoma on post-resection scar biopsy accounting for 0.42 percent, which authors noted likely reflected specimen contamination rather than true persistent serrated tissue regrowth.
Despite these favorable pooled metrics, the systematic review authors highlighted several vital methodological constraints that must temper clinical interpretation. Every included investigation was observational in nature and carried a moderate baseline risk of bias under the ROBINS-I tool due to inherent confounding and participant selection, while Kagemoto and colleagues carried a serious risk of bias due to missing outcome data. Significant heterogeneity across en bloc and R0 resection outcomes was likely influenced by variations in operator expertise, particularly because several studies involved endoscopists trained directly by the originator of underwater resection, alongside procedural variations such as cap-assisted devices or mucosal marking. Additionally, long-term surveillance remains limited, and direct head-to-head randomized comparisons against cold endoscopic mucosal resection, conventional injection-assisted mucosal resection, or endoscopic submucosal dissection are entirely lacking. The authors concluded that while underwater endoscopic mucosal resection is safe, effective, and technically accessible for sessile serrated lesions, randomized comparative trials remain essential before establishing it as a primary universal standard.
