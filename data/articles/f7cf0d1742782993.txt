Anthropic Researcher Says Claude Helped Him Prove Riemann Hypothesis, as Human Rival Posts His Own Draft
An Anthropic mathematician says he has produced a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the 167-year-old conjecture about the distribution of prime numbers, and credits the company's Claude model with helping him get there. The claim landed the same week Anthropic became the first frontier AI lab to go public, and it has not yet been independently verified.
Levent Alpöge published the work, written in the Lean proof language, on his personal website. In an accompanying essay he said he texted Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei late on a Sunday night about how to announce the result, and wrote that "the precise timing is horrible." The publication came after the company's Thursday IPO. Anthropic has not commented on the announcement or said it was involved.
The Riemann hypothesis, first proposed in 1859, holds that the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function all sit on a single vertical line. A proof would expose a hidden order beneath the apparent randomness of the primes. It is one of seven Millennium Prize problems, each carrying a $1 million award from the Clay Mathematics Institute. The institute notes that the first ten trillion zeros have already been checked, but a single zero found off the line beyond that range would still disprove the hypothesis.

Alpöge's document has not been accepted by the institute, and under its rules a proposed solution must be published and survive at least two years of scrutiny, including "general acceptance in the global mathematics community," before it can be considered for the prize. Only one Millennium problem, the Poincaré conjecture, has been officially resolved in the quarter-century since the awards were created.
The announcement drew immediate attention from number theorists, who began dissecting the argument in a public Zulip chat. Chris Birbeck, a number theorist at the University of East Anglia, described the cohomology bound as resting on what appears to be an automorphy-lifting argument, invoking Galois representations attached to regular algebraic, polarized cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n over CM fields, along with base change, Jacquet-Shalika, Clozel's purity, Bernstein-Zelevinsky, the main theorem of CM and Deligne-Ribet. Mathematicians in the thread appeared largely confident the formalized Lean result will hold, though a full human-readable explanation is still to come.
The formal side has since moved faster than the mathematical one. MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland reported on Nov. 20 that all 175,187 Lean modules in the release compiled and passed replay through Lean’s kernel, the component that checks each deduction, and that a separately implemented checker, nanoda, accepted the full exported proof. His audit found that the five headline theorems, including the classical Riemann hypothesis and statements about Dirichlet L-functions, depend only on Lean’s three standard axioms. An end-to-end comparison run and the mathematical community’s judgment of what the statements mean are still outstanding.
Alpöge wrote that his obsession with the problem since childhood left him unable to wait, and described the process as a "stream-of-consciousness jaunt" through confusion with Claude, saying the model "flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line." He has committed to producing an explanation that mathematicians can read and check against each other.
The same day brought a second claim. Ravi Vakil, a Stanford professor and president of the American Mathematical Society, posted a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis written by Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo, noting that "it is all human, with no AI." Vakil said the plan had been to bring Masullo to Stanford for a year to polish the writing and circulate it widely before announcing, but that "now it seems best to put out the current draft." Masullo said the work represents twelve years of effort and offered a message to mathematicians unsettled by the current pace: "we are still here. We are very much alive. We are not going anywhere."
Anthropic said in August that it was working on the Riemann hypothesis with Claude and reported that at least 67.25% of the nontrivial zeros, in the asymptotic sense, lie on the critical line and are simple, but cautioned at the time: "We don't expect that the techniques Claude used will lead to proving the Riemann hypothesis."
The claims extend a frenetic year in which frontier labs have pushed into pure mathematics. OpenAI said in September that it had cracked the Navier-Stokes problem, a fluid-flow question, after spending millions of dollars and deploying 10,000 agents. That result is widely viewed as credible but has not received formal recognition, and it has been shadowed by allegations that the company drew on unpublished work shared with its tools; OpenAI has strongly denied wrongdoing. Alpöge and NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, who had been working on Navier-Stokes, have also released a separate 79-page paper arguing that smooth solutions can break down in finite time.
Alpöge said in July that he had settled the Jacobian conjecture using Claude. The volume of results has left mathematicians struggling to absorb the material and to work out where their discipline is heading.

The stakes reach beyond mathematics. Anthropic's IPO on Nov. 19, priced at $83 a share and raising $81.3 billion in the largest listing on record, gives public investors their first direct exposure to a frontier lab, and a verified Millennium Prize proof produced with its models would be a powerful marketing datapoint for the company and for the wider thesis that AI can generate original scientific knowledge rather than merely assist it. A rival, fully human proof of a related conjecture would complicate that narrative.
For now, the central question is verification. Lean can confirm that a proof compiles, but not that the statement it proves is the one mathematicians care about, and the Clay institute's two-year review standard means neither claim will be settled quickly. The Zulip discussion suggests the community is treating Alpöge's formalization seriously while waiting for a version it can read.
