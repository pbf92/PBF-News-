On this page
JAMA’s The Rational Clinical Examination is a landmark, ongoing systematic review series published in the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) and hosted on McGraw-Hill’s JAMAevidence platform that critically appraises the diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and likelihood ratios of clinical history elements and bedside physical examination maneuvers against gold-standard reference criteria.
This guide describes academic publishing standards, methodology, and educational platform tools; it does not provide clinical care or individualized medical advice.
What Is JAMA The Rational Clinical Examination?
Initiated in 1992 under clinical epidemiologist David L. Sackett and JAMA executive editor Drummond Rennie, The Rational Clinical Examination was created to address reliance on unexamined clinical dogma in medical education. For generations, physical diagnosis textbooks cataloged hundreds of maneuvers and signs without evaluating their empirical validity or diagnostic reliability. Clinicians routinely performed maneuvers with little knowledge of whether a positive finding meaningfully raised disease probability or whether a negative finding safely excluded it.
The Rational Clinical Examination reframed the medical interview and physical examination through evidence-based clinical diagnosis. Rather than treating bedside assessment as an intuitive craft detached from quantitative science, the series applies formal clinical epidemiology to the diagnostic encounter. Each systematic review addresses a focused clinical dilemma, such as acute abdominal pain, suspected pulmonary embolism, unexplained dyspnea, suspected deep vein thrombosis, or potential meningitis.
Beyond journal articles, the series exists as a reference compendium edited by David L. Simel, MD, MHS, and Drummond Rennie, MD, published by McGraw-Hill Education as The Rational Clinical Examination: Evidence-Based Clinical Diagnosis. Digitally, this compendium forms the core of the JAMAevidence platform, providing literature appraisals, diagnostic calculators, instructional podcasts, and downloadable PowerPoint educational slide sets.
Who Runs It and What It Covers
The Rational Clinical Examination is published under the editorial authority of the American Medical Association (AMA) in Chicago, Illinois. Bibliographically indexed in the National Library of Medicine (NLM) Catalog under NLM ID 7501160 (Print ISSN: 0098-7484; Electronic ISSN: 1538-3598), JAMA maintains indexing across MEDLINE, PubMed, and Index Medicus core clinical journal collections. The journal operates on a hybrid model, publishing 48 issues per year with continuous online publication, maintaining a distinguished JAMA Network Impact Factor and broad international readership.
The taxonomic scope of the series spans nearly every organ system and acute inpatient or outpatient clinical presentation. Major domains covered by the series include:
- Cardiovascular and Hemodynamic Assessment: Precision and diagnostic utility of jugular venous pressure, third heart sounds (S3 gallop), peripheral edema, apical impulse displacement, and auscultatory murmurs in heart failure and valvular disease.
- Pulmonary Evaluation: Accuracy of physical examination signs (crackles, wheezing, egophony, dullness to percussion) and bedside decision rules for diagnosing community-acquired pneumonia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and pulmonary embolism.
- Abdominal and Gastrointestinal Diagnosis: Performance of localized peritoneal signs, right lower quadrant tenderness, Rovsing sign, psoas sign, shifting dullness, fluid wave, and Murphy sign in acute appendicitis, ascites, and acute cholecystitis.
- Neurological and Infectious Presentations: Diagnostic value of nuchal rigidity, Kernig sign, Brudzinski sign, and jolt accentuation in suspected acute meningitis, alongside structured clinical scoring for stroke and peripheral neuropathy.
- Musculoskeletal and Rheumatologic Examination: Validity of provocative maneuvers for rotator cuff tears, carpal tunnel syndrome, meniscus tears, and cruciate ligament stability.
Each review follows a disciplined architectural anatomy. An article opens with an illustrative clinical scenario framing the diagnostic challenge, followed by epidemiological background detailing prevalence and outcomes. The authors execute a rigorous systematic review across bibliographic databases to identify prospective or retrospective diagnostic accuracy studies that compare physical findings against a predefined reference standard (such as computed tomography, ultrasonography, histopathology, or cardiac catheterization). Extracted data are synthesized using bivariate random-effects models to derive pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratios (LR+), and negative likelihood ratios (LR-). The paper concludes by returning to the clinical scenario, demonstrating how likelihood ratios shift pre-test probability to post-test probability.
Methodological Foundations: Likelihood Ratios and Bayesian Diagnostic Reasoning
A core pedagogical contribution of The Rational Clinical Examination is popularizing likelihood ratios as the primary metric for bedside diagnostic reasoning. While traditional medical education emphasizes sensitivity and specificity, these metrics describe how a test performs in known diseased or non-diseased cohorts, which runs backward from the clinician’s diagnostic challenge. A clinician at the bedside does not know whether the patient has the disease; rather, they observe a clinical sign and must determine the probability of disease.
Likelihood ratios solve this inverse probability problem by integrating sensitivity and specificity into a unified multiplier that modifies the baseline odds of disease using Bayes’ theorem. The positive likelihood ratio reflects how much the odds of disease increase when a physical sign is present:
LR+ = Sensitivity / (1 – Specificity)
Conversely, the negative likelihood ratio reflects how much the odds of disease decrease when a physical sign is absent:
LR- = (1 – Sensitivity) / Specificity
In clinical practice, the diagnostic magnitude of a likelihood ratio can be interpreted using standard benchmark categories:
- LR+ greater than 10 (or LR- less than 0.1): Generates large, often conclusive shifts from pre-test to post-test probability, frequently confirming or ruling out a diagnosis.
- LR+ between 5 and 10 (or LR- between 0.1 and 0.2): Generates moderate, clinically meaningful shifts in disease probability that significantly influence management decisions.
- LR+ between 2 and 5 (or LR- between 0.2 and 0.5): Generates small shifts in probability that rarely alter a diagnosis in isolation but contribute to cumulative risk assessment.
- LR+ between 1 and 2 (or LR- between 0.5 and 1.0): Generates minimal shifts in probability, providing negligible diagnostic value.
- LR equal to 1.0: Offers zero diagnostic information, leaving pre-test probability unchanged.
To apply likelihood ratios without manual odds-to-probability calculations, clinicians utilize Fagan nomograms or digital calculators on JAMAevidence. By placing a straightedge connecting the patient’s estimated pre-test probability through the test’s likelihood ratio, the clinician reads the corresponding post-test probability on the opposite axis.
How to Search and Use JAMA The Rational Clinical Examination
Clinicians, researchers, and educators can navigate The Rational Clinical Examination through either the JAMA Network digital repository or the dedicated JAMAevidence platform. The following sequential workflow illustrates how to locate, evaluate, and apply evidence from the collection:
- Access the Platform Portal: Navigate to the JAMAevidence portal at jamaevidence.mhmedical.com or access the JAMA Network collection page at jamanetwork.com. Institutional users sign in through university or hospital library proxies via Shibboleth or OpenAthens; individual subscribers log in using McGraw Hill Access Profiles or AMA credentials.
- Locate Content via Search or Browse: Utilize the centralized search bar at the top of the interface by entering targeted clinical queries (for example, “acute appendicitis” or “hypovolemia”). Alternatively, click the “Books” dropdown in the primary navigation menu and select The Rational Clinical Examination: Evidence-Based Clinical Diagnosis to browse chapters organized by specialty.
- Review the Clinical Scenario and Research Question: Open the selected chapter or review article. Begin by examining the introductory “Clinical Scenario” section, which outlines the clinical problem, patient demographics, and presenting symptoms. Verify whether the study population matches your clinical setting.
- Analyze Summary Tables and Diagnostic Operating Characteristics: Navigate to the “Accuracy of Findings” and summary evidence tables within the chapter. Review reported pooled sensitivities, specificities, and summary likelihood ratios, alongside 95% confidence intervals reflecting statistical precision.
- Deploy Interactive Diagnostic Calculators: Access the “Tools” or “Calculators” tab on the JAMAevidence navigation ribbon. Select the diagnostic test calculator, input your estimated baseline pre-test probability based on local prevalence or clinical suspicion, and enter the positive or negative likelihood ratio to compute the exact post-test probability.
- Export Educational Decks and Teaching Resources: When preparing residency conferences or academic teaching, click the “Download Slide (.ppt)” button at the top of the article. These slide sets include pre-formatted figures, diagnostic flowcharts, and detailed instructor notes addressing common learner misconceptions.
Worked Clinical Diagnostic Walkthrough: Evaluating Acute Dyspnea in the Emergency Setting
To understand how Rational Clinical Examination data functions in real-world clinical decision-making, consider a practical clinical scenario drawn from the systematic review by Wang and colleagues (2005) evaluating adult patients presenting to the emergency department with acute dyspnea.
A 68-year-old patient with a history of hypertension presents to the emergency department with acute shortness of breath worsening over 48 hours. In an unselected emergency department cohort presenting with acute dyspnea, the baseline prevalence (pre-test probability) of acute decompensated heart failure is approximately 50%.
Converting this pre-test probability to pre-test odds yields:
Pre-test Odds = 0.50 / (1 – 0.50) = 1.0
During physical examination, the clinician auscultates a distinct third heart sound (S3 gallop). According to Wang et al., an S3 gallop has a pooled sensitivity of 24% (it is absent in most patients with heart failure), but an extraordinary specificity of 99%, yielding a positive likelihood ratio of 11.0 (95% CI, 4.9 to 25.0).
Multiplying the pre-test odds by the positive likelihood ratio calculates the post-test odds:
Post-test Odds = 1.0 x 11.0 = 11.0
Converting post-test odds back to post-test probability demonstrates the diagnostic shift:
Post-test Probability = 11.0 / (11.0 + 1) = 91.7%
The identification of an S3 gallop elevates the probability of acute heart failure from an uncertain 50% to over 91%, providing sufficient diagnostic certainty to initiate intravenous diuretic therapy and vasodilators promptly.
Conversely, consider a second scenario where a patient presents with similar dyspnea, but examination reveals no S3 gallop and no peripheral edema. Because an S3 gallop has a low sensitivity (24%), its absence carries a negative likelihood ratio of 0.77, which only marginally reduces probability (from 50% down to 43%). The absence of an S3 gallop does not exclude heart failure. However, when laboratory testing reveals a serum B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) below 100 pg/mL, the negative likelihood ratio is 0.11.
