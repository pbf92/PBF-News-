TITLE: Wesley Huff Apologizes for Plagiarism
OUTLET: Christianity Today

Wesley “Wes” Huff, a Christian influencer whose videos defending the historical accuracy of the Bible have been viewed nearly 30 million times, apologized Thursday for plagiarizing his academic work.
“I have plagiarized the work of others,” he said in a post on X. “This was wrong, and it was sinful. Over the years, I allowed myself to develop justifications for my actions, minimizing their seriousness and, at times, even pushing them so far from my mind that I failed to reckon with what I had done.”
Huff had been a relatively little-known YouTuber before appearing on the Joe Rogan podcast in January 2025, an episode that has been viewed nearly 10 million times on YouTube. That appearance helped build his audience tenfold.
The plagiarism took place during Huff’s studies at Tyndale Seminary, where he received a master’s degree, and at the University of Toronto, where he was a doctoral student.
In his statement, Huff said he would step aside from Apologetics Canada, a ministry where he was a vice president and speaker, as well as from church ministry.
“My heart has always been to tell people about Jesus, to share the gospel, and to help others discover the beauty and richness of the Bible and its history,” he said. “But having a good desire does not excuse the ways I have fallen short in pursuing it. I have clearly failed to uphold the standards of integrity that I have encouraged others to value.”
In a statement, Apologetics Canada said the ministry had been aware of allegations of plagiarism against Huff for weeks, citing a video posted November 8 by scholar and social media influencer Dan McClellan. That video led to a host of social media posts with additional allegations and online debates over plagiarism among pastors.
McClellan said in an email that he first had concerns about Huff’s work after reading one of his academic papers online.
“As I began to read, I noticed the language and the reasoning bounced back and forth between sophisticated and sloppy, and between insightful and rather banal,” he said. “This is frequently what happens when someone is plagiarizing the structure of their paper and then having to fill in the rest with their own less expert and well-reasoned thoughts.”
McClellan said he did some digging and found much of Huff’s paper included the work of a different scholar. He eventually found more instances of plagiarism in other papers. Since Huff has gained attention for defending the Bible—and his credibility was built on his academic work—McClellan said it was important to raise questions about alleged plagiarism.
Huff initially responded to the allegations of plagiarism in a video, saying the work in question was not plagiarism but merely mediocre writing. That video was later deleted from his YouTube channel, which has more than a million subscribers.
Leaders at Apologetics Canada said that the ministry has been providing counseling and support for Huff and his family, as Huff’s young daughter was hospitalized around the same time that the allegations of plagiarism became public.
The ministry also apologized for the “hurt, confusion and frustration” caused by the allegations against Huff.
“We want to be clear that the allegations are serious and warrant a careful and in-depth review that we are committed to,” leaders said in a statement on social media. “Over the coming months, the Board of Directors, alongside expert independent third parties, will continue to conduct a full review of concerns.”
A video course taught by Huff on the historical reliability of the Bible has been removed from Peterson Academy, an online site started by Canadian author and academic Jordan Peterson.
Among Huff’s most popular videos online are a debate with author and podcaster Billy Carson that has been viewed more than 4.7 million times, his critique of the King James Bible, a discussion of angels in the Bible and a discussion of why he is a Protestant. All have been viewed more than a million times.
In his statement, Huff said he would submit to the care of his church and to the leadership of Apologetics Canada.
“To those individuals, and to everyone who has trusted me and my work, I am deeply sorry. I ask for your forgiveness, recognizing that forgiveness cannot be demanded and that rebuilding trust, if it is possible, will take time,” Huff said in his statement.
McClellan said that plagiarism calls into question Huff’s academic integrity—and suggested that the seminary where he studied might need to rescind his master’s degree.
“Beyond those academic consequences,” he said, “I hope Wes is willing and able to accept full accountability for his misconduct and hopefully learn and recover from this issue so that he can be professionally successful in some way that allows him to continue to provide for his young family.”
