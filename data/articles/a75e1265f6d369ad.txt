TITLE: Microsoft rebuilds its bid model, citing 10% more value per click
OUTLET: PPC Land

On November 18, 2026, Microsoft Advertising began rolling out what it calls the biggest upgrade to its bidding models in a decade, rebuilding the model that predicts whether a click will turn into a sale and what that sale is worth. Advertisers using Maximize Conversion Value or Target ROAS saw on average 10% more conversion value per click at a similar return on ad spend, according to Microsoft's internal data for August 2026.
The announcement came in a post titled "Building the next generation of automated bidding at Microsoft Advertising," published on the Microsoft Advertising blog on November 18, 2026, under the byline of Carlo Buchmann, Principal Product Manager. It describes the rollout as "the first stage of our updates" and says Microsoft will "continue to roll out improvements automatically to more accounts over time," with "more to come in 2027." Alongside the model change, the post sets out five practices Microsoft associates with the new bidding, including a threshold of five account-level conversions for starting new campaigns on conversion-based strategies, a recommendation against keeping Maximum CPC caps, and a line that sets Microsoft apart from unnamed "other platforms" on what happens to bid targets when budgets run short.
In Short
Microsoft has rebuilt the system that predicts what each ad click is worth on its search and audience inventory, and says bids now hit cost and return targets more consistently, react nearly twice as fast when targets change and spend budgets more evenly through the day. This matters to anyone paying for clicks on Bing and Microsoft's partner sites through automated bidding, because Microsoft attaches average gains of 10%, 8% and 18% to the new models and to switching strategies, all drawn from its own unpublished data. The practical change is in the guidance: Microsoft says accounts with at least five conversions should start new campaigns on conversion-based bidding rather than Enhanced CPC, and that bid caps work against the targets they sit beside.
What Microsoft rebuilt
The post locates the change in a single component. "Every bid depends on a prediction of whether a click will become a sale, and how much that sale is worth," it states. "We rebuilt that prediction model with new user signals and a better way of valuing each conversion." The new signals are not named, and the post does not explain what the "better way of valuing" each conversion consists of.
Microsoft lists three effects of the upgrade. Under "More consistent performance," it says bids "meet advertisers' cost per acquisition (CPA) and return on ad spend (ROAS) targets more consistently - without giving up conversion volume or value." Under "Faster optimization," it says "Bids adjust nearly twice as quickly when advertisers update a CPA or ROAS target." And under "More predictable delivery," it says "Budgets pace more evenly throughout the day and campaigns ramp to full budget faster."
Two of those three claims concern time rather than outcomes. A bid system that is slow to respond after a target is changed keeps bidding to the old instruction in the meantime; one that responds in half the time shortens that period. The post gives no baseline, though. "Nearly twice as quickly" is not expressed in hours or days, and "ramp to full budget faster" carries no figure at all.
Microsoft frames the upgrade as a response to customers. "At Microsoft Advertising, we're continuously listening to customer feedback to understand the ways we can make our bidding deliver better results for advertisers, and now we're rolling out the biggest upgrade to bidding models in a decade," the post reads.
The three figures, and what each one measures
The post cites three averages, each footnoted to Microsoft internal data. They do not all measure the same thing.
Only the first figure is presented as the effect of the new models. "The impact of these upgraded bidding models is already beginning to show up in performance," the post says before citing it. The 8% and 18% figures describe what happened when advertisers moved from one strategy to another. The 18% figure covers June and July 2026, earlier than the August period behind the 10% figure, and the post does not say whether the upgraded models were in place for any of those campaigns.
The 8% figure carries the post's argument for value-based bidding, the approach in which an automated system bids according to the predicted value of a conversion rather than counting every conversion as equal. "That's why advertisers are seeing better results when they use value-based bidding," the post says, linking the rebuilt valuation to the result. "The greatest improvements come when advertisers optimize using actual transaction values, such as purchase revenue."
None of the three figures comes with a number of advertisers, a number of campaigns, a confidence interval or a definition of "similar." That distinguishes them from the figure Microsoft used for AI Max in the summer. When Microsoft said AI Max testers gained 8% more conversions, PPC Land traced the number to a blog footnote describing 44 advertiser-run A/B tests between June and August 2026, with bid strategy and budget held constant; the 8% was the lower bound. The bidding post offers nothing comparable. The strategy-switch figures are also averages across advertisers who chose to switch, and the post does not say whether they were compared against campaigns that did not.
The naming in the post reflects a change Microsoft made last year. In July 2025, Microsoft folded Target CPA and Target ROAS into Maximize Conversions and Maximize Conversion Value as optional targets for new campaigns from August 4, 2025, so "Target ROAS" in the November 18 post refers in practice to Maximize Conversion Value with a ROAS target set.
Five practices, as Microsoft describes them
According to Microsoft, the post sets out five practices for advertisers "to take full advantage of these new bidding models." They are recorded here as the post states them.
- Conversion tracking. "Conversion tracking is a prerequisite for automated bidding," the post says, because the bid strategies "optimize toward conversions, so without the right conversion signal there's nothing for the system to optimize." It names three methods: Universal Event Tracking (UET), Conversions API and offline import. Microsoft recommends UET "since it provides richer, more granular signals that help the bidder optimize more effectively," and describes the Conversions API as extending coverage "across more customer touchpoints alongside UET, connecting signals from your apps, CRM, stores, and other sources directly to Microsoft Advertising in real time."
- Bid strategy choice. The post ties the starting strategy to account history. "For new campaigns, if your account has at least five conversions, then we recommend starting on a conversion-based bid strategy right from the beginning, as opposed to starting with Enhanced CPC," it says. The reason given is that "conversion history at the account level provides bidding signals to all the campaigns within your account." Accounts that are new and have fewer than five conversions "can start with Enhanced CPC to gather conversion history and first allow the system to learn before moving to Maximize Conversions with a target CPA or Maximize Conversion Value with a target ROAS."
- Target changes. "Targets can be changed at any time, and the system learns continuously," the post says. It names three changes that "tend to drive volatility": creating a new campaign, switching bid strategy, and adding or removing conversion goals. "Large target CPA increases or ROAS decreases open up new inventory," producing "some short- term volatility while the system calibrates." To limit that, the post describes changes "in 15% increments" with a review "after one conversion cycle," which it defines as "the time period it typically takes for your business to move a click to conversion."
- Outcome-based controls. "Keeping a Maximum CPC set on your campaigns is not recommended," the post states. "These settings override advertisers' own CPA/ROAS targets, which provide conflicting instructions to the system and cause advertisers to miss their overall desired outcomes, even when the cap is above average CPC." The controls Microsoft names instead are "budgets, bid targets, and conversion value rules."
- Evaluation. Performance should be judged "after accumulating sufficient conversions (ideally 30 conversions in 30 days)" and "1-2 conversion cycles after major changes are made," according to the post, because "Smaller samples create variance that can obscure true performance." It places evaluation "at the campaign or portfolio level (not at the ad group or keyword level)" and on "conversions and CPA/ROAS, not keyword-level CPCs."

Targets as levers, not rules
One paragraph in the post addresses a dispute that has occupied search marketers since the summer, without naming the company at its centre. "Unlike other platforms, Microsoft Advertising's bidding continues to allow campaigns to overachieve on target CPA or target ROAS efficiency, regardless of budget limited status," it reads. "On our platform, targets are not rigid values that must be met at the expense of potentially better available ROI, but rather as directional levers to help the system balance volume and efficiency."
The contrast is with Google. On June 15, 2026, Google Ads announced a bidding overhaul for August under which budget-limited campaigns on target-based strategies would perform more consistently toward their bid target, with the example of a $10 Target CPA delivering at $5 moving toward $10. In an August 5 question-and-answer post, Google said targets become the efficiency lever in budget-capped campaigns. On August 17, Google began pushing overperforming CPAs up across five campaign types: Search, Shopping, Performance Max, Demand Gen and Travel. Weeks later, an advertiser said the change cut Shopping sales on a campaign set at a 300% target ROAS that had been returning 3,000% to 4,000%.
Microsoft's position runs the other way. Under its description, a campaign that is limited by budget and could beat its CPA or ROAS target is allowed to do so, and the target acts as a direction rather than a point the system must reach. For advertisers who run the same campaigns on both platforms, that means the same target number can now produce different behaviour on each, particularly in accounts where budgets, not targets, set the limit on spend.
The post does not say whether this has always been Microsoft's approach or whether it was reviewed as part of the upgrade. "Continues to allow" suggests the former.
From removing bid caps to discouraging them
The guidance on Maximum CPC extends a change Microsoft began earlier this year. In August, Microsoft Advertising said it would drop Max CPC from new campaigns on October 1, removing the setting from new campaigns on standalone automated strategies while portfolio strategies, Enhanced CPC and Target impression share kept it. At the time, neither Microsoft's notice nor its liaison's post gave a reason.
The November 18 post supplies one. A Maximum CPC sets the most an advertiser will pay for a single click; Microsoft's argument is that this per-click ceiling overrides the CPA or ROAS target the advertiser has also set, so the system receives two instructions that can conflict.
