TITLE: Global Umbrella Review Analyzes Severe Complications and Mortality From Seasonal Influenza
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive global umbrella review has synthesized worldwide evidence on seasonal influenza, demonstrating that the viral infection imposes a substantial and uneven burden across different age groups and geographic regions. The international investigation evaluated existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses to provide a consolidated assessment of severe outcomes associated with seasonal influenza infections, specifically examining complication rates, hospital admissions, and mortality across diverse populations around the world.
Seasonal influenza continues to exert a major global health impact, falling especially hard on young children, older adults, and individuals living with underlying medical conditions. Although public health authorities conduct extensive surveillance and researchers have published numerous systematic reviews over the years, the true worldwide impact has remained challenging to quantify reliably. This persistent difficulty stems largely from inconsistent epidemiological methods and variable data quality across published evaluations, prompting the investigators to undertake a rigorous synthesis of the broader evidence base.
To evaluate the comprehensive literature, researchers gathered systematic reviews and meta-analyses from PubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The search was updated through August 31, 2026, and applied no restrictions regarding publication date or language. The evaluation was guided by Population, Exposure, and Outcome criteria focused on the general population, specifically tracking outcomes related to influenza-associated hospitalizations, fatal cases, and clinical complications.
The research protocol followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement and was registered on the PROSPERO database under identifier CRD42024589457. Investigators evaluated the methodological quality of the included reviews using the AMSTAR 2 critical appraisal tool. Due to substantial heterogeneity across reported clinical endpoints, the authors synthesized the extracted findings narratively rather than pooling data through direct quantitative statistical meta-analysis.
The synthesis ultimately included fifty qualifying reviews, consisting of twenty-one systematic reviews and twenty-nine meta-analyses. Within the assembled citation matrix, the authors identified 1,533 review-publication occurrences, which corresponded to 1,213 unique primary research publications. This extensive literature base allowed the authors to map patterns of influenza severity across diverse demographic segments and global regions.
Hospitalization patterns demonstrated that children younger than five years of age and adults aged sixty-five years and older experience the highest rates of influenza-associated hospital admission. However, in-hospital mortality was observed to be markedly higher among adult patients than among pediatric cohorts. Overall mortality was consistently elevated among older individuals and patients with comorbid conditions, with severe outcomes exhibiting pronounced geographic variation and greater impact in regions characterized by limited healthcare resources and low vaccination coverage.
The review also identified serious extrapulmonary consequences, noting that eligible cardiovascular evidence revealed a marked short-term surge in the risk of acute myocardial infarction and stroke during the first month following influenza infection. The authors concluded that these findings reinforce the necessity of targeted vaccination programs for high-risk demographics, strengthened epidemiological surveillance, improved epidemic intelligence, and advanced mathematical modeling to refine future disease burden estimates.
