TITLE: OpenAI's agents finish 46% of long tasks as Ireland logs 9 halted AI launches
OUTLET: PPC Land

Sunday, November 22, 2026 was a day for counting what machines can do and who gets to stop them. OpenAI said its internal coding agents now complete nearly half of the research tasks that would take a person two to four hours, while admitting they still have poor taste. Ireland's Data Protection Commission published five years of AI supervision figures, showing nine product launches delayed or cancelled after its intervention. Turkey's competition authority ordered Google to let news publishers leave AI Overviews without losing their place in search, Germany's top court sent a fake quote case against Meta to Luxembourg, and the Bundeskartellamt warned PayPal over its ban on surcharges. The European Parliament prepared to lift a GDPR record-keeping exemption to firms with up to 1,000 staff. In search, Lily Ray argued that real people outlast every shortcut, a study found newly matched phrase-match queries costing 28% more per lead, and Google closed Maps to 41 AI crawlers in its own robots.txt. Amazon put an AI agent in front of Brand Store page building and restated data when brands add products, Microsoft moved on alcohol, new customers and holiday budgets, and Google said AI Max sits outside its accredited click counts. Television kept fragmenting into pause screens, addressable households and premium streaming launches.

The jagged frontier
OpenAI has started measuring how much of its own research its machines can carry. PPC Land reported that OpenAI's research agents now finish 46% of two-to-four-hour tasks alone. The figure comes from "Research acceleration: The jagged frontier," published on November 18, the company's second report on how AI coding agents are used inside its research teams. In March, the agents completed about 23% of tasks that would take a person two to four hours without any human intervention. For tasks of four to eight hours, success rose from 13% to 35%.
The gains are uneven, which explains the title. Agents succeed on 79% of tasks that involve monitoring training runs, but on only 29% of the work of building evaluations, reinforcement learning setups and agent environments. "Lower-performing categories and tasks could become bottlenecks to high overall uplift," the report says. OpenAI's researchers said people originated nearly all of the key ideas behind recent releases, with one partial exception involving Codex on GPT-6.2 Astra. The report is blunt about the limits: "Agents have poor taste in research ideas, code quality, data quality, and writing."
The spending is not small. Median spend on agents per researcher, at API list prices, reached about $2,400 a day in October, up 62% from $1,500 in September, and the 90th percentile hit about $23,000. Agent runtime rose from half a workday to 5.7 workdays per researcher per day, and by mid-October more than two-thirds of researchers averaged peaks of at least four agents running at once. In September, agentic usage cost about twice the non-equity labour it supported. Internal deployment takes 2% of research compute. The figures are self-reported and unaudited, and a harness change from August 21 depressed recorded token spend until it was reverted from September 10. OpenAI says the reporting does not signal an intent to pursue full recursive self-improvement. For marketers, the more useful reading is the shape of the curve: agents are already good at watching and checking, and still weak at deciding what is worth doing.
OpenAI's hardware ambitions also surfaced. PPC Land reported that an OpenAI patent describes earbuds that read silently mouthed words. Application US 2026/0369273 A1, "In-Ear Silent-Speech Detection System," was published on November 19, naming OpenAI OpCo, LLC as applicant and Douglas Stuart Brodie and Daniel Boothe as inventors. It describes an earbud-style device that interprets words the wearer mouths without voicing them, by sensing how the ear canal moves or changes shape as the jaw moves. Accelerometers and gyroscopes sampled at roughly 100 to 1,000 Hz, strain gauges and other biometric sensors feed a four-stage pipeline that turns signals into phonemes, from a 44-entry English set, and then into words, commands or speech. The output could drive language models and agents that compose messages, update schedules and query databases, and the application also covers silent two-way communication. The nonprovisional application was filed on May 14, 2026, claims priority to a provisional from May 14, 2025, and has 20 claims; it has not been granted. It gives no accuracy figures, product name or data-handling details. The context is OpenAI's roughly $6.5bn acquisition of io in May 2025. A device that listens to a jaw rather than a voice would open a new kind of private interface, and a new kind of data that privacy regulators have not yet had to consider.
Who gets to say stop
Ireland's privacy regulator opened its books on AI. PPC Land reported that Ireland's DPC delayed or cancelled 9 AI launches in five years of reviews. The Data Protection Commission's 75-page report, dated September 2026, covers its AI supervision from 2021 to 2025: about 180 AI products and services from companies including Meta, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, TikTok and X, most reviewed before launch by its Technology Multinational Supervision Unit. Engagements rose tenfold over the period. Generative AI accounted for 143 of them, about 79%, climbing from 25 in 2023 to 36 in 2024 and 82 in 2025.
Nine launches were delayed or cancelled because of the regulator's intervention: one in 2022, one in 2023, four in 2024 and three in 2025. Transparency problems appeared in 72% of its recommendations, and 59% of the AI products launched in the EU in 2024 and 2025 received recommendations, though 95% of engagements ended in a launch without delay. The process is voluntary and non-binding. "The DPC will never approve or state that a process, product, or service is compliant with the GDPR," the report says. It wants at least eight weeks' notice of a launch. Three formal inquiries remain open: one into Google's PaLM 2, opened on September 12, 2024, and two into X Internet Unlimited Company over Grok, opened on April 11, 2025 and February 17, 2026. The report recalls that Meta paused its 2024 plan to train AI on European users' data and resumed in 2025 after revised assessments, and that a chatbot opt-out bug in January 2025 potentially affected about 7.7 million mobile users in the European Economic Area. Training AI on private messages, it says, faces a threshold "very difficult to meet." On consent and notice, it sets a high bar: "The DPC considers it insufficient to demonstrate that individuals are merely aware of the possibility." For advertisers building on these platforms' AI features, the report is a reminder that much of what reaches Europe has already been shaped by a regulator's comments.
Turkey went further than advice. PPC Land reported that Turkey forces Google to let news sites exit AI Overviews but stay in search. The Turkish Competition Authority announced on November 2 that its Competition Board had adopted interim measures on October 8 and opened a full investigation into abuse of dominance under Article 6 of Act no 4054, following a preliminary inquiry opened on August 27. The measures require Google to avoid discrimination among news publishers, to reflect the economic value of their content, and to let publishers opt out of AI use "independently of their visibility and indexing on other services provided by Google." The Board cited "the existing indications for exclusionary and exploitative behavior concerning news publishers." No deadline, fine or technical method was announced, and Alphabet and four subsidiaries are treated as a single economic unit. A November 19 analysis on the Kluwer Competition Law Blog by Ahmed Saim Pehlivanli and Doga Kucukay said the reasoned decision, published only in Turkish, gives Google three months to comply, with daily fines after that. Google runs a paid News AI pilot with more than 400 publications as of October 15. Turkey has now written into a competition order what publishers in several other markets are still asking for: separate controls for search and for AI.
Germany's highest civil court has sent a question about platform liability to Luxembourg. PPC Land reported that the German top court halts Künast's fake quote case against Meta for EU ruling. The Federal Court of Justice suspended case VI ZR 64/24 on October 27 and referred six questions to the Court of Justice of the EU. The dispute concerns a Facebook meme attributing a sentence about learning Turkish to a politician identified in German media as Renate Künast. The Frankfurt Regional Court granted her claim on April 8, 2022; the Higher Regional Court dismissed damages on January 25, 2024 but upheld the injunction. The questions ask whether a network that stores and algorithmically recommends posts remains a neutral host under the E-Commerce Directive and the Digital Services Act, whether it must manually assess reworded copies, whether it is fully a controller under the GDPR, and whether hosting privileges apply to GDPR damages claims. Künast reported nine posts, which Meta removed; she seeks an injunction covering equivalent memes and at least EUR10,000 in damages. Meta argues it need only remove specifically identified posts and warns of overblocking. The answer will matter well beyond one meme, because recommendation is now the core of every feed that carries advertising.
Payments drew a warning of their own. PPC Land reported that PayPal faces possible clawback in Germany over its no-surcharge rule. On November 11, the Bundeskartellamt sent PayPal a preliminary assessment that the "Rules about surcharging" clause in its merchant terms, which bars shops from charging extra for paying with PayPal and has applied since 2018, may breach competition law. Because PayPal's merchant fees are above average, shops spread the cost across all prices, so shoppers using other methods help pay for it, and cheaper rivals such as cards, Klarna and Wero struggle to compete on price. "Other, cheaper providers would find it more difficult to gain an edge over PayPal," said Andreas Mundt, the authority's president. The authority is considering disgorgement of economic benefits, without naming an amount or period. The proceeding opened on January 23, 2023, nearly four years ago. "PayPal now has the opportunity to comment on the Bundeskartellamt's preliminary assessment of the matter," the authority said. For online retailers, a ruling against the clause would make checkout pricing a lever again.
Brussels is also loosening a rule. PPC Land reported that firms under 1,000 staff win GDPR records exemption in EU deal. The European Parliament prepared Amendment 44 to a consolidated Omnibus IV text for a plenary vote indicatively scheduled for November 23 in Strasbourg. If adopted and approved by the Council, the exemption from keeping records of processing under Article 30(5) of the GDPR would rise from companies with fewer than 250 employees to fewer than 1,000. It would not cover processing likely to result in high risk or core large-scale monitoring, assessed activity by activity. The Commission's proposal of May 21, 2025 had set the line at 750; a provisional agreement came on June 9, 2026. The text defines "small mid-cap enterprises" as having fewer than 1,000 staff and turnover up to EUR200m or a balance sheet up to EUR172m, with lighter rules in five other regulations, though for Article 30 only headcount counts.
