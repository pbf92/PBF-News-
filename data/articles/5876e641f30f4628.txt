TITLE: Spinal Cord Stimulation Offers Salvage Option for Refractory Postherpetic Neuralgia
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive narrative review published in Frontiers in Neurology reveals that spinal cord stimulation may provide a meaningful salvage therapy for patients suffering from postherpetic neuralgia whose debilitating nerve pain resists conventional medical management. Originating as a persistent neuropathic complication following acute herpes zoster infection, postherpetic neuralgia is characterized by continuous pain that lingers after the characteristic dermatomal rash has fully resolved, with diagnostic criteria across the clinical literature specifying pain durations of at least one month or extending past three months. The review synthesizes findings from forty-eight clinical investigations, comparative trials, observational studies, and case reports identified through structured literature database searches spanning PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, CNKI, and Wanfang through May 2026. The authors demonstrate that while targeted epidural electrical stimulation can alleviate intractable pain, enhance restorative sleep, improve affective functioning, and lessen reliance on systemic analgesics, the clinical evidence base continues to be constrained by substantial methodological heterogeneity, modest sample sizes, and a pronounced scarcity of large-scale randomized controlled trials focused strictly on established chronic disease.
The underlying disease burden of postherpetic neuralgia is extensive and rooted in persistent cellular neuroinflammation initiated by the latent reactivation of the varicella-zoster virus residing within primary dorsal root ganglia or the trigeminal ganglion. Epidemiological analyses summarized in the review indicate that approximately 7.7 percent of individuals in China contract herpes zoster during their lifetimes, with between 9 and 43 percent of these patients progressing to postherpetic neuralgia as incidence rates climb sharply alongside advanced chronological age. When declining cell-mediated immunity permits dormant viral particles to replicate and migrate along peripheral sensory pathways toward cutaneous surfaces, intense local inflammatory processes erupt within ganglia and spinal rootlets. This cascades into widespread axonal destruction, patchy nerve demyelination, ectopic peripheral discharges, abnormal sodium channel redistribution, and elevated concentrations of proinflammatory mediators including tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-1 beta. Patients endure a distressing constellation of symptoms that frequently blend burning, stinging, tearing, cutting, and sudden electric shock-like sensations, culminating in severe sleep disruption, chronic anxiety, psychological depression, and significant loss of physical function.
As ongoing peripheral nerve injury bombards the central nervous system with aberrant nociceptive signaling, pathological central sensitization establishes itself within the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. The narrative synthesis details how persistent afferent input drives the hyperactivation and amplification of glutamate-dependent N-methyl-D-aspartate and alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor pathways, alongside the functional loss or structural exhaustion of local gamma-aminobutyric acid-releasing inhibitory interneurons. Standard conservative options, including calcium channel modulators, tricyclic antidepressants, and opioid analgesics, often yield incomplete pain relief or cause adverse effects such as dizziness, drowsiness, anticholinergic effects, or risks of tolerance and dependence.
Spinal cord stimulation counters these refractory pain circuits through minimally invasive epidural placement of programmed electrode arrays that deliver targeted electrical currents across dorsal column pathways. Grounded conceptually in the seminal Gate Control Theory introduced by Melzack and Wall in 1967, traditional tonic stimulation activates large-diameter myelinated afferent nerve fibers to stimulate spinal inhibitory interneurons, thereby directly suppressing the ascending transmission of nociceptive signals carried along smaller A-delta and unmyelinated C fibers. Supraspinal pain centers, including the thalamus and somatosensory cerebral cortex, also demonstrate altered processing patterns during sustained stimulation.
Within current clinical practice, conventional low-frequency spinal cord stimulation operates as the historical standard for refractory postherpetic neuralgia. Applying electrical impulses across frequencies spanning 30 to 100 Hertz, pulse durations from 100 to 500 microseconds, and amplitudes between 0.5 and 5.0 Volts, conventional systems intentionally generate a comfortable paresthesia sensation engineered to overlap and mask the precise painful dermatomes. Observational studies, case series, and long-term cohort follow-ups reviewed by the authors highlight that properly selected patients exhibiting well-defined, localized pain distributions frequently achieve marked decreases in visual analog scale pain scores and reduce their dependence on heavy analgesic regimens. Nevertheless, conventional low-frequency therapy requires patients to comfortably tolerate persistent tingling sensations, which can cause patient fatigue or perceptual discomfort.
To overcome the sensory limitations imposed by paresthesia-dependent paradigms, specialized waveform modalities have entered the neuromodulation landscape. High-frequency spinal cord stimulation applies high-rate pulses at 10 kilohertz to induce profound pain suppression without generating any detectable cutaneous tingling or numbness. By directly dampening the firing rates of wide-dynamic-range neurons located in the dorsal horn and reshaping thalamocortical networks, high-frequency stimulation provides an appealing therapeutic alternative for patients who cannot tolerate conventional paresthesia or who fail to achieve coverage from standard low-frequency stimulation. However, the authors caution that direct clinical evidence supporting 10-kilohertz stimulation specifically in postherpetic neuralgia remains sparse, warning that favorable outcomes documented in painful diabetic neuropathy, spinal cord injury, or experimental animal preparations must not be uncritically assumed to apply to postherpetic pain. Furthermore, high-frequency devices exhibit substantially higher electrical power consumption, requiring more frequent battery recharging or shorter operational generator lifespans, alongside higher overall healthcare equipment costs that require rigorous prospective health-economic evaluation.
The review notes that while chronic neuropathic pain studies suggest potential effects of burst spinal cord stimulation on both pain intensity and pain-related emotional processing, postherpetic neuralgia evidence remains preliminary. Clinical data cited in the synthesis include a single case study of an eighty-year-old male presenting with severe left T2 dermatomal postherpetic neuralgia and a baseline visual analog scale score of 8 out of 10. Following targeted epidural lead implantation extending across the T1 to T4 vertebral levels and six months of continuous burst therapy, the patient’s pain rating dropped to 3 out of 10. While demonstrating promising clinical utility, such isolated individual reports represent low-level evidence that cannot justify broad standardized adoption without larger comparative trials.
The review also evaluates dorsal root ganglion stimulation as an anatomically distinct neuromodulation approach that should not be categorized as a subtype of spinal cord stimulation. By typically applying frequencies of 10 to 70 Hertz and pulse widths of 90 to 1,000 microseconds, this modality modifies the excitability of principal sensory neurons in the dorsal root ganglion and blocks the central transmission of pain impulses. Though clinically established for focal neuropathic disorders like complex regional pain syndrome and causalgia, its utility in postherpetic neuralgia is supported only by limited case reports and small preliminary series describing isolated or combined dorsal root ganglion and spinal stimulation protocols.
Evaluating candidate eligibility requires stringent adherence to clinical indications and recognized safety contraindications. Spinal cord stimulation is indicated exclusively for patients with established, pharmacologically refractory postherpetic neuralgia experiencing moderate to severe pain with high visual analog scale ratings confined to discrete thoracic, lumbar, or select cervical dermatomes. The authors emphasize that studies investigating early temporary spinal cord stimulation deployed during the acute or subacute phases of herpes zoster address active symptom control and secondary chronic pain prevention rather than the remediation of established chronic neuralgia, meaning their favorable results cannot be conflated with chronic therapeutic efficacy. Conversely, neurostimulation is strongly contraindicated in patients with active localized skin lesions or systemic infections, uncorrectable coagulopathies, an inability to safely pause mandatory antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications, uncontrolled severe psychiatric disorders, proven allergies to device biomaterials, or explicit patient refusal. Furthermore, individuals presenting with diffuse, poorly localized pain spanning multiple broad nerve territories typically exhibit disappointing clinical responses, making neurostimulation unsuited for generalized presentations.
When monotherapy provides incomplete pain reduction, multidisciplinary combined treatment strategies can bridge remaining analgesic gaps. Short-term opioid use may manage severe breakthrough episodes, though clinicians must carefully monitor risks of tolerance and dependency. Interventional synergies also demonstrate clinical utility: pulsed radiofrequency applied to peripheral nerves suppresses localized ectopic firing and functions alongside central dorsal column stimulation to create a peripheral-central dual-regulation pathway. Peripheral nerve blocks can rapidly interrupt acute pain exacerbations while spinal stimulation stabilizes long-term baselines. Nonetheless, the authors caution that multimodal benefits must not be conflated with the isolated efficacy of spinal stimulation itself.
Safe clinical deployment requires vigilant prevention and swift management of both intraoperative and postoperative technical complications. Intraoperative hazards center around lead misplacement, accidental dural puncture, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, and epidural hemorrhage. If unintentional dural penetration or epidural bleeding occurs, operators must pause or revise lead trajectories, achieve definitive hemostasis, and initiate close neurological observation. During the initial one to two weeks following surgery, mechanical lead migration and localized incision site pain represent the most frequent postoperative complications.
Future clinical investigations must adopt standardized temporal definitions of neuralgia, report technical programming parameters and adverse outcomes systematically, track long-term durability over multi-year follow-ups, and rigorously identify patient biomarkers that accurately predict durable therapeutic responsiveness.
