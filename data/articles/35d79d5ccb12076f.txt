TITLE: Steel industry waste and seashells yield a material that cleans water and produces hydrogen
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Researchers at the Federal University of São Carlos (UFSCar) and the Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte (UFRN) in Brazil have developed a sustainable nanomaterial from steel industry waste and seashells. When activated by sunlight, the compound can perform two tasks simultaneously: breaking down water pollutants and producing hydrogen (H₂), a promising clean fuel for the energy transition.
This technology operates via a heterojunction, which is an interface formed by combining two materials with different electrical properties: hydroxyapatite (HAp) and iron oxide (Fe₂O₃). When exposed to sunlight, this combination enables the system to remove approximately 75% of the methylene blue from the water in just two and a half hours. Methylene blue is widely used in the textile industry and poses risks to human health. It also blocks light in bodies of water, impairing photosynthesis in aquatic organisms.
The heterojunction also demonstrated the ability to generate hydrogen by combining wastewater treatment, fuel production, and waste utilization strategies into a single system.
Iron oxide was recovered from blast furnace dust, a particulate byproduct generated during the gas-cleaning stage of steelmaking that was destined for disposal. Hydroxyapatite was obtained from marine shell waste. From 5 grams of blast furnace dust, 1.02 grams of iron oxide were recovered, yielding 25%. The yield for hydroxyapatite production from marine shell waste was 63%.
The study, supported by FAPESP, was described in an article published in September in the International Journal of Hydrogen Energy. The study involved researchers from the Functional Materials Development Center (CDMF), a Research, Innovation, and Dissemination Center (RIDC) that received FAPESP funding until June of this year.
The researchers combined the two materials in three different iron oxide ratios (20%, 40%, and 60%), resulting in samples named HFe20, HFe40, and HFe60. They evaluated the structural, morphological, optical, and electrochemical properties of the samples, as well as their photocatalytic activity, which is the process by which light energy activates a material to degrade pollutants and split water molecules, generating hydrogen, under solar radiation. HFe40 was the heterojunction that performed best.
In photocatalytic hydrogen production tests, 20 milligrams of HFe40 were added to 20 milliliters of a 10% lactic acid solution. The system was then exposed to solar radiation for four hours, during which time the gas sensor monitored the hydrogen released. During this period, the heterojunction produced more than 25 micromoles (0.05 milligrams) of H₂ per gram of material.
Another significant finding was the stability of HFe40. After five reuse cycles in the methylene blue degradation process, photocatalytic activity decreased by only 6%.
To simulate natural environmental conditions, the researchers tested the material in various aqueous matrices, including rainwater, lake water, and tap water, and observed minor differences in HFe40 activity.
Next steps
Despite the positive results, the researchers emphasized to the CDMF Press Office that challenges remain before any potential large-scale application. These include increasing the efficiency of hydrogen production, more accurately quantifying the gas produced, and evaluating the stability of the material over longer periods.
According to the authors, the main result of the study is demonstrating an integrated circular economy strategy in which industrial and bio-based waste can be transformed into functional materials for simultaneous applications in environmental remediation and solar fuel production.
The research was supported by the Coordination for the Improvement of Higher Education Personnel (CAPES), the National Council for Scientific and Technological Development (CNPq), and the Graduate Program in Materials Science and Engineering at UFRN. The team at the Brazilian Nanotechnology Laboratory (LNNano) of the Brazilian Center for Research in Energy and Materials (CNPEM) also collaborated on the research.
