TITLE: Surgery Shows Greater Symptom Relief Than Pessary for Pelvic Organ Prolapse
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Surgical intervention yields greater improvements in patient-reported symptom relief and specific pelvic floor distress than pessary treatment for individuals with symptomatic pelvic organ prolapse, according to a meta-analysis in Archives of Gynecology and Obstetrics. Researchers evaluated both approaches to assess comparative effectiveness and safety, identifying measurable advantages for surgery across several clinical rating scales while noting equivalent outcomes across broader measures of daily living.
The systematic evaluation followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered under PROSPERO identifier CRD420251109479. The team searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from inception up to June 2025. The search identified randomized controlled trials and cohort studies comparing pessary devices against surgical procedures specifically in patients presenting with symptomatic pelvic organ prolapse.
Across the seven included studies, the analysis evaluated 1,261 patients. Risk of bias was assessed using Cochrane tools for randomized controlled trials and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for cohort studies. Quantitative data were synthesized through random-effects models in RevMan 5.4.1, reporting standardized mean differences for continuous inventory scores alongside risk ratios and 95 percent confidence intervals for dichotomous outcomes.
Analysis of primary clinical outcomes demonstrated statistically significant advantages for surgery across multiple metrics. Surgical patients achieved greater improvement on the Patient Global Impression of Improvement scale, yielding a risk ratio of 0.93 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.87 to 0.99 and a P-value of 0.03. Symptom severity ratings on the Patient Global Impression of Severity scale also favored surgery, with a risk ratio of 0.79, a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.66 to 0.96, and a P-value of 0.02.
Reductions in pelvic distress also favored surgery across validated inventories. The Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory, or PFDI-20, showed a standardized mean difference of 0.23 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.10 to 0.37 and a P-value of 0.0005. Significant improvements were also observed for colorectal-anal distress on the CRADI-8 subscale, with a standardized mean difference of 0.27 and a P-value of 0.001, and urinary distress on the UDI-6 subscale, showing a standardized mean difference of 0.23 and a P-value of 0.005.
Despite these advantages, secondary outcomes showed no statistically significant differences between surgery and pessary use. The treatments demonstrated comparable results for pelvic floor impact on the PFIQ-7, sexual function in sexually active patients on the PISQ-IR, and prolapse distress on the POPDI-6 subscale. Safety comparisons also revealed no significant difference between the two approaches regarding complications such as urinary tract infection.
The authors noted that statistical significance for certain findings depended on specific individual studies. Sensitivity analyses revealed that the apparent superiority of surgery on the PGI-I and PFDI-20 was contingent upon specific trials, indicating that findings require cautious interpretation. No publication bias was detected among the included investigations, with Egger’s test yielding a P-value greater than 0.05.
Because both interventions produced comparable effects on quality of life, sexual function, and complications, the authors concluded that treatment decisions should not be uniform. Instead, management for symptomatic pelvic organ prolapse should be individualized, balancing surgical outcomes and conservative pessary placement according to patient-specific factors, clinical circumstances, and personal preferences.
