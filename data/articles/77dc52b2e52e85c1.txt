TITLE: Opera loses 5 million users but earns 22% more from each one
OUTLET: PPC Land

Opera today reported third-quarter 2026 revenue of $182.8 million, up 20% from a year earlier, as advertising sales climbed 22% to $116.8 million and query revenue - the category built on search - rose 17% to $65.0 million. The Oslo-based browser company got there with fewer people: its average monthly user base across all products was 279 million in the quarter, against 284 million in the same period of 2025, while annualized revenue per user rose 22% to $2.60.
In Short
Opera, the Norwegian browser maker, made 20% more money in the third quarter of 2026 than a year before, almost all of it from ads and search, even though about 5 million fewer people used its products in an average month. Its biggest ad category, online shopping, slowed, which the company tied to cross-border challenges and new EU rules, while gaming ads more than doubled and Opera began selling connected-television ads on other companies' inventory. Opera kept its full-year targets almost where they were, nudging only the bottom of its revenue range up by $1 million, and will swap its US-listed depositary shares for ordinary shares on December 7.
The quarter in figures
The headline numbers were not a surprise. On November 3, Opera pre-announced revenue of $182.8 million and adjusted EBITDA of $43.2 million, along with 22% advertising growth and 17% query growth, according to a company release that day. Chief financial officer Frode Jacobsen opened today's call by noting the figures had been "previewed a couple of weeks ago," according to a transcript published by Investing.com. What today's release added was the detail beneath them - costs, users, cash and guidance - plus a separate announcement that changes how the company's shares trade.
Revenue landed at the top of the $181 million to $183 million range Opera had set in August. Adjusted EBITDA of $43.2 million cleared the top of the $41 million to $43 million range, according to the company's second-quarter release of August 19.
Every figure in the Q3 2026 column comes from the Form 6-K Opera filed today. The Q3 2025 user and per-user figures come from Opera's third-quarter 2025 release, because today's document does not print them.
The comparison with the previous quarter shows where growth slowed. The second-quarter column below comes from Opera's August 19 release.
Fewer users, more revenue from each
Opera's release presents its audience in terms of where it grew. The company said average monthly active users were 279 million across all products and services, with annualized average revenue per user up 22% to $2.60, and that user growth was "strong in high-ARPU markets and products, including 1 million MAUs added in Western markets, and more than 4 million MAUs added for Opera GX year-over-year in Q3." Opera GX, the gaming-focused browser, averaged 37 million monthly users across PC and mobile, up 13%.
The release does not give the prior-year total. Opera's third-quarter 2025 release put that figure at 284 million, and its second-quarter 2026 release put the April-to-June average at 288 million. On those numbers, the base shrank by roughly 5 million people over twelve months and by about 9 million in three months. The arithmetic is consistent with Opera's own growth rates: revenue up 20% and revenue per user up 22% can only coexist if the user count fell slightly.
Chief executive Lin Song - introduced on the call as Song Lin, the order Opera itself used in its November 3 release - put the third-quarter dip down to the calendar. "While the third quarter is always affected by summer holiday seasonality, especially in Europe, we continue to see strong user growth in high ARPU markets and products," he said, according to the Investing.com transcript. Seasonality explains a fall from the second quarter. It does not, on its own, explain a lower figure than the same summer a year earlier.
The trade-off shows up in the per-user figure, which was $2.13 a year earlier and $2.46 in the second quarter. Jacobsen said on the call that the Opera Ads platform "scaled particularly well in both high-value U.S. and fast-growing Asia," which "led to an even stronger ARPU growth of 22%."
The fourth quarter started better, according to the company. Opera's desktop browsers passed 100 million monthly users in October, up 16% on October 2025 and up from 94 million in the second quarter, according to the November 3 release. Opera One for iOS grew 20% in October, a figure PPC Land reported on November 12 alongside a 46% rise in US iPhone users. Asked by Lake Street Capital Markets analyst Jacob Stephan how much of the iOS growth came from browser choice screens, Lin said the US was growing "more than 40%" year over year and that the rise was "partly a browser choice, but maybe even more" a product of AI making people aware that alternatives exist. In the second quarter, Opera had reported 93% iOS growth in the UK and 50% in the US, so the October rates are lower, though the periods are not like for like.
On AI usage, Lin gave no number. He said that "a big chunk of user base, you could almost say most of them, on a monthly basis, would already be engaged in AI in one form or the other," counting address-bar autocomplete, visits to AI websites and Opera's own WebAI feature.
Advertising: e-commerce cools, gaming doubles
Advertising produced 64% of revenue. Opera's release called the strength "broad-based, with especially strong growth across gaming and travel in Q3." Over nine months, advertising revenue reached $349.1 million, up 24% on $281.6 million.
The quarter's 22% rate was five points below the 27% Opera reported for the second quarter, and the dollar gain over the second quarter was $1.4 million. The reason sits in the company's largest vertical. Jacobsen, answering Goldman Sachs analyst Eric Sheridan, described e-commerce as "a vertical that scaled extremely rapidly for us over the past three years or so," adding: "I think we 6Xed the starting point up to last quarter."
Then the brakes. "What we see exactly as you point to is some of the cross-border challenges, EU new rules," Jacobsen said. "The market needs to adapt to that, quite not too unlike what happened the beginning of last year in the U.S., and that led to a slowdown." He continued: "We still have growth across all key verticals, including e-commerce, but slower than what we saw earlier in the year. We saw that as of this summer, and Q3 is a more full picture of that."
Opera does not name its e-commerce advertisers. The timing, though, lines up with changes PPC Land has tracked. On July 1, 2026, the EU ended its €150 customs duty exemption and began charging €3 per item on low-value imports. A week later, PPC Land reported Smarter Ecommerce data showing that, as the change approached, Temu's presence in Google Shopping auctions had fallen by about half since March and SHEIN's had dropped close to zero. In late October, Pinterest said the pullback from cross-border sellers was roughly an 11-point drag on its Europe growth. Jacobsen did not spell out the US comparison. In 2025, the US dismantled its de minimis exemption in stages, ending with an executive order removing it for low-cost goods from every country from August 29, 2025.
Jacobsen's fourth-quarter comment in the release points the same way. The quarter "laps the exceptional year-end quarters of 2024 and 2025, in which sequential growth was far above average as we scaled e-commerce to become our most significant advertising vertical," he said.
Gaming and travel
Gaming is the replacement candidate. "We are definitely talking about more than 100% growth year-over-year, and it has become a sizable part of it," Lin told Piper Sandler analyst Jim Callahan, describing it as "double digits or more percent of our total Opera Ads revenue." Jacobsen sized it against e-commerce's history: "relative to total advertising, I would say it is maybe similar in size now to where e-comm was in terms of its share of total advertising three years ago."
Lin linked the gaming growth to attribution. Opera has long had a gaming audience through GX and on mobile, he said, but struggled to prove that a desktop impression led to a mobile install. Third-party cross-platform attribution now lets Opera use desktop intent signals to serve ads that lead to app installs on phones, according to his account on the call.
Callahan also asked whether big releases lift GX engagement, noting Grand Theft Auto VI arrives later this week - Rockstar Games releases it on November 19. Lin said Opera has historically worked with game franchises on launches and expected a boost for GX, but deferred to advertising partners on whether it would show up in revenue.
Travel is smaller. "It's still single-digit percent of the total advertising mix, but growing at a much faster rate than the totality," Jacobsen said.
Third-party inventory and connected TV
Opera Ads is not only Opera's own browser inventory. "Opera Ads is our advertising and monetization platform that combines our own inventory and hundreds of millions of users with audiences reached through third-party inventory," Lin said in prepared remarks. "By combining our strong first-party data asset from our browsers and modeling off of it, we deliver strong ROI performance to many of the largest advertisers in the world."
The newest piece is connected TV. "This year, we are seeing encouraging signs from newly adapted CTV inventory through an audience extension," Lin said. "While it is early, we are observing strong campaign performance, especially as advertisers deploy a cross-channel approach." In the Q&A, he described a chain that runs across three screens: Opera identifies intent from desktop browsing, shows a connected TV ad to the household, and the conversion - an app install, for example - happens on mobile, with a third-party platform attributing the result back to Opera. Opera gave no CTV revenue figure. The company has worked on CTV traffic before: in September 2024, HUMAN added pre-bid fraud detection to Opera Ads with a focus on CTV.
Jacobsen said Opera will "invest in new gaming clients and new sources of third-party inventory, like CTV, to establish additional growth vectors in 2027."
What that inventory costs
Buying third-party inventory has a price, and it shows up in cost of revenue. The release puts technology and platform fees, content cost and cost of inventory sold at $74.1 million, or 41% of revenue, "primarily reflecting the continued scaling of the Opera Ads platform." A year earlier, the same three lines totaled $52.6 million, or 35% of revenue (PPC Land calculation from the statement of operations).
The largest line, cost of inventory sold, rose 45% to $70.3 million while advertising revenue rose 22%. Opera does not allocate cost of revenue by revenue type in the release. If the whole of cost of inventory sold is set against advertising - which the release's own explanation suggests - advertising revenue left after that cost was $46.5 million, against $47.4 million a year earlier, a decline of about 2% (PPC Land calculation). Over nine months, the same measure rose about 12%, to $154.8 million. Revenue after all cost of revenue items grew about 9.5% in the quarter, to $108.7 million, half the pace of headline revenue (PPC Land calculation).
Jacobsen addressed the trade-off directly. "As a reminder, while Opera Ads carries a higher cost of revenue profile, it also requires few additional operating expenses and drives healthy incremental profitability to our overall business," he said. The fourth quarter will lean further that way: Opera expects "cost of revenue items to increase by 5-6 percentage points to represent about 46% of revenue in Q4, or just over 40% for the year as a whole," according to the transcript.
