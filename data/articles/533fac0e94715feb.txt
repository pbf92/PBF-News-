TITLE: Asmongold, a GTA first-timer, rates GTA VI a nine after nine hours
OUTLET: PPC Land

Asmongold TV today published "So I Played GTA 6..", a recording that runs to just over nine hours in which the streamer Asmongold, who tells viewers he has never played a Grand Theft Auto game, works through the opening of Rockstar Games' Grand Theft Auto VI on an Xbox and ends with a verdict: "I would rate it like a nine." By 20:02 UTC, the video page showed 2,358,707 views, 83,324 likes and 14,000 comments, and placed the upload at No. 28 on YouTube's trending list for video games.
Grand Theft Auto VI went on sale today. For the advertising trade, the score matters less than the world the recording walks through. Rockstar's fictional state of Leonida has a photo app called Snapmatic that lets one character "ingest everything about a person", a bank that refuses an account and points the customer to a phone app called Buck Me, a ride-hailing app called RideMe and a radio station that jokes about sponsorship. The comment section under the video argued about other things. Its most-liked comment, with about 11,000 likes and 813 replies, says the game stops a car driven at protesters, a scene the recording never shows, and much of the rest repeats, in harsher words, Asmongold's own running joke that the game was written by women.
In Short
Asmongold, whose YouTube channel has 4.76 million subscribers, posted a nine-hour first playthrough of Grand Theft Auto VI on the game's release day, rated it "like a nine" and said it would probably win game of the year, while saying it should not because it is not on PC. The video drew more than 2.3 million views within hours and is the kind of long, unscripted creator content that brands buying gaming audiences around a major launch have to judge, in a game that itself parodies social media, fintech apps and ad-funded radio. Under it, a comment section of more than 14,000 comments is led by complaints about limits on violence, "HR women" and the two leads, and none of the 20 comments the page displayed at 20:02 UTC mentions those parodies. The public view count now includes YouTube's estimate of people watching together on a TV and registers from the first frame, so it says little about how much of a nine-hour video anyone actually watched.
The video and its numbers
The upload sits on Asmongold TV, the streamer's main YouTube channel. According to its description, "Asmongold plays GTA 6 (Grand Theft Auto VI) for the first time, exploring Vice City and the open world of Leonida in Rockstar Games' long-awaited release." The description credits two channel editors, "CatDany & Daily Dose of Asmongold", and lists the places where the streamer broadcasts and republishes: a Twitch channel under the handle zackrawrr, a Kick channel, an X account, a subreddit, and two "VOD Rerun" channels, one on Twitch and one on Kick.
Two other lines in the description concern money and rights. One is an affiliate link for a gaming guide add-on unrelated to Grand Theft Auto: "Download the fastest leveling guide on the market which seamlessly integrates into your in-game RestedXP addon and get 10% off with my link." The other is a takedown route: "If you own the copyright of content showed in this video and would like it to be removed:", followed by two X accounts belonging to the editors. The page tags the video with a single game, Grand Theft Auto VI, dated 2026.
The oldest comments on the page were marked as posted 14 hours before 20:02 UTC, which puts the upload in the early hours of November 19.
How much those 2,358,707 views say about attention is a separate question. YouTube began including an estimate of people watching together on a TV in public view counts this month, and a view registers when playback starts, across long-form videos, Shorts and live streams. YouTube says Partner Program earnings still run on engaged views rather than on the public number. For a nine-hour recording, the counter therefore records starts, partly modelled, and nothing about how far into the session anyone stayed.
The captions are YouTube's automatic transcript, and they carry no speaker labels. Game dialogue, the streamer's commentary and viewer messages that he reads aloud run together in the same lines, so the attributions in this article are made from context. Profanity appears in the captions as "[ __ ]", and quotes below keep that placeholder where it falls.
A first-timer on an Xbox
Asmongold returns several times to the fact that this is his first Grand Theft Auto. "I've never played a Grand Theft Auto game before in my life," he says an hour and a half in. At 5:47:22 he extends the point to the genre: "I've never played Saints Row." In the closing review he puts the caveat on the record: "But this is my first um this is my first GTA game that I've ever played."
The platform was a choice of timing rather than preference. Asked by a viewer at 1:59:57 whether he is on a PlayStation, he answers: "No, it's an Xbox." At 5:08:23 he explains why, after reading out a viewer's complaint that the game "runs like ass on Series X": "It's because people said I couldn't do that New Zealand [ __ ] on a PS5. They said I can only do it on a uh on an Xbox." The remark appears to refer to setting a console's region to New Zealand, one of the first places where midnight on a release date arrives, to start playing early. The stream does not show how the game was unlocked.
The Xbox detail has a commercial echo. On October 28, Microsoft chief executive Satya Nadella pointed to GTA VI arriving on Xbox in November, in a quarter when Xbox revenue fell 2% to $5.38bn.
Performance barely registers in the commentary. When a viewer asks "How does the 30 fps feel?", the answer is "It's nothing. It's really nothing." Later he adds: "Like maybe like the first five minutes I noticed it, but then it's like you just get used to it and then that's just the game." The controller is another matter. "I can't aim with a controller. I really can't," he says at 5:07:19, and near the end he spells out what he would rather have: "I want to play it on a computer at 120 FPS with high-end graphics and aim with a mouse and have it on Steam and that's it, you know?" A viewer's message read aloud claims a PC version will arrive in the first quarter of next year. Nothing on the stream confirms that date.
What the stream shows of the game
The recording is a long first look at the game's opening chapters, and the on-screen prompts and dialogue the streamer reads out record a set of systems that will matter to anyone writing about, sponsoring or advertising around the game. According to the transcript, they are:
- Neighbourhood restrictions. Jason Duval, the first of the two leads, lives in a stilt house in the Keys. A prompt at 1:06:44 states: "Vehicles in your neighborhood can't be stolen." It returns at 2:10:30. A second prompt, garbled in the captions, says a weapon cannot be equipped "while you're in your neighborhood."
- Police dispatch. "Crime reported" warnings follow traffic offences. At 2:18:51 a character installs an app on Jason's phone with the line: "Now you'll be able to see when and where the cops are being dispatched."
- Cash, a home stash and a bank. Money is deposited in a hidden stash at the house, with a "Cash deposit" option at 2:56:41. At 5:17 a bank turns Jason down and offers a phone-only account instead, described below.
- Vehicle damage and repair. Cars lose wheels and durability, are totalled, and are repaired and resprayed at garages. One garage owner offers to tow Jason's car and put it "on Brian's account."
- Shopping and appearance. A haircut costs "$60". Outfits can be saved with a "Save current outfit" option. Flowers and a custom necklace spelling the name of Lucia's son, Tomas, are ordered for delivery, the necklace in "a couple of days."
- House repairs and a relationship meter. Once Lucia Caminos, the second lead, is released from the Hamlet Correctional Facility, a run of household tasks opens up: fixing a railing, repairing a leaking roof and repainting walls, with the choice "Pastel peach, stormy blue, or tropical green." At 6:15:40 the streamer reads out the result: "It says house repairs complete and then I got the plus heart." Earlier, at 4:48:01, he asks why he has just lost a heart.
- Side activities. The transcript records pool, a bar fight, dancing, a dating app ("Just checking the field. Swiping my way to success"), workouts ("Finish the workout"), meditation on a dock and games played on a friend's console.
- Ride-hailing. At 5:30:43 a prompt reads: "You can use the RideMe app to order a ride." The fare on screen is $26. "$26. Are you kidding me?" the streamer says, and walks.
Leonida's ad economy
Grand Theft Auto has long parodied the businesses that fund American media, and the transcript shows the new game aiming at the ones PPC Land covers every day: social platforms that harvest personal data, fintech apps that serve people banks turn away, gig work, wellness resellers, AI and sponsored audio.
Data, scrolled
The sharpest line on personal data comes from Mandy, a tenant whose blocked sink Jason has to clear before she will pay the rent. "It's just so crazy what you can learn from a little scrolling on Snapmatic," she says. "You can just like ingest everything about a person." Everything, she goes on, "like what kind of music they listen to or they used to date or where they work out." Jason answers: "Yeah, I think that is called stalking, Mandy." Her reply: "Nuh-uh. It's not stalking if you've met in person."
A second social network appears four hours later. Cal, Jason's friend, shows him their landlord Brian's phone. "Dude was all over his ex-wife's life invader," Cal says. "Anyway, he liked a few photos, you know, left a few comments." Brian, by Cal's account, "Came to old Uncle Cal to make it look like it never happened."
Banks, credit and an app
The fintech satire is the most developed in the recording. Lucia needs a bank account for her prison work wages, and Jason does not have one. At the bank, an employee tells him his credit is "dog [ __ ]" and says: "The computer won't even let me get you an account, much less one of our mortgage products." The alternative is an app. "The best thing I can do is set you up with some Buckme accounts. It's on your phone. There are transaction limits," the employee says, adding: "build your credit up and I'll be able to get you a real account. Until then, my hands are tied."
The app opens with "Welcome to Buck Me." An advert follows on the phone, captured mid-sentence by the captions: "Your family's future by opening a Buck family account today. See online for details." Lucia then relays Cal's theory of retail banking: "You know, Cal says that banks use your late fees to fund pleasure cruises for washed up celebrities and war criminals."
Rent collection gets the same treatment. "You know, it's really annoying that Brian makes everyone pay the rent manually," Mandy says, complaining that it happens "on a sketchy little website that looks like it was made by a fifth grader." Her verdict: "Like, hello. Ever heard of autopay?"
Work, wellness and AI
Mandy also announces a promotion. "I'm chief director of product now," she says, and "I manage like a million engineers, but I'm totally work from home." Andy, a friend of Lucia's who works at a restaurant, has a second job: "I give people access to great wellness products without the crazy overhead of retail. It's been super lucrative so far." Neighbours argue about short-term lets. "People rent out their houses," one says. The other calls the house next door "a goddamn revolving door" with "a different set of strangers every weekend."
AI turns up twice. Cal introduces an independent game made "in a log cabin in Liberty State" with the words "The design, the code, the art. He even did the voiceover."
