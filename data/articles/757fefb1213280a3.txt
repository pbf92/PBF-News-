TITLE: UK watchdog bans 3 Tony's Chocolonely ads that topped Sainsbury's search
OUTLET: PPC Land

Britain's advertising regulator ruled on November 25, 2026 that three sponsored search results for Tony's Chocolonely chocolate bars on Sainsbury's website were paid-for online ads for less healthy food, and that they broke a rule in force since January 5, 2026. The Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) upheld a complaint against Tony's Chocolonely Ltd, which had paid for the three bars to appear as the first three results when shoppers typed "Chocolate" into the supermarket's search bar.
The decision turns on one question: when a brand pays a retailer to push its product higher in the shop's own search results, is that an advert or just a product listing? The ASA said it is an advert. "The ads must not appear again in the form complained of," the ruling states.
In Short
Tony's Chocolonely paid Sainsbury's to put three of its chocolate bars at the top of search results for "chocolate", and the UK ad regulator ruled that those paid spots were online ads for unhealthy food, which have been banned since January 2026. It affects every maker of sweets, snacks and other less healthy food that buys sponsored slots on supermarket websites, not just Tony's Chocolonely. The key point is that paying for a better position turns an ordinary product listing into an advert, even when the shopper was already searching for chocolate.
What shoppers saw on August 12
The three ads were seen on August 12, 2026. Each sat in the top three places of the results page on Sainsbury's website after a search for "Chocolate", and each looked much like an ordinary product tile: a photo of the bar in its wrapper, part of the product name, the price, a button to add it to the basket, the words "Nectar Price" (the label Sainsbury's uses for its loyalty-card prices) and the word "Sponsored".
Only the colour of the wrapper and the flavour changed from one ad to the next.
All three are 127g filled chocolate bars. The text on screen was cut off, so ad (a), for example, read "Tony's Chocolonely 127g Filled Creamy Praline & Crunchy Cara...". The full product names come from the company's own response to the complaint.
The complainant, who is not named, challenged whether the three ads breached the advertising rules "because they were paid-for ads for identifiable less healthy food products placed on the internet."
The rules behind the case
The ban on paid online ads for less healthy food and drink came into force on January 5, 2026, when new rules were added to the two UK advertising codes: the CAP Code, which covers non-broadcast advertising such as websites and social media, and the BCAP Code, which covers TV and radio. According to the ruling, the rules reflect the requirements of three pieces of law: the Communications Act 2003, The Advertising (Less Healthy Food Definitions and Exemptions) Regulations 2024, and The Advertising (Less Healthy Food and Drink) (Brand Advertising Exemption) Regulations 2025.
The rules came with a separate guidance document from the Committee of Advertising Practice, titled "Advertising of less healthy food and drink products". The ruling describes it as setting out "various tests and exemptions relevant to the ASA's approach to assessing individual ads under the relevant Code rules."
The rule at the centre of this case is CAP Code (Edition 12) rule 15.19, headed "Placement of less healthy food and drink product advertisements online". The ruling summarises it this way: the rule, "reflecting the provisions of the relevant legislation, required that persons must not pay for ads for an identifiable less healthy food or drink product to be placed on the internet."
Unlike television, there is no time window online. Under the industry agreement that preceded the law, ads for identifiable less healthy products could not appear as paid-for online advertisements at any time, while TV and on-demand ads for them were barred before the 9pm watershed. The industry began applying the restrictions voluntarily on October 1, 2025, before the legal start date three months later.
How Tony's Chocolonely defended the ads
Tony's Chocolonely did not dispute the basic facts. It confirmed that it had paid for the listings "to be given an enhanced prominence in search results, labelled as 'Sponsored', on Sainsbury's website when shoppers searched for a relevant term such as 'chocolate'." It also accepted that each ad showed a specific, identifiable "less healthy" product.
Its case rested on how it read the official guidance. The guidance, according to the company's response, said that product listings on retail sites were ordinarily outside the scope of the less healthy food rules "as they were communications in an advertiser's own media space, but that different considerations applied where listings were afforded enhanced prominence through a paid commercial relationship." The guidance added that the ASA would look at the terms of that commercial relationship and, in particular, "whether a listing had been placed in a manner different to ordinary, organic product listings."
Tony's Chocolonely took that to mean the ASA would judge sponsored retail search ads case by case, "rather than that there was a blanket prohibition." It placed the ads, it said, in good faith.
The company then made three further points:
- The ads appeared only when a shopper actively searched for "chocolate", so the shopper had "already chosen to browse products which fell into a 'less healthy' product category."
- Its bars would have shown up in the ordinary results anyway. Paying only moved them higher.
- The paid placement "did not introduce the products into a context where consumers would not otherwise have encountered them, in contrast to, for example, a paid-for display banner on a third-party website or a paid-for social media ad."
It also told the regulator that sponsored search ads for "less healthy" products "remained widely available across UK retail platforms", which it believed suggested that others in the industry read the rule the same way. The ruling does not comment on that claim.
Once the complaint arrived, Tony's Chocolonely paused its sponsored search result ads for identifiable "less healthy" products "across all retail platforms", while it waited for the decision. The ruling does not say when the complaint was received.
What the ASA decided
The regulator accepted the facts the company had stressed. The ASA "acknowledged they were presented only to consumers who were searching Sainsbury's website under relevant search terms, and that the paid placement had the effect of boosting the products' prominence in search results in which they would anyway have appeared."
Those facts did not change the outcome. "However, the CAP Code applied, generally, to paid-for search listings," the ruling states.
On the guidance, the ASA said it "highlighted that while listings on retail sites were ordinarily out of scope of the rule, payment from the manufacturer or supplier could in some circumstances bring the listing into scope." It then drew the line in a single sentence: "We considered that payment from a manufacturer or supplier to a retail website for a product listing's enhanced prominence in search results was an ad that the manufacturer or supplier of that product had paid to be placed on the internet."
The difference between a paid result and an ordinary one did the rest. "Because Tony's Chocolonely had paid for the product listings to be placed in a manner different to ordinary, organic product listings (to which the Code did not apply), we considered they were paid-for ads which had been placed on the internet," the ruling reads. That put the ads within rule 15.19, provided the products were identifiable and less healthy.
Both conditions were met. "We concluded the ads were paid-for ads for identifiable 'less healthy' foods, and therefore breached the Code."
How the ASA tested the ads
According to the ruling, the assessment followed four steps.
- Was it a paid-for ad? Yes. Tony's Chocolonely paid Sainsbury's for enhanced prominence, and the listings were placed differently from ordinary results. Ordinary, unpaid product listings on a retailer's site are not covered by the code.
- Was it placed on the internet? Yes. A payment from a manufacturer or supplier to a retail website for better search placement counts as paying for an ad to be placed on the internet.
- Was the product identifiable? A less healthy product is identifiable, in the ruling's words, "if persons in the UK could reasonably be expected to be able to identify the ad as being for that product." Each ad showed a photo of a specific bar in its wrapper and part of its name. The ASA considered that shoppers could tell which product each ad was for, "and that clicking on the ads would take them to their respective product listings."
- Was the product less healthy? A food or drink must pass two tests to count. First, it must be classed as high in fat, salt or sugar (HFSS) under the Department of Health and Social Care's Nutrient Profiling Technical Guidance. Second, it must fall into a food or drink category set out in law. All three bars were classed as HFSS and fell within Category 4, which the law describes as "Confectionary including chocolates and sweets".
What Tony's Chocolonely must change
The outcome is short. Ads (a), (b) and (c) "breached CAP Code (Edition 12) rule 15.19 (Placement of less healthy food and drink product advertisements online)."
The ASA's instruction covers more than the three ads. "We told Tony's Chocolonely Ltd to ensure their paid-for ads on the internet were not for identifiable 'less healthy' foods," the ruling states. The ruling sets no financial penalty.
Why this matters for retail media
Sponsored product slots inside a shop's own search results are a core product of any retail media network. Brands pay to move their goods up the page at the moment a shopper is looking for something to buy. In the UK, online retail media spending reached £1.5 billion in the first half of 2025, according to IAB UK.
Tony's Chocolonely's defence rested on an argument that many in the business would recognise: a sponsored result on a grocer's search page is different from a banner on a news site, because the shopper is already looking for chocolate. The ASA did not reject the facts behind that argument. It simply held that they do not matter once a brand has paid. Under this ruling, it is the payment, not the shopper's intent or the context, that turns a listing into an ad.
The ruling draws a clear split between two kinds of onsite placement - ads and listings shown on the retailer's own website. On one side sit ordinary product listings, which the retailer shows in its own media space and which the code does not cover. On the other side sit listings that a manufacturer or supplier pays to boost. Paid boosts of this kind, often sold as sponsored products, are now treated as online ads under rule 15.19 whenever they show an identifiable less healthy product.
There are limits to how far the decision reaches. The ruling is addressed to Tony's Chocolonely as the advertiser that paid. It says nothing about any duty on Sainsbury's, or on other retailers that sell such slots. Brand advertising, which promotes a company without showing a specific restricted product, is also treated differently: the UK government confirmed on May 22, 2025 that it would exempt brand ads that do not feature less healthy products. The three Tony's Chocolonely ads were not brand ads. Each showed one bar, in its wrapper, with its name.
The ruling lands in the first Christmas season under the rules, and chocolate has been at the centre of the new regime from the start. At Easter, Lindt topped System1's UK ad rankings with 5.9 Stars in what PPC Land described as the first big seasonal test of the restrictions. In November, Sainsbury's shared first place in System1's early Christmas rankings, in a list where supermarkets took seven of the top 12 places.
