TITLE: Cochrane review: Hearing aids improve quality of life but evidence lacking for loneliness, memory
OUTLET: Hearing Practitioner Australia

Hearing aids provide substantial benefits for hearing-related quality of life and listening ability in adults with mild to moderate hearing loss, but an updated Cochrane review found no evidence they improve loneliness or working memory.
The review, Hearing aids for mild to moderate hearing loss in adults, examined the effects of hearing aid use in adults, building on an earlier version of the review published in 2017. The original review assessed randomised controlled trials comparing hearing aids with passive or active controls in adults with mild to moderate hearing loss.
It now includes 16 randomised controlled trials involving 2,261 participants, adding 11 new studies to the evidence considered in the previous review.
Studies were conducted in the USA, Europe, Brazil, Hong Kong and Australia and published between 1987 and 2025. Participants had mild to moderate hearing loss, with average ages across the studies ranging from 58 to 83 years. Study duration ranged from four weeks to three years.
The review found hearing aids likely provide a large benefit for hearing-specific health-related quality of life, including participation, based on moderate-certainty evidence.
There was also moderate-certainty evidence of a likely large benefit to listening ability, while hearing aids may provide a small benefit to general health-related quality of life, although the evidence for this outcome was considered low certainty.
However, the review did not provide evidence that hearing aids were effective at improving loneliness or working memory.
Review author Melanie Ferguson, Adjunct Professor at Curtin University, said the findings remained compatible with the established role of hearing aids in managing hearing difficulties.
“Evidence remains compatible with the widespread provision of hearing aids as first-line clinical management in those who seek help for those who seek help for hearing difficulties,” she said on Linkedin.
“The review did not show improvements in loneliness or working memory – although absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Longer-term, placebo-controlled trials are needed, but may not be ethically justified.”
The study also showed evidence for pain is “very uncertain”. Six studies (1186 participants) monitored adverse effects. Of these, one study reported two instances of pain or discomfort (one participant stopped using hearing aids due to pain while wearing them; one stopped using hearing aids due to ear dryness requiring treatment).
“Certainty of evidence was very low, downgraded for risk of bias and extreme imprecision,” the authors stated.
