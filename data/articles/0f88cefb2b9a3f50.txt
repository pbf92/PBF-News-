TITLE: Researchers Question Trial Design and Sponsor Transparency in Infant Diet Study
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Medical researchers have raised significant methodological and transparency concerns regarding the JASMINE trial, a key clinical study evaluating an exclusive human milk diet for very low birthweight infants in Japan. In correspondence published in the Journal of Perinatology, authors Kazuhiko Kabe and Takeo Saio scrutinized the evidence base that supported regulatory approval and national reimbursement for the dietary regimen, outlining critical issues spanning trial execution, competing interest reporting, and downstream economic evaluations.
A primary criticism focuses on the open-label design of the JASMINE trial, registered as jRCT2031210384, which the commentators argue introduces substantial risks of observer bias. The published trial outcomes reported superior weight gain velocity of 13.44 grams per kilogram per day for infants on an exclusive human milk diet compared to 11.96 grams per kilogram per day in the control group, yielding a statistically significant difference of p equals 0.006. Additionally, the trial observed a shorter time to achieve full enteral feeding, recording 20.0 days in the intervention arm versus 25.9 days in the comparator group, with a p-value of 0.03.
In their critique, Kabe and Saio explicitly asked the trial investigators to clarify whether blinded endpoint adjudication was ever considered during the initial trial design.
The critique also targets a prominent discrepancy between declared competing interests and the trial’s comprehensive corporate funding structure. Although the JASMINE study authors formally declared that they had no competing interests, the operational execution revealed deep commercial involvement. Prolacta Bioscience Inc. funded the clinical trial, designed its statistical analysis plan, supplied the study fortifier, and covered institutional review board fees through a contract research organization. Furthermore, medical writing support was funded by Clinigen K.K., the entity that subsequently secured Japanese marketing authorization for the commercial product.
The commentators also noted that two named employees of Prolacta Bioscience directly performed data verification and provided statistical support for the final manuscript. While acknowledging that International Committee of Medical Journal Editors guidelines differentiate individual financial ties from sponsor-level support, Kabe and Saio stated that declaring no competing interests fails to meet expected scientific transparency standards given the sponsor’s extensive operational integration across design, analysis, and manuscript preparation.
These methodological and disclosure limitations further propagated into health technology assessments and cost-effectiveness models used for national reimbursement decisions. A related cost-utility analysis co-authored by JASMINE investigator Katsumi Mizuno and two individuals disclosing direct financial payments from Prolacta Bioscience modeled the diet as cost-effective at prices up to 4,750,000 Japanese yen per course. However, its pivotal effectiveness input was a necrotizing enterocolitis relative risk of 0.31 derived from an earlier modeling study rather than direct trial results.
In reality, the JASMINE trial recorded only a single case of necrotizing enterocolitis, providing an insufficient empirical basis for robust clinical risk reduction estimates. Furthermore, the model’s favorable relative risk estimate of 0.31 contrasts sharply with the null finding of a relative risk of 0.95 documented in a comprehensive 2019 Cochrane systematic review comparing human milk-derived fortifiers to bovine-derived fortifiers. Kabe and Saio concluded that evidence underpinning public regulatory approval and national healthcare reimbursement must maintain demonstrable independence from commercial sponsor influence.
