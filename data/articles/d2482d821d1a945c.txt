TITLE: Device and app makers face German fines up to 2.5% of sales from 2027
OUTLET: PPC Land

Germany today published the law that decides who polices the European Union's Cyber Resilience Act inside the country, and the answer is a single agency. The act, printed in the Federal Law Gazette (Bundesgesetzblatt 2026 Teil I Nr. 355), makes the Federal Office for Information Security, known by its German initials BSI, responsible for checking that connected hardware and software sold in Germany meet the EU's security rules. Part of the agency's new job starts tomorrow, November 26, 2026. Its power to fine companies does not start until December 11, 2027.
The law is dated November 20, 2026, the day it was signed by Federal President Frank-Walter Steinmeier, Chancellor Friedrich Merz and Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt. It does not add a single obligation for companies. What it does is name the enforcer, hand it its tools and set the dates from which each tool can be used.
In Short
Germany has put its federal cybersecurity agency, the BSI, in charge of checking that connected devices and software sold in the country follow the EU's Cyber Resilience Act. From tomorrow the agency approves the labs that test products and acts as the national desk for security incident reports, but it cannot fine anyone until December 11, 2027, when the EU rules apply in full. For companies selling smart TVs, streaming boxes, routers or apps in Germany, the rules and the fine levels stay exactly as Brussels wrote them; what changes is that there is now a named authority, with a budget and staff, to enforce them.
A short law with a long reach
The act has four articles. Article 1 rewrites parts of the BSI Act (BSI-Gesetz), the statute that defines what the agency is allowed to do. Articles 2 and 3 make smaller changes to the Telecommunications Act and the Energy Industry Act. Article 4 sets the start dates.
Its full title is "Gesetz zur Durchführung der Verordnung (EU) 2024/2847 über horizontale Cybersicherheitsanforderungen für Produkte mit digitalen Elementen (Cyberresilienz-Verordnung)", which translates as Act implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements.
The road to today was quick by German standards. The federal government sent the bill to the Bundesrat, the chamber that represents the 16 states, on May 1, 2026, flagged as particularly urgent. It reached the Bundestag as printed paper 21/6134, dated May 26, 2026. The Bundestag's Interior Committee recommended it on October 14, 2026, with one change: the start dates. According to the parliamentary record, the full Bundestag passed it in its 102nd sitting on October 15, 2026. The committee's report shows the governing CDU/CSU and SPD, plus the Greens, voting in favour, the AfD against, and Die Linke abstaining.
An analysis by Henrik Hanssen and Henri Bringmann of law firm Hogan Lovells Cadwalader, published on October 20, 2026, gives October 14 as the adoption date. That was the day of the committee vote. The same analysis says the Bundesrat did not object and that "the urgency of this legislative initiative was emphasized throughout the legislative process in light of the CRA's advancing applicability."
Hanssen, a counsel at the firm in Hamburg, wrote on LinkedIn today that the act "sets out how the CRA will be supervised and enforced in Germany."
What the EU regulation does
The Cyber Resilience Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, dated October 23, 2024 and published in the EU's Official Journal on November 20, 2024. According to the Hogan Lovells Cadwalader analysis, it "establishes essential cybersecurity requirements for 'products with digital elements', including many hardware and software products and their remote data processing solutions."
Put simply, the regulation covers most products that run code and connect to another device or a network. Their makers must design them to meet a list of security requirements, keep handling vulnerabilities after sale, and report serious security problems to the authorities. Importers and distributors carry their own, lighter duties.
Why does a regulation that applies directly in every member state need a German law at all? Because it still needs national machinery to work. "Although the CRA, as an EU regulation, applies directly in all EU Member States it still requires national implementation in several respects," the law firm wrote. Each country must name market surveillance authorities - officials who check products already on sale and can order them fixed, withdrawn or recalled - and must be able to impose the fines set out in Article 64 of the regulation. Each country must also name a notifying authority: the office that approves the independent testing labs, known as conformity assessment bodies, which must check some higher-risk product types before they can carry the CE mark and go on sale.
Germany has filled every one of those slots with the same agency.
One agency, four jobs
The act inserts a new Part 8 into the BSI Act titled "Cybersicherheit von Produkten mit digitalen Elementen" (cybersecurity of products with digital elements). According to the Hogan Lovells Cadwalader analysis, the BSI is designated as "the sole competent national market surveillance authority" for the regulation. The wording of the designation itself is brief. Section 65(1) reads: "Das Bundesamt ist die zuständige nationale Marktüberwachungsbehörde gemäß der Verordnung (EU) 2024/2847." In English: the Federal Office is the competent national market surveillance authority under the regulation. Section 66(1) uses the same construction for the testing labs: "Das Bundesamt ist die notifizierende Behörde gemäß der Verordnung (EU) 2024/2847."
A CSIRT - computer security incident response team - is a national emergency desk for cyber attacks and security flaws. Under the new § 5(3) sentence 2 no. 6, the BSI performs "seine Aufgaben als CSIRT gemäß der Verordnung (EU) 2024/2847", its tasks as a CSIRT under the regulation.
The single-agency model is a choice. For the EU's AI Act, the German draft criticised by the digital industry association BVDW in October 2025 spread market surveillance across several authorities, with a coordination point at the Bundesnetzagentur, the federal network regulator. The same regulator already acts as Germany's Digital Services Coordinator under the DSA and, since 2026, as the country's Data Act enforcer. Product cybersecurity now sits with a different federal body.
Two start dates, one long gap
Article 4 of the act splits the timetable in two. Paragraph 1 lists what takes effect "am Tag nach der Verkündung", the day after promulgation, which is tomorrow: the BSI's new task entries for market surveillance and notification, its CSIRT role, the whole of § 66 on testing labs, and the changes to telecoms and energy law. Paragraph 2 covers everything else: "Im Übrigen tritt dieses Gesetz am 11. Dezember 2027 in Kraft" - in all other respects, the act enters into force on December 11, 2027.
That second date mirrors the EU regulation. Under Article 71(2) of the Cyber Resilience Act, the regulation applies from December 11, 2027, with two exceptions: Article 14, on manufacturers' reporting duties, has applied since September 11, 2026, and Chapter IV (Articles 35 to 51), on the notification of testing labs, since June 11, 2026.
Germany was late for both earlier dates. The government's bill had set June 11, 2026 for the task entries and the testing-lab provisions, and September 11, 2026 for the CSIRT provisions. The Interior Committee replaced both with the day after promulgation, noting that the June and September dates had been overtaken by the calendar. The BSI's notifying role therefore starts 168 days after the EU chapter it serves began to apply, and its CSIRT role 76 days after the reporting duty, by PPC Land's count.
Hanssen described tomorrow's step on LinkedIn this way: "Following today's publication, the Federal Office for Information Security (BSI) will formally assume its role as market surveillance authority tomorrow (26 November 2026). While the material reporting obligations under Art. 14 CRA apply since 11 September 2026, the German provisions on administrative fines apply from 11 December 2027, the date of general applicability of the CRA."
The law firm's October analysis made the same point at greater length: "while the BSI assumes the role of market surveillance authority on the day following the promulgation of the German CRA Implementation Act, it may exercise the corresponding enforcement powers, including the imposition of administrative fines, only from 11 December 2027, in line with Art. 71(2) first sentence CRA."
The gazette text draws a finer line than either summary. What takes effect tomorrow is an entry in the list of BSI tasks: the agency will "Aufgaben und Befugnisse als zuständige Marktüberwachungsbehörde gemäß der Verordnung (EU) 2024/2847 wahrnehmen" - perform the tasks and powers of the competent market surveillance authority under the regulation. The section that formally names the BSI as that authority, § 65, takes effect on December 11, 2027, along with the complaints office.
How a testing lab gets approved in Germany
Section 66, in force from tomorrow, sets out the approval path for the labs that will assess higher-risk products. According to the gazette text, it runs in five steps.
- The national accreditation body assesses and monitors the lab, under the EU's 2008 accreditation regulation (Regulation (EC) No 765/2008). Accreditation can come with conditions, be time-limited, be granted subject to revocation, and have conditions added later.
- As an exception, the BSI can assess a lab itself "sofern an der Notifizierung der Konformitätsbewertungsstellen ein öffentliches Interesse besteht" - where there is a public interest in notifying it. In that case the BSI also monitors the lab.
- The BSI decides, on the strength of an accreditation certificate or its own assessment, whether the lab meets the requirements in Article 39 of the regulation.
- If it does, the BSI authorises the lab to carry out conformity assessments and notifies it to the European Commission and the other member states under Article 35(1).
- The authorisation can carry conditions and time limits, and is granted under the condition that neither the Commission nor another member state objects under Article 43(5) of the regulation.
According to the Hogan Lovells Cadwalader analysis, step four leaves the BSI no discretion: "the BSI must designate the body in question if the accreditation certificate, or its own assessment, confirms that the body meets the requirements of Art. 39 CRA." The analysis adds that, according to the European Commission, a relevant number of these labs should be designated in all member states by the end of 2026. Manufacturers are preparing too: LG Electronics said in its third-quarter report that it had obtained TÜV Rheinland's qualification as a designated EU cybersecurity testing lab, without saying which EU framework that testing serves.
How a security report reaches the BSI
Reporting is the one part of the regulation already running. According to the European Commission's page on reporting under the Cyber Resilience Act, the process for a manufacturer works like this:
- A manufacturer becomes aware of an actively exploited vulnerability in its product or a severe incident affecting the product's security.
- Within 24 hours, it submits an early warning.
- Within 72 hours, it submits a full notification.
- For an actively exploited vulnerability, it files a final report no later than 14 days after a corrective measure is available. For a severe incident, the final report is due within a month of the 72-hour notification.
