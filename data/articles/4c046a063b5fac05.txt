TITLE: HUMAN traces up to 40 billion fraudulent ad requests a day to cheap TV boxes
OUTLET: PPC Land

On November 23, 2026, HUMAN Security published the findings of an investigation into BADBOX 3.0, a fraud operation that hid video ads inside cheap television boxes and, at its height, produced about 40 billion requests for ads every day. The company's Satori Threat Intelligence and Research Team says it uncovered and disrupted the operation, and calls it "the largest ad fraud operation by bid volume ever publicly disclosed." That ranking is HUMAN's own, made on HUMAN's own chart. On that chart, the previous record belonged to VASTFLUX, a scheme HUMAN dismantled in 2023 that peaked at 12 billion requests a day.
Each of those requests was a bid request: the message an app or website sends out to advertisers' buying systems when it has an ad slot to fill, asking who wants it and at what price. According to HUMAN, BADBOX 3.0 accounted for more than 4% of all connected TV (CTV) traffic seen by its detection system at the peak. Connected TV means televisions and streaming boxes that play internet video and the ads inside it. HUMAN's report says the peak was "a larger peak than the last five years' worth of published Satori investigations combined."
The scheme did two jobs at once. Malicious add-ons on the boxes played video ads in windows the owner could not see, while sending ad requests that claimed to come from well-known, legitimate TV apps. Other add-ons quietly enrolled the boxes, without their owners' knowledge or consent, in eleven residential proxy networks. A residential proxy is a service that routes a paying customer's internet traffic through someone's home connection, so the traffic looks like it comes from an ordinary household. HUMAN links the operation's central software catalog to TXCZ, which it describes as a Chinese electronics manufacturer. The report contains no response from TXCZ.
In Short
Cheap streaming boxes, mostly unbranded and built in mainland China, were infected with software that secretly played video ads in a hidden window and rented out their owners' home internet connections. Advertisers who bought what looked like ads on well-known TV apps may have paid for ads that nobody saw, and HUMAN says the operation peaked at about 40 billion ad requests a day, more than three times the previous record on its chart. HUMAN says its own customers are protected, but its report names neither the TV apps whose names were borrowed nor the box models involved, and it warns that the people behind the scheme have rebuilt after every previous takedown.
The numbers HUMAN published
The report, written by eight Satori researchers, leads with three figures: about 40 billion bid requests a day at peak, more than 4% of the CTV traffic observed by the Human Defense Platform at peak, and a volume more than three times that of VASTFLUX. HUMAN's comparison chart is titled "Daily bid requests, publicly disclosed ad fraud operations" and uses each operation's daily volume "as reported at disclosure."
Source: HUMAN Satori Threat Intelligence and Research Team, Figure 1 of the report. By PPC Land's calculation, the six other operations on the chart add up to about 31 billion requests a day, which is consistent with HUMAN's statement that BADBOX 3.0 alone exceeded five years of its published investigations.
Two cautions apply to that comparison. It is HUMAN's chart, compiled by HUMAN's own team, and it measures something different from money lost. A bid request is an offer to sell an ad slot, not a sale. The report does not say how many of the 40 billion daily requests were actually bought, at what price, or how much advertisers paid in total.
The scale of the operation is also hard to translate into a count of infected boxes. HUMAN does not publish one. The closest figure it offers comes from a single file mirror, discussed below, which HUMAN describes as a floor rather than an estimate.
How the scheme worked, step by step
According to HUMAN's report, an infected box moves through six stages, from the factory to the ad market.
- The box. The devices are "low-cost, off-brand, uncertified hardware" running the Android Open Source Project, the free version of Android that any manufacturer can use without Google's approval, according to HUMAN. They were built in mainland China and shipped internationally. HUMAN stresses that they are not Android TV OS devices and are not Play Protect certified, the label Google gives to Android devices that have passed its security and compatibility checks.
- The backdoor. The investigation began with a hidden entry point, a backdoor, packaged as a system application. It has two addresses written into its code. One fetches the list of add-ons to load in the background; the other handles logging.
- The check-in. The box contacts a command-and-control (C2) server, the computer the operators use to send instructions to infected devices. The server answers with a structured list of available add-ons, which HUMAN calls modules. Each record gives the module's name, an internal identifier and version, the piece of code to run, the settings to pass to it, and the places to download it from.
- The download. Modules come mainly from public code repositories, the sites where software developers store and share code. Backup copies sit on a second code platform, on a file server the operators control and, for some modules, on a free mirror service.
- The hidden ad. Ad fraud modules load real video ad software and play ads in a window the owner cannot see.
- The borrowed name and the rented connection. The resulting ad requests name a legitimate CTV app. Separately, proxy modules enroll the box in one of eleven residential proxy networks, so that traffic routed through the box, ad requests included, looks like ordinary home viewing.
The operators choose what each box runs. "That is why 'catalog' is an apt word," the report says. "A single, maintained set of repositories offers proxy enrollment, ad fraud, and loaders side by side, and the operators choose what each device runs."
What the owner sees
"Nothing." That is the report's full answer to the question in its own subheading.
HUMAN describes two of the applications it took apart. The first, a system application called com.android.systemsafe, uses an advertising package called com.speed.adv to load three separate VAST video ad kits. VAST is the industry's standard format for describing a video ad so that any player can show it. HUMAN says this app, and others like it, received CTV video campaigns and played them "in a transparent player positioned outside the visible screen."
The second application presents itself, falsely, as "GoogleAd." It uses the same com.speed.adv family and contains an activity named com.google.AdActivity with two ways of showing an ad. One, called showAd, plays a visible video ad with sound. The other, showHideWindowAd, starts a background process and plays the ad through a hidden view. That application asks a TXCZ web address for its ad settings. In HUMAN's test lab, the server returned no settings, which the researchers attribute to the server recognising the test device or targeting specific devices. The hidden-ad code, HUMAN says, "is present and complete in the application."
The report includes two video clips recorded on an infected box in HUMAN's lab. In the first, the screen looks normal. In the second, the researchers switched off the hiding behaviour, and the ads that had been playing out of sight appeared on screen.
What advertisers saw
The apps whose names appear in the ad requests never ran on the infected boxes, according to HUMAN. Most of the boxes likely did not have them installed at all. Instead, the malicious code contacted the same ad servers the official apps use, in the same way the official apps do, and received similar ad settings and ads in return. Each resulting request named the legitimate app. "The malware also sends hardcoded values that let the ad server attribute the traffic to the threat actors," the report says. In other words, the code told the ad server whose account should be credited.
"To buyers, the traffic looks like premium CTV inventory: a well-known app, a residential IP address, and a full video ad," according to the report.
The industry has two public files meant to catch this kind of impersonation. App-ads.txt lists the companies an app has authorised to sell its ad space, and sellers.json lists the sellers an ad exchange works with. HUMAN says the apps actually sending the BADBOX 3.0 requests "have no business relationship with the apps they claim to be, and they do not appear in those apps' sellers.json or app-ads.txt files, where such files exist." The phrase "where such files exist" matters: a check that relies on these files can only work when the files have been published and when buyers check against them.
HUMAN also tested the legitimate app at the centre of the mid-2026 traffic spike, and the hardware it is designed for, and found no malicious behaviour in either. HUMAN does not name that app, or any of the other apps whose identities were borrowed.
Signals that gave the traffic away
HUMAN lists four patterns that separated the fraudulent requests from real viewing.
Not all of the fraud needed an infected box. HUMAN says it also saw spoofed SSAI traffic sold through many of the same routes. Server-side ad insertion, or SSAI, is the method many streaming services use to stitch ads into the video stream on their own servers before it reaches the viewer. In a spoofed request of this kind, according to HUMAN, "the device does not exist and no ad was ever loaded."
The report sorts the volume into three streams. Requests from malicious apps disguised as system components, which HUMAN assesses as entirely invalid; a second stream carrying the identity of a legitimate CTV channel app; and, making up "the vast majority" of the 40 billion, requests that carried spoofed, misrepresented or fabricated details. HUMAN does not give the split between the three.

A catalog built to survive takedowns
The modules live in public code repositories. A screenshot in the report shows one of them, now taken down, on GitHub, a widely used code-sharing site. Hosting there, HUMAN says, lets the operators hide their deliveries "inside ordinary developer traffic." It also left a trail. Through the repositories' version history, HUMAN's researchers could see the whole collection and how it changed over time.
The older of the two repositories was created in late November 2023, within weeks of HUMAN's first BADBOX disclosure. It received its last update in January 2026, on the same day the second repository was created. "That handoff, with one repository going quiet the same day another begins, likely reflects a planned migration rather than an interruption," according to the report. The newer one has been updated frequently since.
The version counter on the module list had reached the mid-500s by early 2026, which HUMAN reads as sustained development. A server response shown in the report, dated December 30, 2025, lists version 421. Module code names imitate Android's own system software, using a com.google.system naming pattern that blends into a list of running processes. Antivirus detection of the newer modules "was effectively absent at the time of analysis," HUMAN says, consistent with code that had not been seen before.
