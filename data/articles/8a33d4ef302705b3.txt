TITLE: Working with Israël: Who decides if it's okay?
OUTLET: UKrant

Eighteen professors are protesting against the UG’s research on the Dead Sea Scrolls. Not because of the research itself, but because of the lack of transparency surrounding the decision to go ahead with it. ‘They’re acting as if we don’t understand what this is about, but we’re not stupid.’
Sensitive partnerships
Who decides whether collaboration is okay?
It’s not even so much about the research into the Dead Sea Scrolls. Nor is it solely about cooperation with Israel and the genocide taking place in Gaza.
Don’t get them wrong. Marcel Brus, professor emeritus of public international law, and Mariano Méndez, professor of astrophysics, feel that what is happening in Gaza and on the West Bank is appalling. They firmly believe that the UG should not collaborate with Israeli government bodies.
But their main concern is the ease with which the board of directors has brushed aside questions on the matter. ‘They haven’t come up with any decent arguments’, say the two. ‘They’re just saying things without telling anyone which experts have been consulted.’ If the board had done that, they might have been able to accept it, even if they disagree.
‘The board of directors’ response seems naïve, claiming neutrality in an incredibly complex situation’, says Méndez. ‘They’re acting as if we don’t understand what this is all about, but we’re not stupid. We’re employees of the university and we don’t feel properly represented by it.’
Open letter
Méndez and Brus are two of the eighteen professors who wrote an open letter to the board of directors two weeks ago. In it, they called on the UG to halt the ‘Tracing Scribes and Scrolls’ research project, in which UG researchers aim to determine exactly where the Dead Sea Scrolls were written.
They lay claim to everything that is Jewish heritage, but the rest is being erased
Just before the summer, these researchers received an ERC grant of 2.5 million euros for the project. The only problem is that the Dead Sea Scrolls – once discovered at Qumran, then part of Jordan but now part of the occupied West Bank – are located in Israel and are in the hands of the Israel Antiquities Authority (IAA). And the researchers must work together.
That is simply wrong, according to the eighteen signatories, including several professors with extensive expertise in the fields of international law and ethics. Not when the country you’re collaborating with is committing war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide against the Palestinian people. Not when that country is also using the research for its own political ends. ‘Israel’s Minister of Heritage described the scrolls as testimony to “the deep roots of the people of Israel in its land”’, they write.
After all, they argue, research does not take place in a vacuum. ‘The Israeli authorities and a section of the population are laying claim to everything that is Jewish and located in Palestine. In doing so, the rights of the Palestinian people are being completely disregarded. So Israel is claiming rights that it does not grant to Palestinians or others’, says Brus.
Legitimacy
The question of who is the rightful owner of the Dead Sea Scrolls is an immensely complex issue. ‘You could write entire books about it’, says Brus. But whilst the Dead Sea Scrolls are carefully preserved and guarded, Palestinian and Arab heritage is being destroyed on a massive scale. ‘Mosques, manuscripts. They lay claim to everything that constitutes Jewish heritage, but the rest is being erased. There’s a cultural genocide being committed on top of an actual one.’
By cooperating with the Israeli authorities, the UG is legitimising what is happening. Sure, not in legal terms. But there is also such a thing as social responsibility.
However, the board of directors takes a different view, as is evident from the response the professors received on 11 November. The board describes the study of the Dead Sea Scrolls as ‘a legitimate scholarly undertaking in its own right’. However, this research can only be carried out by collaborating with the Israeli IAA, which is in charge of the scrolls. ‘Our cooperation concerns access, conservation, and scientific analysis; it does not constitute recognition by the university of ownership or sovereignty’, the board of directors emphasises.
Our cooperation does not constitute recognition of ownership or sovereignty
Nor does the UG accept any responsibility for anyone using the research findings for political purposes. ‘The university’s position rests on independent scholarship and the open publication of findings’, the board of directors writes.
Finally, the board invokes academic freedom. ‘Good research requires academic freedom: the freedom to choose which topics and questions to investigate; the freedom to work with other researchers at home and abroad; and the freedom to share results and to teach.’
The eighteen professors, however, believe that the UG is taking the easy way out. After all, when it comes to Russia, different standards apply. Moreover, the university is shirking any responsibility for the fact that the Israeli government is benefiting from the collaboration. ‘It’s very easy for Israel to say that there’s nothing wrong if a prestigious university like Groningen is also collaborating with them.’
Other universities
The issue is being felt across the country. Following numerous protests and occupations, most universities have drawn up policies and restricted their collaborations. Utrecht University, for example, is not entering into any new partnerships and has reviewed all existing projects. ‘We do not want certain academic ties and exchange partnerships to contribute to the (continuation of the) cycle of violence’, the university states on its website.
As recently as October, Utrecht University decided to withdraw from a Horizon project involving the Israeli Ministry of Health.
Israel can easily say that nothing is wrong if the UG is working with them
Delft is not entering into any new partnerships and has critically reviewed existing ones. Leiden terminated partnerships and established the Committee on Human Rights/Conflict Zones. That committee issued its first recommendations after just two months. It found that in virtually all cases, universities and research institutions were so closely linked to politics that the suspension of cooperation was necessary.
However, Groningen remains cautious. In May 2024, the UG stated: ‘Since November 2022, research collaborations have been subject to the general, country-neutral knowledge security policy.’
There are no formal collaborations with universities in the Palestinian territories, the university stated. However, there is collaboration with individual academics. ‘In our international activities, we sometimes collaborate with universities in countries with which relations are complicated or sensitive.’
Delaying tactic
However, a Knowledge Security & Sensitive Partnerships Advisory Team was set up in June 2025 – an expansion of the earlier Knowledge Security Advisory Team following the pro-Palestine protests at the Harmonie square. On that occasion, too, Brus, Méndez, and their supporters had written a letter to the board.
The team was a form of compromise, they say. While its members remain anonymous to ensure their safety, there would at least be people to carry out ethical assessments of sensitive collaborations.
‘We agreed to this reluctantly, because we were afraid it was a delaying tactic’, says Brus.
Now he thinks that may well have been the case. As the university council also noted last week, nothing more has been heard of the advisory team since its establishment. A recommendation on the European REACT programme – with which an Israeli professor, who is also a lieutenant-colonel in the Israeli army, was affiliated – is still pending after a year. That collaboration remains suspended.
Seven recommendations
The university’s response makes no mention of the advisory team. Brus suspects that the reasoning appears to have been ‘written by the person calling the shots’. The UG’s spokesperson confirmed the advisory team wasn’t involved in writing the response.
Sometimes, in an institutional context, you simply have to say: let’s not do that
That’s exactly the problem. Not the fact that the board of directors is making decisions with which Brus, Méndez and their supporters disagree. Rather, it is that the UG is hiding behind terms such as ‘academic freedom’ and the fact that it bears no legal responsibility.
But the advisory team is definitely doing what it’s supposed to do, the university’s spokesperson says. Since its inception, it has issued seven recommendations: three to the board of directors, two to faculty boards, and two directly to research projects. The advisory team has also developed a guideline for the realisation of collaboration recommendations. ‘In the future, when we’re implementing new procedures, all recommendations will be put before the board of directors.’
Objective assessment
‘The point is that an objective assessment must be carried out. One in which voices such as mine are taken into account, but also voices from the other side’, says Brus. ‘But it must be an objective assessment based on a framework established in advance, as Utrecht does, for example.’
The same rules and considerations would then apply to this project as to all other projects. ‘It could just as easily have been a human rights project by a colleague whom I hold in high regard and with whom I have worked on a daily basis’, he emphasises. ‘But sometimes, within the institutional context, you simply have to say: let’s not do that.’
And what about the Dead Sea Scrolls? Of course they’re important, Brus emphasises, but research into something so ancient and timeless can also be put on hold. ‘We can wait until we’re no longer in a situation where they might be exploited for political ends. But again: that’s a judgement that an expert committee should make.’
The REACT recommendation has been issued and the board of directors is currently taking it under advisement. The advisory team is also reviewing the partnership with Israel relating to the Dead Sea Scroll research. But its recommendation process will remain confidential in these times of ‘intense social polarisation’. The resulting recommendation will not be made public, either. ‘Recommendations might contain information that could be traced back to individual people, which means privacy concerns prevent us from making them public’, the spokesperson says. The ‘criteria and considerations’ that led to the recommendation will however be made public.
UKrant asked Mladen Popovic, research lead for Tracing Scribes and Scrolls, for his views on how sensitive collaborations are handled at the UG. He referred us to the UG’s press office.
