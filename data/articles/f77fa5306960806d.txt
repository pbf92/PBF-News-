TITLE: Perioperative Dexamethasone Fails to Provide Clear Protection Against Postoperative Cognitive Complications
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis examining perioperative intravenous dexamethasone has revealed that the widely administered anti-inflammatory steroid cannot be confirmed to reliably prevent postoperative delirium or delayed neurocognitive recovery in surgical patients. Published in Frontiers in Medicine by researchers from the Department of Anaesthesiology at Ruijin Hospital and Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, the synthesis evaluated evidence across seven independent randomised controlled trials comprising eight published reports. The pooled analysis revealed that observed reductions in postoperative delirium were statistically uncertain and accompanied by wide prediction intervals encompassing potential benefit as well as potential harm, while evidence regarding delayed neurocognitive recovery exhibited virtually no discernible protective effect. According to formal assessments using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool and the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework, the certainty of evidence for both primary neurocognitive outcomes was rated as very low, demonstrating that current surgical data are insufficient to support routine intravenous dexamethasone administration solely for cognitive prophylaxis.
Perioperative neurocognitive disorders encompass a broad spectrum of acute and lingering mental disturbances following invasive operative interventions, presenting severe risks to elderly and vulnerable patient populations. Under the consensus nomenclature established in 2018, these post-surgical conditions are categorised according to diagnostic features and clinical presentation timelines, distinguishing acute postoperative delirium from delayed neurocognitive recovery occurring within thirty days of surgery, as well as longer-lasting postoperative neurocognitive disorders extending up to twelve months. Delirium represents an acute, fluctuating disturbance in awareness, attention, and cognitive orientation that frequently emerges during the initial days of recovery, heavily influenced by baseline vulnerability and frailty. From a pathophysiological perspective, physical trauma from major surgical procedures triggers profound systemic inflammatory cascades, compromising the structural integrity of the blood-brain barrier and provoking microglial activation in central neural tissues. Because dexamethasone provides potent systemic immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory activity, clinicians long hypothesised that perioperative administration might shield vulnerable brain tissue from inflammatory stress; nevertheless, prior individual trials and pooled reviews yielded contradictory results due to inconsistent diagnostic standards, heterogeneous populations, and mixed steroid regimens.
To rigorously resolve these longstanding clinical uncertainties, the investigative team searched five major electronic research databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Ovid MEDLINE, identifying studies published through early September 2026. Eligible investigations were strictly restricted to randomised controlled trials that enrolled adult surgical patients undergoing any operative procedure, compared perioperative intravenous dexamethasone against placebo or standard care, and recorded at least one formally structured neurocognitive outcome. Unlike earlier systematic overviews that combined diverse synthetic glucocorticoids such as hydrocortisone and methylprednisolone, this investigation maintained complete pharmacological specificity by isolating intravenous dexamethasone. Furthermore, the investigators eliminated confounding overlap by identifying shared patient cohorts across multiple publications, linking substudies to parent investigations. In particular, the massive DExamethasone for Cardiac Surgery trial was accurately linked so that its substudy by Sauër and colleagues provided delirium outcomes, while a companion substudy by Ottens and colleagues provided structured neuropsychological evaluations mapping to the delayed neurocognitive recovery interval, avoiding double-counting patients within individual quantitative syntheses. Overall, seven parent randomised trials involving procedures ranging from cardiac bypass to microvascular decompression and geriatric hip fracture repair satisfied all eligibility criteria.
The meta-analysis for postoperative delirium incorporated four independent randomised trials encompassing 1,069 unique surgical participants, comparing 530 patients assigned to intravenous dexamethasone against 539 controls receiving placebo or standard management. Delirium developed in 71 dexamethasone recipients, corresponding to an absolute event rate of 13.4 percent, compared with 98 control recipients, who experienced an event rate of 18.2 percent. Synthesis using a random-effects Mantel-Haenszel model produced a pooled risk ratio of 0.62 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 0.37 to 1.03. While the point estimate favoured dexamethasone, the confidence boundary crossed the null threshold of 1.00, demonstrating that the observed reduction failed to reach statistical significance. More critically, an exploratory calculation of the 95 percent prediction interval yielded an exceptionally wide range from 0.09 to 4.26, indicating that the true effect in a future prospective trial could span from profound protective efficacy to substantial clinical harm. When the investigators examined delirium occurrences broken down across specific postoperative periods, day-by-day estimates also demonstrated no definitive benefit, yielding risk ratios of 0.91 on postoperative day one, 0.73 on day two, and 0.74 on day three, with confidence intervals spanning no effect across each interval.
For the co-primary outcome of delayed neurocognitive recovery, the synthesis analysed four independent trials contributing six separate comparisons and encompassing 1,533 unique participants. The resulting pooled random-effects risk ratio was 0.95 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.65 to 1.41, demonstrating an effect essentially centered on the null value alongside notable between-study statistical heterogeneity characterized by an I-squared value of 64 percent. Individual study estimates varied substantially in both magnitude and direction across different clinical contexts. For example, lower rates of cognitive decline were observed among dexamethasone recipients in smaller cardiac surgery trials and specific strata of non-cardiac surgery, whereas the preplanned substudy of the large cardiac surgical cohort by Ottens reported higher rates of cognitive dysfunction in dexamethasone recipients at one month. An exploratory parent-trial random-effects model consolidating these comparisons into four independent study estimates yielded a pooled risk ratio of 0.92 with a confidence interval of 0.56 to 1.54 and an even wider prediction interval ranging from 0.11 to 8.05. Furthermore, exploratory subgroup comparisons evaluating cardiac versus non-cardiac surgical procedures revealed no statistically significant interaction, generating imprecise risk ratios of 0.90 in cardiac operations and 1.01 in non-cardiac interventions.
Methodological quality assessments conducted under the Cochrane RoB 2 framework highlighted persistent design vulnerabilities that contributed directly to the very low certainty rating assigned to both primary outcomes under GRADE. For postoperative delirium, only two trials, conducted by Sauër and Kluger, achieved an overall low risk of bias, whereas an investigation by Huang raised some concerns due to retrospective trial registration and exploratory outcome reporting. A cardiac trial by Mardani was judged to possess a high overall risk of bias due to substantial and imbalanced post-randomisation exclusions, losing twelve dexamethasone participants and five control participants from a modest sample of 110 patients. When the researchers performed sensitivity analyses excluding the high-risk Mardani study, the pooled risk ratio for delirium shifted closer to the null, moving to 0.70 with a confidence interval from 0.41 to 1.17. For delayed neurocognitive recovery, all four contributing studies raised some methodological concerns, primarily stemming from lack of prospective analysis verification or missing data handling. In Valentin’s factorial trial, aggregate event numbers had to be mathematically reconstructed from published stratum percentages, while trial-defined cognitive decline across older studies relied on historic definitions of cognitive dysfunction that failed to conform fully to contemporary neurocognitive diagnostic standards.
Crucially, the systematic review identified a paradoxical dosing signal within individual trial data that cautions against assuming larger corticosteroid doses confer neurocognitive protection. In a trial by Fang and colleagues evaluating younger adult patients undergoing microvascular decompression surgery, investigators tested two distinct weight-based doses against a divided control group. While patients receiving a lower dose of 0.1 milligrams per kilogram experienced an early cognitive decline rate of 20.6 percent compared with 22.3 percent in controls, participants allocated to a higher dose of 0.2 milligrams per kilogram exhibited a significantly elevated cognitive dysfunction rate of 31.4 percent, generating a statistically significant risk ratio of 1.43 relative to controls. Sensitivity analyses exploring assessment timing further indicated that while five early comparisons conducted within the first postoperative week yielded a pooled risk ratio of 0.85, the solitary trial evaluating patients at thirty days post-surgery demonstrated a risk ratio of 1.87, highlighting that acute recovery signals must not be conflated with lasting cognitive stability.
Beyond neurocognitive endpoints, the review compiled secondary clinical recovery metrics, including duration of mechanical ventilation, hospital stay, and intensive care unit length of stay. Meta-analyses of continuous data from two cardiac surgery trials comprising 254 participants suggested modest recovery advantages for dexamethasone, showing a reduction in extubation time of 1.43 hours, a shorter hospital stay of 0.58 days, and a decrease in intensive care unit duration of 13.82 hours. However, the authors explicitly emphasized that these secondary recovery findings remain strictly exploratory and highly susceptible to bias. Nearly the entirety of the statistical weight driving the extubation and hospital duration syntheses was derived from the single cardiac study by Mardani, which had already demonstrated substantial methodological compromise, imbalanced patient losses, and high risk of bias. Conversely, median-based reporting from the far larger Sauër trial documented identical median extubation times of eight hours in both treatment groups, alongside virtually indistinguishable median intensive care unit stays of 23 hours with dexamethasone and 22 hours with placebo. Similarly, measures of delirium duration and severity using Memorial Delirium Assessment Scale scores yielded descriptive, unadjusted case-only observations that cannot be interpreted as causal proof of symptom reduction.
Any theoretical neuroprotective benefit of perioperative dexamethasone must also be critically weighed against its direct metabolic and physiological adverse effects, especially perioperative hyperglycemia. While pooled analysis of trial-defined categorical hyperglycemia across 239 participants showed an imprecise trend toward increased risk, with 27 events among 120 dexamethasone recipients compared to 17 events among 119 controls yielding a risk ratio of 1.58, continuous biochemical monitoring consistently confirmed significantly elevated blood glucose concentrations in steroid-treated patients.
