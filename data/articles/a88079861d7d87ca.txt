Colorectal Cancer Rates Rise Again in U.S.
A new study finds the overall U.S. colorectal cancer rate rose in 2023, the first increase in more than 20 years.
Updated on Nov. 27, 2026 in Cancer

A study published October 27 in NEJM Evidence found the U.S. overall age-adjusted colorectal cancer rate rose to 38.3 cases per 100,000 people in 2023, up from 36.7 in 2022. The increase was the first rise in the overall rate in more than 20 years.
Why it matters
Colorectal cancer is the second leading cause of cancer deaths overall in the U.S. and ranks first among adults younger than 50. Rates are climbing in nearly every age group under 70, which the researchers say is a trend that warrants continued attention to screening and risk.
The overall age-adjusted rate climbed from 36.7 to 38.3 cases per 100,000 people between 2022 and 2023, while rates for ages 45 to 49 rose about 12.76% per year from 2019 to 2023. The authors' explanation for the sharpest increase among that age group is not confirmed by the data alone.
The players
National Cancer Institute
The National Cancer Institute is the U.S. federal agency that leads cancer research and runs the SEER Program, which tracks cancer incidence and survival across the country.
University of Chicago
The University of Chicago is a private research university whose researchers led the study, which was published in NEJM Evidence.
The details
Researchers from the University of Chicago, UCLA, Emory University and UC San Diego used data from the National Cancer Institute's SEER Program. The authors attribute the sharpest increase among ages 45 to 49 to expanded screening after national recommendations lowered the start age from 50 to 45.
Timeline
- 2000: Overall age-adjusted colorectal cancer rate was 55.2 per 100,000.
- 2019 to 2023: Rates for ages 45 to 49 rose about 12.76% per year.
- 2022: Overall age-adjusted rate fell to 36.7 per 100,000.
- 2023: Overall rate rose to 38.3 per 100,000, the first increase in more than 20 years.
- October 27, 2026: Study published in NEJM Evidence.
Health Landscape
The rise comes after nearly two decades of decline in the overall U.S. rate, and the sharpest increase among ages 45 to 49 follows national recommendations that lowered the screening start age from 50 to 45. Researchers are watching whether the upward trend in younger adults reflects detection of existing cancers or a true increase in disease.
Adults nearing the recommended screening age should discuss colorectal cancer screening with their doctor, since the national start age is now 45 for average-risk adults. Screening is recommended routinely through age 75 for average-risk adults, and symptoms such as persistent changes in bowel habits or rectal bleeding warrant prompt medical evaluation.
The takeaway
Colorectal cancer is increasingly affecting younger adults, so checking screening eligibility and paying attention to new digestive symptoms can matter at any age. Earlier detection through screening remains one of the clearest ways individuals can act on this trend.
