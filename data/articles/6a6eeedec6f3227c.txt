TITLE: Beetroot Nitrate Boosts Resistance Repetition Capacity but Not Velocity or Strength
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and three-level meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Nutrition has quantified the ergogenic effects of beetroot-derived nitrate across multiple distinct dimensions of resistance exercise performance in healthy adults. Synthesizing twenty-one randomized crossover trials that encompassed twenty-eight separate report units and two hundred seventy-one participants, the researchers found that dietary nitrate supplementation significantly improved repetition capacity compared with placebo or control conditions. However, the pooled analyses revealed no clear ergogenic benefits for maximal muscular strength, mean movement velocity, or peak movement velocity. These findings indicate that the functional advantages conferred by beetroot-derived nitrate are specifically linked to the maintenance of repeated muscular contractions rather than the augmentation of instantaneous force production or rapid movement velocity during isolated resistance tasks. The synthesized evidence establishes an average benefit for total repetition volume, yet the authors emphasized that substantial between-study heterogeneity and low certainty of evidence warrant measured interpretation regarding its universal efficacy across varied athletic environments.
The biological rationale for investigating beetroot-derived nitrate in sports nutrition centers on the enterosalivary nitrate-nitrite-nitric oxide pathway. In skeletal muscle tissue, nitric oxide modulates physiological processes including local muscular blood flow, microvascular perfusion, mitochondrial respiration efficiency, sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium handling, and the energetic cost of force development. Prior resistance exercise investigations had yielded conflicting observations regarding whether these cellular alterations translate into measurable performance enhancements. While some studies documented improvements in muscular endurance and movement speed, others reported negligible changes during strength assessments, suggesting that the ergogenic efficacy of nitrate is highly dependent upon the physiological demands of the specific exercise protocol.
To resolve these discrepancies, the review team established a rigorous evaluative scope aimed at discerning how acute and short-term beetroot-derived nitrate supplementation influences independent performance domains while examining whether relative resistance load modifies repetition outcomes. Previous quantitative reviews had suggested that dietary nitrate preferentially aids sustained muscular efforts over brief maximal outputs. For example, earlier meta-analyses reported trivial influences on maximal voluntary strength alongside small improvements in muscular endurance. Similarly, investigations in resistance-trained men documented enhancements in repetitions completed to failure and mean velocity during bench press and back squat exercises, but failed to identify benefits for peak power or peak velocity. However, those previous syntheses frequently pooled disparate resistance loads or evaluated load through broad groupings, leaving unresolved whether the repetition benefit remains uniform across different mechanical intensities or follows a continuous load-response trajectory.
Conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and prospectively registered in PROSPERO under registration identifier CRD420261405915, the study utilized an exhaustive literature search strategy. Two researchers independently queried six major electronic databases—PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, EBSCOhost, and the Cochrane Library—from database inception through May 18, 2026. The search terms coupled descriptors for beetroot products and dietary nitrate with terms representing resistance exercise and resistance training. From six hundred sixty-three initially identified bibliographic records, two hundred seventy-nine duplicate entries were verified and removed. Independent screening of three hundred eighty-four titles and abstracts resulted in the exclusion of three hundred twenty-two citations, leaving sixty-two full-text publications for detailed eligibility appraisal. Applying strict PICOS criteria, forty-two reports were excluded due to irrelevant outcomes, non-eligible interventions, or absence of peer-reviewed full text, while reference screening supplied one eligible investigation, culminating in twenty-one crossover studies for meta-analytic synthesis.
The included twenty-eight report units encompassed a cumulative sample of two hundred seventy-one healthy adult participants aged eighteen years and older. Individual cohort sample sizes were modest, ranging from eight to nineteen participants, with mean study ages spanning from approximately twenty to sixty-nine years. Study cohorts exhibited marked diversity in physical conditioning, incorporating resistance-trained individuals, recreationally active participants, and healthy untrained volunteers. However, participant demographics were heavily skewed toward young males; only two cohorts enrolled women exclusively, two investigations included mixed-sex samples, and an older adult population was represented in just one cohort.
Methodologically, the authors addressed a critical limitation of previous research by adopting a three-level random-effects meta-analytic framework estimated via restricted maximum likelihood. Because individual crossover trials frequently evaluated multiple exercise loads, sets, or movement velocities within identical participant groups, standard meta-analytic models risk violating statistical independence assumptions. The three-level model accounted for dependency among multiple effect estimates contributed by the same study cohorts, while CR2 cluster-robust inference with Satterthwaite-adjusted degrees of freedom was applied at the parent-cohort level. Effect sizes were computed as Hedges’ g using condition means and pooled raw-score standard deviations, incorporating within-participant correlation adjustments with a default baseline correlation of r = 0.50. Statistical uncertainty and dispersion were characterized using 95% confidence intervals, tau-squared, I-squared statistics, and 95% prediction intervals to quantify expected outcome variation in comparable future settings.
The primary synthesis revealed a statistically significant pooled improvement in repetition capacity favoring beetroot-derived nitrate supplementation, yielding a Hedges’ g of 0.642 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.365 to 0.918 and a significance value of p < 0.001. Under parent-cohort cluster-robust CR2 inference, this positive effect remained evident, presenting a 95% confidence interval of 0.314 to 0.970 and p = 0.002 across 7.72 degrees of freedom. In stark contrast, maximal muscular strength demonstrated no clear pooled benefit, with a negligible Hedges’ g of 0.069, a 95% confidence interval spanning -0.021 to 0.159, and p = 0.135. Similarly, movement speed remained largely unaffected; mean velocity yielded a pooled effect of g = 0.047 with a 95% confidence interval of -0.045 to 0.138 (p = 0.318), while peak velocity generated an estimate of g = 0.176 with a 95% confidence interval of -0.162 to 0.515 (p = 0.308). Peak velocity exhibited substantial heterogeneity with an I-squared of 75.7% and a wide 95% prediction interval extending from -0.807 to 1.159.
Categorizing supplementation into acute single-dose versus multi-day administration revealed no significant moderating influence on maximal strength (QM = 0.35, p = 0.554; CR2 p = 0.207) or repetition capacity (QM = 0.49, p = 0.483; CR2 p = 0.591), while velocity comparisons were constrained by sparse representation. Exercise modality produced no detectable differences for strength or velocity; an initial model-based difference for repetition capacity (QM = 3.88, p = 0.049) was not confirmed under cluster-robust adjustment (p = 0.231). Grouping resistance load into categorical tiers—low at 60% or less of one-repetition maximum, moderate between 61% and 79%, and high at 80% or greater—showed no categorical moderation for repetition capacity (QM = 3.41, p = 0.182; CR2 p = 0.430) or peak velocity (QM = 0.46, p = 0.793; CR2 p = 0.837). Set structure likewise displayed no definitive relationship with repetition outcomes.
Recognizing that broad categorical grouping can mask continuous patterns, the researchers conducted an exploratory continuous meta-regression using thirty-three effect estimates from nine report units across eight parent-cohort clusters spanning 60% to 80% of one-repetition maximum. Employing the log ratio of means, an exploratory quadratic model indicated a nonlinear load-response association (quadratic coefficient beta-2 = 0.080, 95% confidence interval 0.006 to 0.153, p = 0.033), explaining approximately 28.4% of between-effect variance. Point estimates indicated relative repetition gains of 16.2% at 60% 1RM (95% CI 8.5% to 24.4%), 12.6% at 70% 1RM (95% CI 4.1% to 21.7%), and 28.1% at 80% 1RM (95% CI 16.9% to 40.3%). Furthermore, repetition gains at 80% 1RM exceeded the pooled average across 60% to 79% 1RM by 11.5% (p = 0.009). However, this apparent nonlinear pattern was not confirmed under CR2 cluster-robust inference (p = 0.125, 95% CI -0.049 to 0.209), and high-load contrasts similarly lost statistical significance under robust adjustments, indicating that the observed curvature was statistically fragile.
Methodological quality assessments and sensitivity analyses provided essential context regarding evidence certainty and model stability. Evaluating study quality via the crossover-specific Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool showed that only one trial (4.7%) had a low risk of bias, seventeen trials (81.0%) exhibited some concerns, and three trials (14.3%) demonstrated a high risk of bias. Primary vulnerabilities stemmed from inadequate reporting of randomization sequence generation, potential period and carryover effects, insufficient washout documentation, and missing prespecified statistical protocols. GRADE evaluations indicated moderate certainty for maximal strength, low certainty for mean velocity and repetition capacity, and very low certainty for peak velocity. Sensitivity analyses varying within-participant correlation coefficients between r = 0.30 and r = 0.70 confirmed stability for the primary repetition effect (g = 0.629 to 0.655). Crucially, the apparent nonlinear load response proved highly vulnerable to the analytical handling of potentially overlapping report cohorts from Ammar and Salem; clustering these reports diminished precision, while entirely excluding them eliminated the nonlinear association (beta-2 = -0.004, p = 0.960).
From a mechanistic standpoint, the selective enhancement of repetition capacity aligns with the physiological conditions governing nitrate reduction in contracting skeletal muscle. Nonetheless, the authors underscored that these physiological mechanisms remain theoretical within the scope of meta-analytic data and require direct empirical verification. Given the low certainty of the repetition evidence, the prediction interval spanning zero (-0.217 to 1.501), and the lack of robust confirmation for the load curve, beetroot-derived nitrate cannot currently support load-specific supplementation recommendations in resistance training practice.
