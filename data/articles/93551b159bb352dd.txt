TITLE: Scoping Review Finds Upper-Lip Entrapment From Anterior Prostheses Remains Clinically Unvalidated
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A scoping review published in The Saudi Dental Journal reveals that while anterior tooth position and prosthetic geometry measurably influence upper-lip position and soft-tissue displacement, direct scientific evidence demonstrating mechanical interference, restricted passage, or functional entrapment during lip movement remains absent. Conducted by researchers Osama Hajeer and Tim Joda, the systematic investigation mapped literature connecting perioral soft-tissue dynamics with anterior dental contours, emergence profiles, and restorative morphology. Their analysis establishes that Upper-Lip Functional Entrapment, discussed as a potential restorative complication, currently exists only as a provisional and unvalidated clinical hypothesis rather than an established medical condition. By synthesizing evidence across dental disciplines, the authors sought to distinguish verified three-dimensional soft-tissue displacement from speculative claims of physical interference during facial animation, smiling, and speech. Their findings emphasize that while dental restorations alter facial soft-tissue support, researchers and clinicians currently lack empirical proof that anterior prosthetic shapes physically trap or hinder the physiological passage of the human upper lip.
In restorative dentistry and prosthodontics, successful esthetic rehabilitation of the anterior dentition requires the harmonious integration of tooth morphology, phonetics, and dynamic perioral soft tissues. Historical clinical principles have emphasized the importance of incisal-edge positioning, smile-arc harmony, and phonetic evaluation during treatment planning, while contemporary digital workflows increasingly incorporate dynamic facial relationships into customized smile designs. Throughout daily activities such as speaking, smiling, and laughing, the upper lip glides across the labial surfaces of the maxillary anterior teeth, and its resting posture and dynamic trajectory are visibly influenced by underlying dental contours. Recent advances in three-dimensional imaging have confirmed that simulated incisor protrusion and increased labial prosthetic thickness generate measurable displacement of the upper lip. However, as the authors identified in their evaluation, existing literature has almost exclusively evaluated static positional changes and soft-tissue support rather than dynamic mechanical interactions or physical resistance occurring during active functional lip movement.
This critical distinction between static anatomical displacement and dynamic functional impedance represents a major unaddressed knowledge gap in restorative prosthodontics. Crucially, the scientific literature lacks established clearance thresholds between the lip and prosthesis, objective measurements of restricted lip passage, or validated documentation of clinically significant mechanical entrapment. To establish a rigorous scientific foundation for evaluating these phenomena, Hajeer and Joda proposed the concept of the labial functional envelope, defining it as the spatial pathway required for physiological upper-lip movement relative to the maxillary anterior teeth. Within this theoretical paradigm, Upper-Lip Functional Entrapment was formally introduced as a provisional, testable biomechanical construct describing hypothesized spatial interference between anterior dental geometry and upper-lip transit, specifically designed to stimulate structured clinical investigation.
To systematically map the evidence surrounding lip dynamics and anterior dental morphology, the investigators designed a scoping review adhering to Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines. A scoping review format was selected to accommodate the heterogeneous evidence base spanning diverse dental disciplines, identify definitive gaps in direct research, and establish clear priorities for future clinical trials. In accordance with open-science principles, the review protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework. The researchers framed their search and eligibility criteria around the Population–Concept–Context structure, targeting human participants and digital anatomical models evaluating relationships between the upper lip and maxillary anterior dentition. The core concepts encompassed incisal-edge position, incisal-third morphology, facial contour, emergence profile, smile dynamics, phonetic relationships, and dynamic upper-lip movement across clinical, experimental, observational, morphometric, and three-dimensional motion-analysis study designs.
The literature search strategy was executed across five major electronic bibliographic databases from their inception dates through October 2026: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. The search algorithms incorporated comprehensive combinations of controlled medical subject headings and free-text terms addressing lip dynamics, soft-tissue displacement, phonetic interactions, smile arcs, anterior restorative geometry, emergence profile design, and potential mechanical lip–prosthesis interference. Supplemented by hand-searching citation lists and conducting citation tracking, the preliminary search retrieved 698 bibliographic records. Following the removal of 204 duplicate citations, two reviewers independently conducted title and abstract screening of the remaining 494 records. This screening phase identified 49 full-text reports for detailed eligibility assessment, from which 32 publications were excluded for failing to meet predefined criteria—such as case reports, uninformative conference abstracts, isolated posterior dental studies, or lack of relevant lip-to-tooth relationships. Ultimately, 14 primary sources met all inclusion criteria and were synthesized in the final qualitative evidence map.
Methodological appraisal of the included literature was conducted using design-appropriate Joanna Briggs Institute critical-appraisal tools, focusing on characterizing methodological limitations and directness of evidence rather than generating arbitrary quantitative quality scores. The synthesized evidence base spanned prosthodontics, orthodontics, phonetics, esthetic dentistry, and three-dimensional facial analysis, which the investigators organized into five distinct thematic domains. The first domain evaluated phonetic incisal-edge relationships, where classic foundational studies established the vital role of maxillary incisal positioning in clear phonation and lip support during speech sounds. However, these traditional phonetic investigations focused primarily on sound quality and static acoustic boundaries rather than quantifying dynamic clearance or testing whether prosthetic thickness physically impedes functional lip transit. The second domain addressed smile-arc and lip-curvature relationships, where multiple studies confirmed that anterior tooth display and dental alignment correlate with perceived esthetic harmony, yet failed to assess functional lip–prosthesis contact mechanics, movement resistance, or kinematic passage restriction during active smiling.
The third evidence domain focused on anterior contour and facial geometry, where morphometric investigations and controlled experimental trials evaluated how dental projection alters surrounding soft tissues. Nonetheless, the scoping review stressed that demonstrating geometry-dependent displacement is fundamentally distinct from proving functional interference. While anterior dental projection pushes the resting lip forward, this positional displacement does not demonstrate that the dynamic lip catches, snags, or experiences restricted mobility when moving over the incisal third of a prosthetic restoration during daily expressive or communicative tasks.
The fourth and fifth domains examined emergence profiles and dynamic lip and smile motion, respectively. Despite the technological sophistication of these dynamic studies, none measured the minimum functional clearance between the moving lip and the restorative surface, nor did they quantify contact duration, movement impedance, or mechanical entrapment. Across the entire mapped literature, the authors found no standardized measurement protocols, no clinically meaningful clearance thresholds, and no validated diagnostic criteria capable of confirming that anterior dental restorations physically restrict upper-lip transit in human patients.
Faced with this complete absence of direct empirical evidence, Hajeer and Joda developed a conceptual framework to transform Upper-Lip Functional Entrapment from an unverified clinical assumption into a rigorous, testable research agenda. Their proposed model links three interdependent categories of candidate variables: prosthetic geometry, dynamic lip behavior, and functional patient outcomes. Prosthetic geometric variables include incisal-third projection, labial curvature, surface contour, and tooth inclination. Dynamic kinematic variables comprise three-dimensional lip trajectories, minimum functional lip–prosthesis clearance, contact duration, and movement velocity across different functional phases. Functional outcomes encompass phonetic performance metrics and validated patient-reported outcome measures evaluating subjective comfort and perceived mobility restriction. The authors explicitly emphasized that their schematic models and candidate variables represent hypothesis-generating targets intended to guide prospective clinical investigations, rather than validated clinical screening tools, diagnostic algorithms, or prescriptive guidelines for modifying tooth preparation or prosthetic design in everyday clinical practice.
In their concluding synthesis, the researchers reaffirmed that while anterior tooth position and prosthetic geometry unquestionably influence upper-lip position and soft-tissue displacement, Upper-Lip Functional Entrapment remains an unproven hypothesis requiring rigorous clinical validation. The scoping review identified critical limitations in current scientific knowledge, including substantial methodological heterogeneity among existing studies, diverse measurement techniques, and an overreliance on static positional assessments rather than dynamic functional analyses, which precluded formal meta-analysis or certainty ratings. Consequently, neither the existence, prevalence, biological causation, nor clinical relevance of functional entrapment can be asserted based on current scientific data. The authors stressed that before clinicians attempt to diagnose functional entrapment or alter restorative guidelines, future prospective studies must utilize standardized three-dimensional motion tracking, controlled geometric alterations of prosthetic contours, objective contact-sensing technologies, and validated patient outcome instruments. Until such empirical validation is achieved, functional entrapment should be recognized strictly as a theoretical biomechanical concept awaiting scientific proof.
