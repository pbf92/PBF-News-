TITLE: Half the homes Samba and OpenX reached online missed the TV ads
OUTLET: PPC Land

Media intelligence company Samba and supply-side platform OpenX published an analysis of 11 advertising campaigns run through their partnership, in an announcement datelined San Francisco on November 21, 2026 and distributed on November 23. On average, 50 percent of the households reached through digital audiences built with Samba data and bought through OpenX were not reached by the same brands' linear TV campaigns, according to the companies. In some campaigns, the share of digital impressions going to homes untouched by linear TV reached 90 percent.
In Short
Samba and OpenX studied 11 campaigns in which brands ran linear TV alongside digital ads targeted with Samba's TV viewing data, and found that, on average, half of the homes reached online had not been reached by the TV ads. The finding matters to TV buyers because the third of households that watch the least television received just 3 percent of linear TV impressions in those campaigns, against nearly 30 percent of the digital impressions. What changes is availability: Samba's audiences, including light TV viewers and cord-cutters, can now be bought in the US through OpenX's OpenXSelect platform, though the companies have not released spend, dates, prices or a comparison against ordinary digital buying.
What the 11 campaigns showed
The central claim of the announcement is narrow and specific. Brands that extended their linear TV campaigns with Samba audiences on CTV, display and online video, bought through OpenXSelect, reached households their TV schedules did not.
That is a claim about incremental reach: the audience a channel adds that no other channel in the same plan already reached. It is the number media planners use to decide whether a second channel extends a campaign or simply repeats it.
According to the release, the two companies "analyzed 11 marketing campaigns conducted through their partnership, and the joint analysis revealed that, on average, 50 percent of the digital audiences reached were not reached on linear TV, allowing brands to complement their TV campaigns by reaching digital-only audiences." The same paragraph adds that "in some campaigns, as much as 90 percent of the digital impressions were to audiences not reached by linear TV."
Those two figures use different units. The 50 percent average is expressed in households. The 90 percent ceiling is expressed in digital impressions. The release does not give the household figure for the campaign that reached 90 percent, nor the lowest result among the 11.
The companies grouped their findings under four headings: half the digital audience was not exposed to the TV campaigns, the biggest gains came from light TV viewers, targeted audiences allowed better frequency monitoring, and digital balanced delivery across markets. The release then condenses these into "four benefits of supplementing linear TV with curated digital audiences: incremental reach, access to light TV viewers, frequency control, and geographic balance."
The light-viewer numbers
The most specific finding concerns the households that watch the least television. The analysis singles out the third of households that watched the least. According to the release, "the third of households that watched the least amount of television account for just 3 percent of impressions for linear TV campaigns." The same households "accounted for nearly 30 percent of the digital impressions a 9.6 times increase in the share of impressions."
The arithmetic holds together. Three percent multiplied by 9.6 gives 28.8 percent, which matches "nearly 30 percent" (PPC Land calculation). Set against an even split, the gap is wider still. A group making up one third of households would receive about 33.3 percent of impressions if exposure were spread evenly. On linear TV, the lightest-viewing third received roughly one eleventh of that even share; on digital, it received about 86 percent of it (PPC Land calculations based on the release's figures).
Phillip Keefe, Samba's VP of Insights and Innovation, put the light-viewer finding at the centre of the announcement. "Linear TV remains effective at building awareness at scale, but it still misses a significant portion of consumers. The third of households that watch the least TV received just 3 percent of linear impressions. Audiences curated by Samba delivered nearly ten times the share of impressions to those households, giving brands a way to reach the people their TV buys leave out."
Frequency and geography
The other two findings come without figures for the digital side. On linear TV, according to the release, "heavy TV viewers were exposed to the same ads at 5.5 times the rate of light viewers." On digital, "ad frequency stayed consistent across both groups." No average frequency is given for either channel or either group.
That pattern is built into how linear TV is sold. A spot airs to everyone watching a channel at a given time, so the households that watch most see it most. Programmatic buying can apply frequency capping, a limit on how often one person or household receives the same ad in a set window, and can select which households receive impressions in the first place. The release attributes the digital result to targeting: "Targeting audiences gave marketers more control over frequency, helping them avoid saturating audiences and reduce wasted spend." No figure for the spend saved is given.
On geography, the release says "geographic distribution is hard to control with TV alone" and that targeted audiences "were more evenly distributed across geographic regions, enabling brands to deliver impressions in areas where TV exposure was low." Digital, it adds, "reached markets with lower TV exposure without oversaturating markets with high TV exposure." Neither the regions nor the size of the imbalance are named.
How the offer works
According to Samba and OpenX, the product described in the release runs in five steps.
- Audiences. Samba builds audiences from its TV viewing data. They are offered "as always-on or custom segments" and include "viewing behavior segments, such as light TV viewers and cord-cutters."
- Activation. Advertisers activate the segments through OpenXSelect, which the release describes as "OpenX's curation and supply-side targeting platform." The deals combine Samba audiences with OpenX supply; the release calls them "curated deals."
- Selection before the auction. The audience is applied on the sell side. Erika Loberg, VP of CTV and Enterprise Solutions at OpenX, described the sequence: "Samba's TV viewing data tells you which households a TV plan isn't reaching. Applying it to the supply side before the auction means inventory is selected for those households from the start, so brands can reach light TV viewers efficiently and maintain consistent frequency across audiences. That extends a linear campaign without duplicating it."
- Household matching. OpenX's ID graph, which the release says has "an addressable audience of 275M+," ties impressions "across devices and channels back to the household so digital and linear could be measured side by side."
- Measurement. Buyers "can then use Samba True Reach and Frequency to measure incremental reach on their own campaigns."
The offering is available in the US across CTV, online video and display, according to the release.
Curation in this sense means packaging inventory and audience data on the supply side into deals that a buyer then activates in its own buying platform. In this arrangement, the data comes from Samba, the inventory from publishers selling through OpenX, and the packaging from OpenXSelect.
How the measurement was done
Samba measured each of the 11 campaigns with its own True Reach and Frequency product. According to the release, "the analysis compared household-level exposure across linear TV and high-quality digital media across CTV, display, and online video." Samba's product page describes True Reach and Frequency as a single, deduplicated view of reach and frequency across linear TV, CTV and digital devices, and lists verification of CTV and digital incremental reach over linear-only campaigns among its uses.
The household link comes from OpenX. Its identity graph, a database that records which devices and identifiers belong to the same home, is what allowed linear and digital exposure to be counted together. The audience size OpenX cites for its graph and platform has been stated in different units over the past 16 months.
The figures come from OpenX's launch of OpenXSelect for agencies, OpenX and TVision's attention targeting for CTV buyers, the OpenXSelect product page and the release. The units shift between users, devices, monthly unique users, CTV users and an undefined addressable audience, so the figures cannot be compared directly. The release also does not say which signals the graph uses to tie a device to a household. That question has weight in CTV. A study by the Coalition for Innovative Media Measurement and Go Addressable, analysed by Truthset, found that IP-to-postal linkages achieve 13% accuracy on average, and in July 2026 an Adstra study found that only 23% of residential IP addresses reached their intended geographic target.
Reading the 50 percent
How much of the 50 percent is a product of Samba's data, and how much would any digital buy have produced? The release does not say. It reports no control group, such as a comparable digital campaign bought without Samba audiences, against which the incremental share could be judged.
The viewing backdrop matters here. In Nielsen's August 2026 Gauge, streaming accounted for 49.6 percent of US TV time, cable 18.9 percent and broadcast 17.5 percent. Cable and broadcast together came to 36.4 percent (PPC Land calculation). With linear's share of viewing at that level, a CTV or online video campaign is likely to land partly in homes that a linear schedule reaches lightly or not at all, whatever data is used to target it. The release itself notes that "as TV viewers continue to shift away from linear TV, it is increasingly difficult for brands to identify and reach these consumers."
The measurement and the product also share an owner. Samba supplies the audiences, sells the measurement and ran the analysis; OpenX supplied the identity graph used to match exposure. The release names no independent verifier.
None of this makes the light-viewer figures wrong. It defines what they are: results from the two companies' own campaigns, measured with the seller's own tool, with no disclosed comparison.
OpenX and the push into supply-side targeting
For OpenX, the announcement extends a strategy it has been building since mid-2025. The company made OpenXSelect generally available on July 16, 2025 as a curation and supply-side targeting platform for brands and agencies. In March 2026 it added OpenX Attention Targeting, built on TVision's attention models and available only through OpenXSelect.
OpenX's own data has charted the growth of the category. Its trend report in October put supply-side targeting at 37% of spend in Q4 2025, up from 11% in Q4 2023. The full report, which PPC Land examined on November 1, showed supply-side targeting reaching 51% of OpenX CTV spend in 2025, up from 9% in 2022, while third-party audience data fell from 78% of supply-side targeting spend in 2022 to roughly 40% in 2025. Samba's segments are third-party data in that framework: audience data from a provider that is neither the buyer nor the publisher.
The partnership also arrives less than three weeks after OpenX named Robert Horler its third chief executive since January, following the death of John Gentry and the departure of Matt Sattel.
