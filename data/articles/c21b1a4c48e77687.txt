TITLE: Is mental illness universal?
OUTLET: Aeon

On a November day in 1994, a 14-year-old girl in Hong Kong named Charlene Hsu Chi-Ying collapsed on a busy sidewalk, in broad daylight, in her school uniform. She had starved herself to death. Charlene had never been diagnosed with anorexia, not because her self-starvation went unnoticed, but because the diagnosis, in its Western form, was mostly unknown in the East. Her death changed that overnight. Splashed across the front pages were stark headlines such as ‘Girl Who Died In Street Was A Walking Skeleton’.
Local journalists scrambled for an explanation of this tragedy, and quickly found a template ready-made: a Western account of what anorexia was, what it felt like, who got it, what caused it. The ideas spread rapidly – particularly that anorexia involved distorted self-perceptions about one’s body (dysmorphia) and an intense, borderline delusional, fear of gaining weight, often cued by prevailing beauty standards (notably, a cultural fixation with slender female bodies). Mass public education campaigns followed. Hotlines opened, celebrities spoke out. Anorexia became part of the zeitgeist.
Before Charlene’s death, the psychiatrist Sing Lee had been quietly studying a small number of women in Hong Kong who were starving themselves to dangerous degrees. Most of these women did not obsess over their bodies, nor fear becoming fat. They instead complained of somatic (bodily) ailments: uncomfortable bloating, a profound lack of appetite. The cognitive hallmark of Western anorexia – a distorted body image – was largely absent. Lee seemed to be documenting a distinct form of self-starvation with its own local logic. The situation has changed dramatically since then. By 2007, more than three-quarters of the anorexic patients Lee treated cited ‘fat phobia’ as a central motivation. Today, anorexia in Hong Kong is terrifyingly common, affecting roughly one in 200 schoolgirls – comparable to trends in the United States.
We do not know the form that Charlene’s illness took. What we do know is that her death became a catalyst for the rapid importation of a Western conceptual framework that reshaped how Hong Kong understood and experienced disordered eating in young women.
Anorexia is just one example from a compendium of curious cases in which culture seemingly shapes mental disorders. For example, anthropologists have documented how cultures sculpt ‘idioms of distress’ – socially learned and sanctioned ways of communicating unusual mental episodes and patterns of psychological suffering. Hallucinations and dissociative states are routinely interpreted as forms of spiritual possession across the globe, as with Zār in northeast Africa and the Middle East.
These cases are not fringe oddities. Even depression – arguably the most common psychiatric condition in the world – seems to look strikingly different depending on when and where it appears. In China, patients who meet Western criteria for depression present mainly with bodily symptoms, such as fatigue, insomnia and headaches, rather than psychological symptoms, like low self-esteem and apathy. Delusions, multiple personality and social anxiety also take different forms in different cultures.
Cultural variation in psychiatry raises fundamental and increasingly urgent questions about abnormal minds. What precisely can culture shape? We usually think of mental disorder as something going wrong inside a person, and of culture as shaping how that internal problem is expressed and socially understood. Can culture penetrate deeper, to reshape the internal problem itself? What would that even look like?
The suggestion that cultural variables can significantly shape the internal mechanisms of mental disorders, including brute neurobiological hardware, may seem counterintuitive, even radical. But I’ll make the case that culture may possess a more substantial ability to do so – in some disorders, to some degrees – than is generally acknowledged. The basic reason is that the brain, the organ of the mind, is plastic and has functions of representing and adapting to a rich social world.
This could well allow cultural ecosystems to inculcate ‘local’ kinds of disorders – ones that may look similar to their cross-cultural counterparts, but differ in their psychological and even neurological profiles in ways consequential for scientific classification and treatment. ‘Anorexia’ or ‘depression’ in one cultural context may then be best understood as a different condition altogether in another. Mental disorders are already notoriously heterogeneous: one study of 3,703 patients diagnosed with depression found 1,030 distinct combinations of symptoms. One source of this variation may be cultural environments, including subcultures within a country’s borders. These possibilities hinge on unsettled questions about how plastic the mechanisms of mental disorders really are, and whether cultural factors have the right kind of causal access to them.
To begin, let me distinguish three widely accepted ways that culture can shape mental disorders, before investigating whether it can also shape their internal mechanisms. When I say ‘culture’, I mean a broad class of sociocultural factors, including social roles, norms, values, institutional and political structures, even particular medical beliefs and practices. By ‘shape’, I mean causally influence.

The first widely accepted way is by shaping our concepts of disorder. Here I am accepting a basic distinction between our various concepts of mental disorder (including lay and scientific theories of what disorders are, and what explains them), and the actual nature of those conditions. Historians have long shown that psychiatric concepts can be covertly shaped by social forces. What counts as ‘normal’ versus ‘eccentric’ versus ‘pathological’ behaviour is often prescribed by prevailing cultural norms and scientific authorities (whether legitimate or pseudo). A particularly dramatic example from 1851 is the diagnosis ‘Drapetomania’, which was given exclusively to Black slaves who fled their captors. The warped logic – which rested on the widespread and pernicious belief that God had ordained Black people to be enslaved – was that slavery was so beneficial, only a mentally ill person would cut and run. In this case, the concept was culturally shaped and pseudo, because it did not refer to a genuine condition, only mentally competent agents acting rationally.
But culture can legitimately inform psychiatric concepts, such as notions of harm and thresholds for ‘appropriate’ behaviour. Answers to questions like How much despondence or apathy is pathological? and Is this person’s gambling significantly interfering with their life? are partly scientific and partly cultural. For example, hearing God speak seems like a textbook case of psychosis. Yet many US evangelicals actively cultivate the experience and consider it a sign of closeness to God. Psychiatry’s own guidelines recognise that such experiences can be a normal part of religious life.
The second accepted form of cultural shaping is by modulating mental health outcomes, through various pathways, including the social determinants of health. Poverty, discrimination, social isolation, stigma and access to care comprise psychosocial risk factors that can significantly impact disorder prevalence, treatment success, even mortality rates. For example, migrants and their children develop schizophrenia at three times the rate of native-born populations, a gap that researchers have linked to discrimination and social exclusion.
The third way involves culture shaping the lived experience of mental disorder. Medical anthropologists distinguish between a ‘disease’ – a biological pathology that explains medical symptoms – and an ‘illness’ – how the symptoms of disease are experienced, expressed and socially understood. Culture can significantly shape illness. For example, an HIV infection may be the same disease everywhere, but the experience of AIDS (the late stage of the HIV infection) can differ considerably across cultural contexts – eg, where there is widespread belief that AIDS is a form of divine punishment.
The changing form of self-starvation in Hong Kong is commonly explained as an interaction of two of those types of cultural shaping. First, a Western theory of anorexia reshaped the concept of anorexia in Hong Kong, as understood by both clinicians and laypeople. Second, that conceptual uptake induced new ways of understanding, experiencing and expressing anorexic distress, thereby producing new symptoms. Culture shaped medical concepts; concepts shaped illness. The philosopher Ian Hacking argued that this situation can create a complex feedback loop, where concepts and illness-experience continuously remake each other. In The Social Construction of What? (1999), he called this the ‘looping effect’.
Can culture penetrate deeper, to shape diseases themselves? By shaping disease, I mean creating new pathologies, or altering an existing one enough that it becomes a new condition, one we must classify, explain and treat differently. In somatic medicine, the answer is generally ‘no’ for culture, but ‘yes’ for chemical and biological factors. If we expose a case of tuberculosis to antibiotics, it can evolve inside a patient into multidrug-resistant TB, which calls for a distinct diagnosis and treatment strategy. Environmental toxins, genetic mutations, infections and diet can all reshape pathologies. Cultural variables may play an upstream role, for example, by disposing doctors to overprescribe antibiotics. But the cultural content – the beliefs and meanings behind such practices – makes no difference to the disease itself. Culture seems to lack the right sort of causal connection to underlying pathologies.
A crisp distinction between ‘illness’ and ‘disease’ seems appropriate for most somatic disorders: cholera, cancer and cirrhosis are the same diseases whether they appear in Beijing or Boston, even if illness profiles can vary wildly. Does the distinction hold for psychiatry?
There are two reasons to suspect it does not. First, the paradigmatic features of mental disorders – abnormal thoughts, feelings, behaviours – overlap massively with features of illness, which can be shaped by culture. A cardiologist does not consult their patient’s beliefs to diagnose or explain a leaking heart valve. But psychiatrists make diagnoses based on mental ‘signs’ (outwardly observable behaviours, like disorganised speech) and ‘symptoms’ (self-reports about lived experience, such as low self-esteem or a fear of fatness). These features are also thought to partially explain disorders: in anorexia, a fear of fatness is sometimes thought to drive self-starvation.
Second, it is not obvious that mental disorders depend on biological diseases, at least in the familiar medical sense. There is no identified pathophysiology for anorexia, depression, schizophrenia or any other mental disorder – no clear account of system dysfunction, no ‘chemical imbalance’. The picture instead is far messier: the brains and bodies of mentally ill people differ from unafflicted populations in myriad subtle ways. Psychiatry is left with diverse theories of disorder pitched at different explanatory levels – some plausible, all partial and piecemeal, none yet mature enough to revolutionise our understanding.
