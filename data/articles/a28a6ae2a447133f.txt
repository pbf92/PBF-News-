TITLE: Plyometric Training Boosts Jump and Sprint Performance in Volleyball Athletes
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic review and meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Physiology indicates that standalone plyometric training is associated with statistically significant improvements in both vertical jump height and linear sprint speed among volleyball athletes. The study, led by Wei Ding, Xi Wang, and Meng Xu, synthesized data from 12 controlled trials encompassing a total of 326 participants. By isolating plyometric exercises from other forms of strength or agility training, the researchers aimed to quantify the independent effects of this specific training modality on core physical performance metrics that are critical for competitive volleyball.
The research addresses a gap in previous literature, where plyometric training was frequently combined with concurrent resistance, technical, or agility programs, making it difficult to determine the specific contribution of plyometrics alone. The authors conducted a comprehensive search across five electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and SPORTDiscus, with the search closing on July 10, 2026. The review was prospectively registered on the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews, ensuring methodological transparency and adherence to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for reporting.
To be included in the quantitative synthesis, studies had to meet strict eligibility criteria. The population consisted of competitive volleyball athletes of any age, sex, or competitive level. The intervention was defined as a structured program consisting exclusively of stretch-shortening cycle-based explosive exercises, with no additional concurrent training modalities such as heavy resistance training or agility drills. The control groups maintained their usual training routines without the addition of plyometric work. This design allowed the researchers to compare the specific outcomes of the plyometric intervention against a passive or usual-training baseline.
The primary outcomes measured were countermovement jump (CMJ) height and linear sprint time. The CMJ is a widely used field assessment that quantifies the function of the stretch-shortening cycle, a mechanism where a rapid eccentric stretch of the muscle-tendon unit stores elastic energy that is subsequently released during the concentric contraction to amplify force output. This mechanical pattern aligns with the force production required for spike takeoffs and defensive block jumps in volleyball. Linear sprint performance, typically measured over short distances, serves as a routine indicator of court transition efficiency, defensive recovery capacity, and offensive fast-break potential.
The pooled analysis revealed that standalone plyometric training produced a statistically significant improvement in countermovement jump performance. The standardized mean difference, expressed as Hedges’ g, was 0.55, with a 95% confidence interval ranging from 0.36 to 0.75. In absolute terms, this corresponds to an approximate mean improvement of 2.4 cm in jump height. Notably, no between-study heterogeneity was detected for this outcome, with an I-squared statistic of 0.0%, indicating that the observed vertical jump gains were highly consistent across the different study protocols and participant cohorts included in the meta-analysis.
For linear sprint performance, the meta-analysis also yielded a statistically significant pooled effect favoring the plyometric intervention. The Hedges’ g value was -0.48, with a 95% confidence interval from -0.90 to -0.07. In this context, negative values denote faster sprint times, indicating an improvement in performance. The between-study heterogeneity for sprint outcomes was low, at 21.2%, suggesting that the transfer of neuromuscular adaptations from plyometric training to horizontal locomotor performance is relatively consistent, though slightly more variable than the vertical jump results.
Subgroup analyses were conducted to identify potential moderating variables that might influence the magnitude of the adaptive response. For countermovement jump performance, no statistically significant differences were found based on age, intervention duration, or sex. Both athletes under 18 years of age and those 18 years and older showed significant beneficial effects, although the effect size was numerically larger in the adult subgroup. Similarly, programs lasting fewer than 12 weeks produced significant benefits, while longer durations did not reach statistical significance in the subgroup analysis, though the difference between these durations was not statistically significant.
In contrast, sprint distance was identified as a statistically significant moderator for sprint performance. The analysis showed larger and more consistent effects for sprint distances of 20 meters and longer, where the Hedges’ g was -0.75. For distances shorter than 20 meters, the effect was small and non-significant. This pattern aligns with the physiological basis of plyometric adaptations, which primarily enhance the rate of force development and ground contact stiffness, factors that have the greatest impact on the acceleration phase of sprinting that dominates performance over longer distances.
The authors assessed the methodological quality of the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool for randomized controlled trials and the ROBINS-I tool for quasi-experimental trials. All 12 included studies were rated as having an overall moderate risk of bias. The primary sources of concern were insufficient allocation concealment and the lack of blinded outcome assessment, which are inherent limitations of open-label exercise intervention research. No study was assessed as having a serious or critical risk of bias, and the domains of intervention classification and missing data were consistently judged as low risk.
Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s regression tests. For both countermovement jump and linear sprint outcomes, the funnel plots exhibited generally symmetric distributions, and the formal statistical tests returned non-significant results. This suggests that the observed effects are unlikely to be materially overestimated by small-study effects or selective publication. However, the authors noted that statistical power to detect publication bias was limited by the small number of included studies, a common constraint in meta-analyses of specific sports populations.
Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled effect estimates. No single study was identified as an outlier that would reverse the overall direction of the effect. The consistency of these results supports the stability of the findings, indicating that the observed improvements in jump and sprint performance are not driven by any single anomalous trial.
The study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The findings are specific to volleyball athletes and cannot be directly generalized to athletes from other court sports, field sports, or endurance disciplines. The analysis only included behavioral performance endpoints and did not incorporate direct physiological or biomechanical indicators such as electromyographic activity or ground reaction force parameters. Consequently, the mechanistic interpretations regarding neuromuscular adaptations are based on established physiological theory rather than direct empirical measurement from the included trials. Additionally, the pooled sprint outcomes included distances ranging from 10 to 50 meters, which reflect different phases of sprint performance, introducing some clinical heterogeneity.
The authors conclude that standalone plyometric training is associated with measurable improvements in both vertical jump and linear sprint performance in volleyball athletes. These findings provide an evidence-based foundation for the design and optimization of volleyball-specific plyometric training prescriptions. Future research should prioritize rigorous methodological designs, including blinded outcome assessment and prospective trial registration, to reduce risk of bias. Standardized controlled trials with uniform training parameters are needed to further explore moderators such as baseline performance level and to establish optimal dosing strategies for different phases of the annual training plan.
Formal certainty of evidence evaluations conducted under the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework provided additional nuance regarding the findings. The authors rated the certainty of evidence for countermovement jump height as moderate, downgrading it by one level due to serious risk of bias stemming from the open-label design of exercise interventions and incomplete allocation concealment reporting. For linear sprint performance, the certainty was graded as low to moderate, receiving downgrades for both serious risk of bias and serious imprecision, the latter driven by relatively wide confidence intervals and fluctuating significance across leave-one-out iterations.
Methodological safeguards guided study selection and data synthesis. Two independent reviewers screened citations and extracted study variables, achieving substantial to excellent inter-rater reliability, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.87 for title and abstract screening, 0.92 for full-text evaluations, and 0.84 for risk-of-bias appraisals. Meta-analytic computations were executed in R version 4.4.1 using the metafor package, applying a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation and Hartung-Knapp adjustments. When computing change-score standard deviations where study-level correlations were omitted, the authors adopted an assumed pre-post correlation coefficient of 0.5. Subsequent sensitivity analyses testing correlation values of 0.3 and 0.7 yielded shifts of less than 0.05 g units, confirming that analytical assumptions did not alter the pooled conclusions.
The synthesized trials enrolled volleyball athletes across competitive tiers, spanning youth academy players to elite first-division and professional competitors. Interventions lasted between 4 and 16 weeks, with training schedules typically comprising one to three weekly sessions. Across these protocols, standalone programs utilized explosive stretch-shortening drills including drop jumps, depth jumps, squat jumps, box jumps, tuck jumps, hurdle hops, and bounding drills. Regarding sprint outcomes by age, subgroup analysis indicated that neither adults aged 18 and older (Hedges’ g = -0.47, 95% CI -0.97 to 0.03) nor younger athletes under 18 (Hedges’ g = -0.63, 95% CI -2.42 to 1.15) showed statistically significant improvements within their isolated cohorts. The researchers noted that these non-significant subgroup outcomes reflect limited statistical power from small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals rather than demonstrable age-related disparities in adaptation.
