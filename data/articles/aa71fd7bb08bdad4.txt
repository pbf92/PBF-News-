TITLE: Global Constipation Analysis Links Diagnostic Methods to Substantial Disparities in Prevalence
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive umbrella review and meta-analysis evaluating worldwide epidemiological literature indicates that constipation affects approximately 24.1 percent of individuals globally, corresponding to nearly one in four people across diverse populations. The systematic investigation, published in Frontiers in Medicine, evaluated 48 eligible articles comprising 18 systematic reviews and meta-analyses alongside 30 primary epidemiological studies, representing a cumulative pooled sample of 2,199,600 participants. While the overarching pooled estimate demonstrates an extensive international burden, the researchers documented that observed prevalence figures vary substantially based upon the specific clinical criteria applied during evaluation. Studies relying on standardized Rome diagnostic criteria yielded a markedly lower pooled prevalence of 18.3 percent, whereas investigations employing alternative diagnostic tools or subjective clinician assessments registered a prevalence of 46.1 percent. This divergence underscores how diagnostic definitions directly shape global health estimates for gastrointestinal conditions.
The authors established their protocol under PROSPERO registration CRD42024527501, designing a rigorous three-tiered analytical approach intended to assess constipation irrespective of subtype or diagnostic classification. Comprehensive database searches were executed across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library from inception through March 2024, followed by an updated systematic retrieval conducted in May 2025 to ensure the inclusion of recent literature. Across initial database interrogations, 3,649 records were screened, leaving 3,395 items following the removal of duplicate or retracted citations, while supplementary manual searches yielded 39 additional eligible publications. After evaluating 119 full-text manuscripts against stringent eligibility benchmarks, the authors selected 48 studies that delivered quantitative epidemiological measurements. Non-systematic publications, scoping reviews, consensus statements, narrative commentaries, and clinical guidelines lacking defined empirical extractions were systematically excluded to maintain strict analytical fidelity.
To evaluate the complex landscape of bowel dysfunction, the authors incorporated studies spanning functional constipation, irritable bowel syndrome with constipation predominance, chronic idiopathic constipation, and secondary constipation caused by medications. The investigators observed that functional constipation is formally defined under Rome IV guidelines by difficult or infrequent defecation accompanied by feelings of incomplete evacuation, whereas irritable bowel syndrome with constipation predominance requires symptoms to persist for at least six months, occur at least once weekly, and lack alternative organic explanations. The review also examined drug-induced secondary manifestations, noting published evidence that up to 80 percent of patients receiving specific pharmacological regimens may develop secondary constipation, frequently presenting without recognized clinical symptoms. Specifically, clozapine therapy was highlighted as inducing symptoms in up to 56 percent of recipients with an odds ratio of 4.5 compared with controls, sharing underlying pathophysiological characteristics of gastrointestinal hypomotility and delayed colonic transit.
Across the tiered synthesis, the overall pooled global prevalence of 24.1 percent carried a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 19.6 percent to 28.6 percent and an identical heterogeneity statistic of 100 percent. The statistical modeling also established a wide 95 percent prediction interval ranging between 3.33 percent and 65.51 percent, illustrating pronounced variability across different investigative settings. When partitioned by evidence tier, published systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrated a pooled prevalence of 19.1 percent, whereas primary epidemiological investigations yielded a higher prevalence of 27.2 percent. Tests exploring potential publication bias through Egger’s linear regression revealed an intercept of 9.79 with a standard error of 9.06 and a p-value of 0.29, demonstrating no statistically significant small-study effects. However, visible asymmetry across funnel plots suggested that between-study variance was driven by distinct healthcare environments, diverse study populations, and disparate diagnostic methodologies rather than selective publication tendencies.
Random-effects meta-regression models executed in statistical software identified diagnostic criteria, age interacted with diagnostic tools, and participant referral sources as key moderators accounting for substantial portions of observed heterogeneity. The comparison contrasting Rome criteria against non-Rome diagnostic approaches generated a significant test statistic of 43.06 with a p-value below 0.0001, while evaluations between specific iterations of the Rome system produced a statistic of 27.44. In regression models, studies utilizing Rome criteria exhibited a negative coefficient of minus 0.28 relative to non-Rome methodologies, yielding a model-predicted prevalence of 18 percent compared against 46 percent for non-standardized methods. Furthermore, investigations applying non-Rome frameworks consistently demonstrated higher prevalence estimates of 38 percent compared against 16 percent for Rome IV criteria and 19 percent for pooled Rome I through III guidelines, reinforcing the sensitivity of epidemiological measurements to operational diagnostic thresholds.
The meta-regression further revealed significant interaction effects when patient age categories were analyzed in conjunction with applied diagnostic tools. When assessed via Rome criteria, adult populations exhibited a predicted prevalence of 17 percent, contrasting sharply with a 39 percent estimate observed when adults were evaluated using alternative clinical assessments. Pediatric cohorts exhibited an identical pattern, registering a predicted prevalence of 17 percent under Rome diagnostic guidelines compared with elevated estimates derived from alternative diagnostic measures. Meta-regression analyzing primary epidemiological literature confirmed that diagnostic methods, generational Rome versions, age interactions, and participant health status served as relevant moderators explaining variance. Conversely, meta-regression models performed exclusively across the 18 systematic reviews did not identify significant moderators, demonstrating that broad evidence aggregations frequently mask underlying methodological disparities present within primary clinical cohorts.
Subgroup analyses stratified by demographic and institutional variables illustrated further variations across clinical populations and geographic sectors. Although adult populations exhibited a higher pooled prevalence of 26.1 percent compared to 22.9 percent among pediatric cohorts, the overall difference did not achieve statistical significance. Referral settings produced pronounced divergence, with clinical recruitment settings generating an adult prevalence of 35.4 percent compared to 20.7 percent within adult community settings and 29.5 percent in mixed healthcare environments. Geographic stratification revealed that European investigations documented the highest pooled prevalence at 34 percent, followed by Asian studies at 26 percent and studies from the Americas at 14 percent. Income-based categorizations demonstrated that high-income nations reported an aggregate prevalence of 27 percent, whereas upper middle-income countries reported a prevalence of 20 percent, though income stratification did not reach statistical significance in meta-regression models.
Health status produced divergent associations depending on participant age, revealing notable contrasts between pediatric and adult cohorts. Among adults, individuals with diagnosed medical conditions or health-related disorders exhibited an elevated prevalence of 39.2 percent compared with 15.6 percent among healthy adult participants. In stark contrast, pediatric populations displayed an opposing pattern, wherein healthy children exhibited a prevalence of 26.0 percent compared to 18.8 percent among children with underlying medical conditions. When subtype classifications were examined, functional constipation showed a pooled prevalence of 20.0 percent, whereas irritable bowel syndrome with constipation predominance registered at 26.5 percent and other categorized types registered at 28.7 percent, though differences between clinical subtypes did not achieve statistical significance in meta-regression modeling.
Evaluating associated risk factors demonstrated that female sex was consistently associated with significantly increased odds of constipation across the entire evidence base. The pooled odds ratio for females reached 1.57 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.27 to 1.95. Subgroup evaluations confirmed this association, with systematic reviews showing an odds ratio of 1.37 and primary studies demonstrating an odds ratio of 1.81. Advancing age indicated a modest numerical increase among older adults aged 60 and above, yielding an odds ratio of 1.18, though this association lacked statistical significance. Among dietary variables, meat consumption was associated with higher odds of constipation at an odds ratio of 2.92 based on single-country data, while food allergy demonstrated an odds ratio of 2.33 across two primary investigations. Factors such as polypharmacy, psychological distress, infrequent physical activity, and inadequate sleep duration exhibited trends toward elevated odds without reaching statistical significance.
Methodological quality appraisal revealed considerable variation in rigor across the synthesized literature. Using the AMSTAR 2 checklist for systematic reviews, 13 of the 18 included reviews were classified as critically low quality, three as low, one as moderate, and one as high quality, primarily due to absent protocol registrations and omitted funding declarations. Evaluation of primary studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale showed higher overall rigor, with 19 studies rated as high quality and 11 as moderate quality. Certainty of evidence assessed via the GRADE framework was rated as low to moderate, driven downward by substantial between-study heterogeneity, wide prediction intervals, and potential overlap across source syntheses. The authors concluded that while constipation affects roughly one in four people globally, standardized diagnostic criteria remain vital to resolve substantial epidemiological discrepancies across worldwide healthcare systems.
