TITLE: Google adds Oklahoma and Alabama to its ad privacy tools, now 24 states
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google has named Oklahoma, Louisiana, Alabama and Vermont as the next four US states whose privacy laws its advertising products will account for. A Google AdSense Help Center page dated November 16, 2026 says the four states "will have privacy law provisions coming into effect" on or after January 1, 2027, and that publishers can apply Google's Restricted Data Processing mode to them, including through the IAB's GPP National v2 string. A second AdSense page, on US state regulations messages, now lists 24 states, and two Google Ad Manager pages set out which consent strings Ad Manager reads and what happens to an ad request once restricted processing is switched on.
The four-state page is one of two AdSense documents dated November 16 on the new laws. Its single date is looser than the statutes behind it: Alabama's law takes effect on May 1, 2027, and Vermont's on January 1, 2028, according to analyses by Venable and Davis+Gilbert.
In Short
Google has listed Oklahoma, Louisiana, Alabama and Vermont as the next US states whose privacy laws its ad products will account for, and its AdSense consent messages now cover 24 states. That matters to publishers earning from AdSense or Ad Manager, because the setting that switches off ad personalization, and the consent strings that trigger it, decide how much user data reaches advertisers in those states. The new pages do not say Google will apply anything automatically in Oklahoma or Alabama, whose laws, according to law firm analyses, do not require businesses to honor browser opt-out signals, and Alabama's law starts on May 1, 2027 rather than January 1. Ad Manager, meanwhile, reads only five state-specific sections of the IAB's consent string and still accepts a format the IAB retired in January 2024.
What the November 16 page says
The page is titled "Upcoming US states with comprehensive privacy laws" and sits at answer 18821562 in the AdSense Help Center. It contains four paragraphs, reproduced here in full because they are the whole of Google's statement on the four states.
"On or after January 1, 2027, Oklahoma, Louisiana, Alabama and Vermont will have privacy law provisions coming into effect. Google's approach in these states is similar to that of existing US states with active comprehensive laws. Activating Restricted Data Processing (RDP) mode turns off ad personalization, which may assist publishers in honoring opt-outs for targeted advertising and avoiding activities that could be considered a 'sale' or 'sharing' of user data."
"Publishers can also choose to send RDP via GPP National v2, which supports these new states."
"Please note that publishers should still work with their legal teams to understand if RDP is the appropriate solution for their compliance. Publishers who currently leverage the RDP should work with their legal teams to understand if they should expand their use of RDP in additional states."
The last sentence is the operative one. It places the decision about Oklahoma, Louisiana, Alabama and Vermont with publishers and their lawyers. A year ago Google took a different line for a different batch of states: in November 2025 it said account-level RDP settings would apply automatically to traffic from Indiana, Kentucky and Rhode Island from January 1, 2026. The November 16 page contains no equivalent statement.
A separate AdSense page, also dated November 16 and titled "Upcoming US states with universal opt-out mechanism," covered only two of the four states. It said Google would receive Global Privacy Control signals directly from users in Louisiana and Vermont from November 17, 2026 and switch those ad requests into RDP mode. Oklahoma and Alabama do not appear on it.
Four laws, four calendars
The four statutes were all signed in 2026, and they do not share a start date.
Effective dates are those given by both Venable (July 30, 2026) and Davis+Gilbert (July 7, 2026), which agree on all four. Signing dates for Louisiana and Vermont come from the Future of Privacy Forum, Hunton and Koley Jessen. Gaps are PPC Land calculations.
Oklahoma
Oklahoma's law, SB 546, was signed by Governor Kevin Stitt on March 20, 2026, according to Mayer Brown and Bass Berry. The House passed it on February 19, 2026 by 84 votes to 4, according to an analysis by Troutman Amin published in the National Law Review, which also says a House amendment pushed the start from July 1, 2026 to January 1, 2027. The law applies to businesses that control or process the data of at least 100,000 Oklahoma consumers, or of at least 25,000 while deriving more than 50% of gross revenue from selling personal data, according to all three analyses.
Consumers get rights to access, correct, delete and port their data, and to opt out of targeted advertising, sales and certain profiling. A "sale" covers only exchanges for monetary consideration, a narrow definition, according to Mayer Brown and Bass Berry. The attorney general enforces the law exclusively, with a 30-day cure period that Bass Berry and Troutman Amin say does not sunset, and civil penalties of up to $7,500 per violation. On signals the three analyses agree: the law does not require businesses to recognize universal opt-out preference signals such as Global Privacy Control.
Alabama
Alabama's Personal Data Protection Act, HB 351, passed both chambers unanimously on April 7, 2026, according to Burr & Forman. Hunton and Burr & Forman date Governor Kay Ivey's signature to April 17, 2026; Freshfields and PYMNTS give April 16. The law takes effect on May 1, 2027.
Its reach is wide on one measure and narrow on others. It covers businesses that process the data of more than 25,000 Alabama consumers, excluding data handled solely to complete a payment, or that derive more than 25% of gross revenue from selling personal data regardless of how many consumers are involved, according to Freshfields and Burr & Forman. Freshfields says the 25,000 figure ties Montana's as the lowest threshold in the country. Davis+Gilbert's summary table lists different thresholds, 35,000 consumers or 5,000 with more than 25% of revenue from sales. Businesses with fewer than 500 employees are exempt if they do not sell personal data, according to MultiState.
The definition of a sale is narrow and carves out some analytics and marketing services arrangements, according to Burr & Forman and Freshfields. The law requires no data protection assessments and, according to Hunton and Freshfields, no recognition of opt-out preference signals. The attorney general enforces it exclusively, with a 45-day right to cure and penalties of up to $15,000 per uncured violation, according to Freshfields.
Louisiana and Vermont
Louisiana's law takes effect on January 1, 2027. Analyses differ on whether its text covers browser signals: the Future of Privacy Forum says it does not reference opt-out preference signals, while Hunton describes an authorized-agent route that can run through a technology signal such as Global Privacy Control. Vermont's S.71 is the only one of the four that plainly requires signal handling: Koley Jessen and Davis+Gilbert say businesses must treat such signals as opt-out requests. It does not apply until January 1, 2028.
A signal list that tracks the statute books
Set against the Future of Privacy Forum's effective-dates chart, dated July 10, 2026, Google's choice of states for its browser-signal handling follows a visible pattern. The chart marks 12 of the 20 earlier laws as requiring businesses to honor opt-out preference signals. PPC Land's coverage records Google treating Global Privacy Control as an opt-out in 11 of those 12: Colorado first, then Connecticut, Montana, Nebraska, New Hampshire, Texas, Minnesota, New Jersey and Maryland on June 30, 2025, then Delaware and Oregon on November 17, 2025. California, the twelfth, has not been part of those announcements.
None of the eight states the chart marks "No" for signals (Florida, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Rhode Island, Tennessee, Utah and Virginia) appears in Google's GPC announcements, and neither do Oklahoma or Alabama. Vermont, whose law requires signal handling, has been added. Louisiana is the exception that the analyses leave open. Google's pages give no reason for the split. The comparison is PPC Land's, built from the chart and PPC Land's own reporting.
Twenty-four states on the messages page
The AdSense page "About US state regulations messages," answer 10961479, carries no date. It describes the message type in one sentence: "The US state regulations message type is displayed to users who are subject to privacy legislation specific to the state in which they're located." It adds a caution: "It is your responsibility to make sure your messages meet legal requirements."
The applicability list at the top of the page names 24 states, including all four on the November 16 page. When AdSense introduced the messages in June 2024 they covered five: California, Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut and Utah. In December 2025, Google added Indiana, Kentucky and Rhode Island along with a targeting option labeled "All current and future supported US States."
The page's section on GPP splits the states by which part of the consent string carries their choices: "The US National section will be used to store and transmit the choices of users in the following US states: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Maryland, Minnesota, Montana, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Oregon, Rhode Island, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, and Virginia. The Florida section will be used to store and transmit the choices of users in Florida." That paragraph names 20 states. The four new ones are on the applicability list but absent from it, so the page does not say which section will carry their users' choices. The November 16 page says only that GPP National v2 "supports these new states."

What the message writes into the consent string
The messages page records exactly how Google's own consent tool fills in the GPP string. When the "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" link appears on a page:
- "SharingNotice, SaleOptOutNotice, SharingOptOutNotice, and TargetedAdvertisingOptOutNotice are set to indicate that notice was provided."
- "SaleOptOut, SharingOptOut, and TargetedAdvertisingOptOut are set to indicate the user did not opt out."
- "MspaCoveredTransaction is set to 'No.'"
If the user opts out, "SaleOptOut, SharingOptOut, and TargetedAdvertisingOptOut are updated to indicate that the user opted out," and "All other fields are set to 'Not applicable,' including those for SensitiveDataProcessing and KnownChildSensitiveDataConsents." The page then puts the downstream question back on publishers: "Publishers must work with their ad partners to understand how they respect GPP signals."
The message itself has three parts, according to the page: the link, a Confirm page titled "Opt out of the sale or sharing of personal information," and two buttons. Dismiss closes the message "without opting out," while Opt out records the choice: "When users click Opt out, the message communicates their opt-out status to Google." The page also notes that, with the California Privacy Rights Act in effect, Google automatically changed default CCPA message text to read "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information." Publishers who had written custom text were told to revise it themselves.
