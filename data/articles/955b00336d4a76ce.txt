TITLE: Anthropic researcher claims Claude helped prove Riemann hypothesis
OUTLET: Chosunbiz

A researcher at artificial intelligence (AI) company Anthropic claimed to have used its chatbot "Claude" to prove the "Riemann hypothesis," a mathematical problem that has remained unsolved for more than 160 years.
The Riemann hypothesis posits that there is a mathematical pattern in the seemingly irregular distribution of prime numbers. It is one of seven major unsolved problems selected by mathematicians, and an officially recognized proof would be a milestone in the history of mathematics.
Anthropic researcher Levent Alpoge, a mathematician, said in a post and a four-page document uploaded to his website on the 20th that he believed he had "discovered a proof of the Riemann hypothesis in collaboration with Claude," according to reports from Agence France-Presse (AFP), the Financial Times (FT) and other international media.
German mathematician Bernhard Riemann first proposed the Riemann hypothesis in 1859. Prime numbers, such as 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11, are divisible only by 1 and themselves. As numbers grow larger, the intervals at which primes appear become irregular. Riemann believed there was a hidden pattern in their distribution and studied a mathematical function called the "Riemann zeta function" to explain it.
The hypothesis holds that certain points where the function has a value of 0 all lie on a single straight line. If proven, it would provide a mathematical basis for explaining the seemingly irregular distribution of prime numbers more precisely.
The Riemann hypothesis is one of the "Millennium Prize Problems," seven major unsolved mathematical problems selected in 2000 by the U.S.-based Clay Mathematics Institute (CMI). The institute offered a $1 million prize (about 1.34 billion won) for solving each problem.
Now, 26 years later, the only problem officially solved is the "Poincaré conjecture," proven by Russian mathematician Grigori Perelman. Besides the Riemann hypothesis, the "P versus NP problem," which concerns the distinction between problems computers can solve quickly and those they cannot, remains unsolved, among others.
Brian Conrey, an authority on the Riemann hypothesis, called the claim "a monumental moment in human history" in comments to AFP. However, Conrey said he had only seen Alpoge's public post and had not yet reviewed the proof. Kit Yates, a professor at Britain's University of Bath, also said the claim "will be subjected to intense scrutiny," and that if it withstands that scrutiny, it will have major implications for mathematics and our understanding of AI's capabilities.
Alpoge released a version of the proof written in Lean, a programming language that uses computers to verify mathematical arguments. Alpoge also claimed to have solved the "generalized Riemann hypothesis," which is broader in scope than the Riemann hypothesis. Alpoge said the work using AI amounted to more than 250,000 pages. Alpoge said the decision to go public came after consulting Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei. The announcement came the day after Anthropic's initial public offering (IPO).
Rival OpenAI previously said in September that its AI model had developed a solution to the "Navier-Stokes problem," another Millennium Prize Problem. But observers continued to point out that experts would need to verify whether the mathematical proof produced by AI was actually valid.
At the time, Alpoge was working on the problem with New York University mathematician Tristan Buckmaster and became embroiled in a dispute over credit, raising the possibility that their research data had been used in the course of using OpenAI's tools. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman dismissed the allegation as an "unfounded claim of plagiarism."
