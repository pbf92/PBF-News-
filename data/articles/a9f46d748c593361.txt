TITLE: Wall Street Journal tells all bots to keep out, except more than 60
OUTLET: PPC Land

Dow Jones, the News Corp company that publishes The Wall Street Journal, Barron's and MarketWatch, now tells every automated visitor to stay off its consumer news sites unless that visitor is on an approved list, according to a Nieman Lab report published on November 24, 2026. The company made the change in May 2026, rewriting the rule file that each site publishes for bots so that all of them are refused by default. The Journal's approved list holds more than 60 bots, including search engine crawlers and three scrapers operated by OpenAI, which has a licensing deal with News Corp worth more than $250 million.
In Short
The Wall Street Journal, Barron's and MarketWatch now ask every bot to stay away unless Dow Jones has put it on a list, which for the Journal holds more than 60 names, among them search engines and three OpenAI tools. The change matters to AI companies, which are being told to negotiate for access, and to publishers and advertisers watching bots grow to about a third of website pageviews in one industry index. The rule is a public request rather than a lock, so its effect depends on whether bots obey it, and a small group of large publishers, many of them owned by News Corp, now works the same way.
From a blocklist to a guest list
For most of the web's history, a publisher that wanted to keep out an unwanted bot named it in its robots.txt file, the plain text page at the root of a website that tells automated visitors which parts of the site they may and may not visit. Everything not named was allowed. Dow Jones has reversed that logic. According to Nieman Lab, "Rather than reactively blacklisting bots through robots.txt, Dow Jones now operates with a whitelist for select crawlers and scrapers." Every bot is refused unless the company has written it in.
The new permissions cover all of Dow Jones' free and paid consumer news sites, Nieman Lab reported, naming The Wall Street Journal, Barron's and MarketWatch. The company revised the robots.txt file of each site as part of the shift, and described the change to Nieman Lab for its November 24 report.
Nolly Evans, chief development officer at Dow Jones, tied the decision to what bots now do with what they collect. "As bot traffic has shifted from discovery-oriented use cases toward systems that can ingest, summarize, or otherwise benefit from our content without a relationship in place, we have moved to a more deliberate signal," Evans told Nieman Lab. "[This] signals to third parties that they must negotiate and partner directly with us for content access."
The distinction Evans draws is between bots that help readers find the Journal, such as the crawlers that build search engine indexes, and bots that take the journalism itself to answer questions or train AI models. He described the pressure on publishers in stark terms. "We're seeing astronomical growth in bot traffic while human referrals and traffic to news sites are being curtailed," he said. "That's a clear signal to publishers that the old norms are no longer working, and that something needs to change."
What Dow Jones has disclosed
The report does not publish the full list of more than 60 approved bots, does not name the three OpenAI scrapers, and does not say whether the Barron's and MarketWatch lists match the Journal's.
How a default-refuse rule works
Based on Nieman Lab's report, the mechanism works in five parts.
- One file per site. Each Dow Jones news site publishes a robots.txt file, which tells bots which parts of the site they can and cannot access.
- The old approach. Unwanted bots were added to the file one by one, after the company noticed them. Anything not listed could visit.
- The new default. Since May 2026 the file disallows all bots unless they are named as exceptions.
- The exceptions. For the Journal, more than 60 bots are named as allowed, including search engine crawlers and three OpenAI scrapers.
- No enforcement inside the file. According to Nieman Lab, robots.txt files "are voluntary guidance for all bots, including those deployed by AI companies. They're not hard technical blocks or legally binding contracts." A bot that is not on the list and obeys the file stays away. One that ignores it meets no barrier from the file itself.
The file has a second limit, which Google's Search Central team set out in a video episode in October: it governs whether a bot may fetch a page, not what the bot's owner does with the data after fetching it. An approved bot is not bound by the file in how it uses what it collects.
OpenAI is on the list. Others are told to negotiate
The presence of three OpenAI scrapers on the Journal's approved list is the clearest sign of what the change is for. OpenAI, according to Nieman Lab, has a licensing deal with News Corp worth more than $250 million. The partnership was announced on May 22, 2024, and press reports collected in PPC Land's coverage of Prebid's AI payment models put it at more than $250 million over five years. News Corp itself gives no value. When it reported results on November 4, 2026, its filings named OpenAI and Meta as partners without stating what either pays.
An AI company without a deal now finds itself in the same position as any unknown scraper. The file asks it to stay away, and Dow Jones' stated position is that the route in runs through a negotiation. Dow Jones already sells licensed content for AI use through Factiva, its business database, which passed 8,000 sources licensed for generative AI in January 2026, with The Wall Street Journal, Barron's and MarketWatch among them.
News Corp's chief executive set out the commercial logic in plain terms. "One of the most important issues for us is making sure that the IP journalists create is respected by the AI companies," Robert Thomson said in an interview with CNBC published on November 19, 2026, as quoted by Nieman Lab. "The principled companies want to have an agreement, and the unprincipled companies don't. We'll try to convince you to be transactional and pay us for our IP and pay us for our journalism, or we'll sue you."
The lawsuit half of that sentence is not hypothetical. Dow Jones' copyright case against Perplexity, No. 1:24-cv-07984, survived Perplexity's motion to dismiss in August 2025. OpenAI, meanwhile, keeps adding publishers: LVMH's media division signed with OpenAI on November 16, 2026, in a release that gave no price.
About 2% of leading publishers now refuse by default
Dow Jones is not alone, though the group it joins is small. Known Agents, a bot analytics company, analysed the robots.txt files of 1,000 leading English-language publisher websites and found that about 2% now disallow all bots by default, according to Nieman Lab. That is roughly 20 sites (PPC Land calculation). Those named include Reuters, Bloomberg Law, Publishers Weekly and The Register, a British technology news site.
Many of the others belong to News Corp, according to Nieman Lab: in Australia, The Australian, The Daily Telegraph in Sydney and the Herald Sun in Melbourne, and in the UK, The Times, The Sun and The Scottish Sun. Inside News Corp, the default refusal therefore reaches well beyond Dow Jones.
Known Agents keeps a public leaderboard of these files. It says it updates the ranking daily, checks the latest robots.txt files of 1,000 leading publishers, and measures what share of the AI data scrapers and AI data providers it knows about each file refuses. The wider picture is far less strict. Known Agents tracks more than 2,000 AI-related bots, according to Nieman Lab, but most English-language news publishers restrict only a fraction of them. A chart in the report, built on a sample of the top 1,000 English-language news sites, shows most sampled sites refusing less than half of those bots, with a separate column of sites at or near 100%, Slate among them.
Approved lists spread across publishing
The pattern has been building for a year. Late in 2025, The Atlantic told Digiday that it only allows bots associated with its licensing deals or those that send it significant traffic, Nieman Lab reported. The Atlantic is also among the publishers whose content Dow Jones licenses for AI use: it was one of the new sources when Factiva crossed the 8,000 mark in January. In June 2026, Digiday first reported that Reuters and Time had started issuing approved lists of their own.
People Inc., the publisher formerly known as Dotdash Meredith, has gone further than a written request. According to Nieman Lab, it switched at the beginning of 2026 to refusing bots by default at a technical level, beyond its robots.txt files, and went from blocking 2,000 unique bots to more than 30,000. People Inc. has a direct financial stake in how AI firms reach its pages. Its licensing and other revenue rose 49% to $52.7 million in the third quarter of 2026, while core web sessions fell 26%. PPC Land's Prebid coverage put Dotdash Meredith's fixed payments from OpenAI at no less than $16 million a year.
Bots at about a third of pageviews
The reason so many publishers are reconsidering, according to Nieman Lab, is volume. Over the past 90 days, around 34% of pageviews across more than 5,000 randomly selected websites came from bots, according to an index maintained by Known Agents. About 29% of that bot traffic was "AI-related," a group that covers AI agents, AI chatbots and AI training scrapers, as opposed to more traditional bots such as search engine crawlers and the Wayback Machine's archivers. Taken together, those two figures put AI-related bots at roughly 10% of all pageviews in the index (PPC Land calculation).

A request, not a lock
The weakness of the approach is written into the file format. Some AI companies, Nieman Lab noted, have used "stealth crawlers" to get around robots.txt files while avoiding detection, and it named Perplexity. Cloudflare published that finding in August 2025, and PPC Land covered it when Perplexity denied training AI models as Cloudflare documented stealth crawlers.
Compliance is uneven even among declared bots. TollBit's data for the first half of 2026 found that about 15% of identified AI page fetchers in Europe reached disallowed URLs. Known Agents' own leaderboard puts compliance with robots.txt rules at 92.4% for the AI scrapers and data providers it tracks, and its index puts the figure at 94.3% for bots overall, without stating the period on its public pages. Google itself added 12 existing crawlers and fetchers to its documentation on November 23, 2026, several of them described as ignoring robots.txt rules.
Gavin King, the chief executive of Known Agents, still argues that publishers leave the file underused. "Robots.txt is the easiest first line of defense," he told Nieman Lab. "The fact that people are reaching out to [internet infrastructure companies to sign] six-figure year contracts instead of updating their robots.txt for free is such a miss." The comparison is with paid bot management services, which identify and stop automated traffic at the network level.
