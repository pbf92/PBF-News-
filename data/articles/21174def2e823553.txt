TITLE: Global Study Finds One in Three Pregnant Women Carry Intestinal Parasites
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Approximately 31.76 percent of pregnant women worldwide are infected with intestinal parasites, according to an updated systematic review and meta-analysis published in the Journal of Epidemiology and Global Health. The study synthesizes data from 45 studies conducted between January 2020 and December 2025 across 15 countries in Africa, Asia, and Europe, representing 22,013 pregnant women.
Their collaborative project sought to resolve a major deficit in contemporary maternal epidemiological literature. While earlier global research reviews focused predominantly on parasitic worms such as helminths and largely neglected single-celled protozoan pathogens, this updated systematic synthesis provides both overall pooled prevalence calculations and species-specific infection rates across diverse populations of expectant mothers in low-resource and developing settings.
The investigators conducted their systematic review and meta-analysis in strict accordance with the PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines, having registered their prospective research protocol with the PROSPERO international database under registration code CRD420261290660. The authors executed comprehensive literature searches across eight major electronic databases: MEDLINE/PubMed, African Journals Online, CrossRef, EMBASE, Google Scholar, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, and Science Direct. Their explicit Boolean search queries paired population descriptors such as pregnant women and antenatal care with specific parasite nomenclature, including intestinal parasite, helminth, Ascaris lumbricoides, Entamoeba histolytica, and Giardia lamblia, alongside epidemiological prevalence terms.
To evaluate the methodological rigor and risk of bias within the included literature, the researchers applied the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies. To synthesize the extracted epidemiological proportions, the team employed a random-effects meta-analysis model utilizing the DerSimonian and Laird estimation method, chosen specifically to account for the anticipated substantial heterogeneity among the underlying observational studies. Heterogeneity across study cohorts was formally evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and quantified through the I-squared statistic. The researchers also evaluated potential publication bias across the published literature through funnel plot inspection, Egger’s linear regression test, and Begg’s rank correlation test.
Overall, the random-effects meta-analysis established a pooled global intestinal parasite prevalence of 31.76 percent (95% confidence interval [CI]: 26.86 to 36.67 percent) among pregnant women, with extreme statistical heterogeneity across cohorts (I-squared = 98.98 percent, p < 0.001). Species-specific analyses showed that among helminth species, the roundworm Ascaris lumbricoides was the most frequently identified pathogen, reported at a prevalence of 13.50 percent (95% CI: 23.29 to 24.40 percent as reported in the study data), followed by hookworm at 5.55 percent (95% CI: 4.39 to 6.71 percent) and Schistosoma mansoni at 5.43 percent (95% CI: 1.67 to 9.20 percent). Among intestinal protozoa, Entamoeba histolytica/dispar demonstrated the highest overall occurrence at 10.89 percent (95% CI: 7.50 to 14.26 percent), followed by Cryptosporidium parvum at 7.41 percent (95% CI: 3.30 to 11.52 percent) and Giardia lamblia at 6.11 percent (95% CI: 5.04 to 7.17 percent).
Subgroup analyses pre-specified by the authors investigated variations across geographic continents, individual countries, study designs, sampling techniques, sample sizes, and laboratory diagnostic methods. Continental pooled prevalence estimates revealed marked variation, with Europe reporting the highest regional prevalence at 42.50 percent (95% CI: 11.95 to 73.06 percent), followed by Africa at 31.89 percent (95% CI: 25.61 to 38.16 percent) and Asia at 28.07 percent (95% CI: 16.54 to 39.60 percent). At the national level, observed prevalence rates spanned a broad spectrum, ranging from a low of 13.46 percent (95% CI: 8.10 to 18.82 percent) among pregnant women in Cameroon to a peak of 60.90 percent (95% CI: 55.50 to 66.29 percent) in Burkina Faso. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated that diagnostic methodology significantly affected detection rates, with studies that utilized multi-method diagnostic approaches reporting substantially higher infection rates than those relying solely on standard direct wet mount microscopy.
Comprehensive sensitivity analyses conducted by the authors affirmed the statistical robustness of the primary pooled prevalence estimates. Nevertheless, the authors emphasized severe limitations that must temper the interpretation of these global figures. Most notably, geographic representation was heavily skewed, with the African continent contributing approximately 80 percent of the included literature (36 out of 45 studies), while Latin America, the Middle East, and Eastern Europe were minimally represented or completely unrepresented. Paired with the massive statistical heterogeneity (I-squared = 98.98 percent), the investigators cautioned against broad overgeneralization of the global average. They concluded that the substantial burden of protozoan infections requires maternal health programs to move beyond traditional helminth-only deworming initiatives toward species-specific diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, while urging future research to address understudied geographic regions.
