TITLE: OpenAI forces AI model resellers to seek its approval first
OUTLET: PPC Land

OpenAI today began requiring operators of platforms that give third parties direct access to its models to obtain the company's prior review and approval, under a rewritten version of its Usage Policies that also adds a section barring the use of OpenAI models and outputs to prepare training material for rival AI systems. The new text, which carries an effective date of November 20, 2026, replaces the version that had applied since October 29, 2025. OpenAI announced the changes in a changelog entry dated October 21, 2026, which gave customers 30 days before the rules took hold.
In Short
From today, a platform that lets other people or companies tap directly into OpenAI's models, such as an AI model router or gateway, needs OpenAI's review and approval first, while an app that only uses the models to run its own features does not. The same rewrite says OpenAI models and their outputs may not be used to build training data for AI systems meant to replace OpenAI's, rewrites the child safety rules, and widens the human review rule from automated decisions to automated actions. For anyone buying AI tools, the line between a vendor that passes on model access and a vendor that builds features on top of a model now carries a different set of obligations.
What OpenAI says changed
The changelog entry is the closest thing to an official summary. "We've updated our Usage Policies to clarify restrictions on using OpenAI models and outputs to train other models," it reads. "We've also clarified protections for children and teens and requirements for high-stakes uses. The update also incorporates our existing national security principles and sets out approval requirements for national security and law enforcement uses, as well as for model-access intermediaries. These changes are effective November 20, 2026."
A help center notice titled "We're updating our Usage Policies" lists the same four themes. According to that notice, the previous version stayed in force until today, and existing agreements and other applicable terms, including any express permissions granted in them, continue to apply. It describes the training language as a clarification of restrictions that already existed rather than a new prohibition, and it describes the intermediary rule as an added approval requirement.
The word "clarify" carries a lot of weight in that summary. The policy body grew from about 830 words in the October 2025 version to about 1,400 words in the new one, according to a PPC Land count of the two texts, which excludes navigation and the changelog. Three headed sections are new: Training and developing models, OpenAI-approved use and Providing access to our models. Most of the introductory text is unchanged, word for word, including the statement that OpenAI reserves "all rights to withhold access where we reasonably believe it necessary to protect our service or users or anyone else," and the privacy section, which still bars uses such as facial recognition databases built without data subject consent, reads exactly as before.
The change drew commentary on the day it took effect. Elena Gurevich, an AI and intellectual property attorney who advises companies on AI governance, wrote in a LinkedIn post on November 20 that "Starting today, Nov 20, OpenAI's updated usage policies kicked in." She summarized the intermediary rule this way: "In simple terms, if you are a business that provides your customers direct access to OpenAI's models, you need OpenAI's approval first. Using these models to power your application's features/functionality doesn't trigger the approval requirement." She gave OpenRouter, which she described as "a platform that streamlines access to multiple AI models from different providers," as an example of the kind of service in scope. That is her reading. OpenAI's policy names no company.
What changed, section by section
The intermediary rule
The provision that prompted Gurevich's post is short. "If you operate a model-access intermediary platform or other service that enables third parties to directly access OpenAI models, you must obtain our prior review and approval," the policy states. "This requirement does not apply solely because an application integrates OpenAI models to power its features or functionality."
Three phrases do the work. "Model-access intermediary platform" names the obvious targets: routers, gateways and aggregators that sit between OpenAI's API and a customer who wants to call a model. "Or other service" widens the net to anything that achieves the same result, whatever it calls itself. And "directly access" is the test that separates a service in scope from the carve-out. An application that uses an OpenAI model to summarize a document or draft an email is integrating the model to power a feature. A service that hands its customers a pipe through which they send their own prompts to an OpenAI model of their choosing looks much more like direct access. Between those two poles sits a large and growing category of products, from agent builders to workflow tools with a model picker, that the text does not place on either side.
The word "prior" also matters. Approval is required before a service of this kind operates, not after the fact. Neither the policy page nor the help notice describes how to apply, how long a review takes, whether services already running on November 20 have a transition window, or whether any intermediary has already been approved.
The rule reverses a direction OpenAI took four years ago. The same changelog records that on October 25, 2022, developers "no longer need to wait for approval after submitting as long as they comply with our policies," and that on November 9, 2022, "We no longer require you to register your applications with OpenAI. Instead, we'll be using a combination of automated and manual methods to monitor for policy violations." The 2026 requirement is narrower than the app review regime OpenAI dropped, since it applies only to services that pass access on to third parties, but for that group it restores a wait for OpenAI's sign-off before operating.
Routers have become a meaningful part of how AI models are consumed. In August, data drawn from OpenRouter showed AI agents using nearly five times as many tokens per task as human users, with agent usage up about 14 times since February 2026 and more than 85% of agent token spend going to cached prompts. A platform that aggregates that volume across providers stands between the model provider and the end customer, and an approval requirement gives OpenAI a view of who those platforms are. OpenAI's documents do not state a rationale for this section.
OpenAI is not alone in tightening control over how access to its models is passed on. When Anthropic's rewritten Usage Policy took effect on November 12, 2026, it added a prohibition on reselling, proxying or otherwise providing access to Claude through unauthorized means, naming services that route requests through consumer subscriptions or misrepresent which product or client is in use.
How the approval provisions are structured
According to the policy text, the new approval mechanisms work in six parts.
- Scope. The access rule applies to anyone who operates "a model-access intermediary platform or other service that enables third parties to directly access OpenAI models."
- Carve-out. The requirement "does not apply solely because an application integrates OpenAI models to power its features or functionality."
- Requirement. Operators in scope "must obtain our prior review and approval." The policy gives no application route, form or review time.
- Separate exception route. Under the heading "OpenAI-approved use," the policy states that "OpenAI may grant specific partners case-by-case exceptions to certain restrictions in these Usage Policies." It names the factors reviewed: "the partner, proposed use, applicable safeguards, and the partner's compliance commitments."
- Terms of an exception. "These uses may require written terms and apply only to the approved partner, use, and conditions." The policy's worked example is national security, intelligence and law enforcement.
- Existing contracts. According to OpenAI's help notice, existing agreements and express permissions in them continue to apply. The training section separately states that its restrictions "do not prohibit uses expressly permitted by your applicable Agreement with OpenAI, including any Permitted Exception under the Services Agreement."
Training rival models
The longest new section opens with a rationale, which is unusual for this document. "Capable models must have safeguards proportionate to the risks they pose before they are deployed," it reads. "Training another model on OpenAI outputs can transfer capabilities without carrying those safeguards over."
The operative prohibition follows: "You must not use OpenAI models or their outputs to generate, prepare, select, or supply training material or signals for models that are intended as alternatives to or substitutes for OpenAI models or services." The policy then lists what that covers, in three items:
- "Creating or preparing training data, training tasks, rubrics, or reinforcement-learning environments."
- "Providing feedback used in training, including labels, scores, rankings, preferences, rewards, or corrections."
- "Selecting, filtering, or validating training data, tasks, or trajectories."
The list reads like an inventory of modern post-training practice. Rubrics, preference rankings, rewards and reinforcement-learning environments are the raw materials of the techniques AI labs use to refine models after pre-training, and trajectories are the recorded step-by-step runs of agents. The policy reaches not just the generation of training data but its selection, filtering and validation, which means a model used purely as a judge of someone else's data falls inside the text.
The reach extends beyond the party that trains the model. "These restrictions apply whether you perform the work yourself, for another party, or through someone acting on your behalf, even if you do not train the resulting model," the policy states. "They also apply when outputs from product use or evaluation are later used or supplied for those training purposes." On a plain reading, that covers data labeling contractors, synthetic data vendors and consultants who never touch the final model, as well as outputs collected during ordinary product use and stored for later.
There are limits in both directions. "You may use OpenAI models for research, software development, model evaluation and benchmarking, and product development, within these boundaries," the section states, and the contract exception covers uses expressly permitted by an agreement with OpenAI. The phrase "Permitted Exception under the Services Agreement" is capitalized as a defined term, but the usage policy does not define it.
Nor does the policy define "alternatives to or substitutes for OpenAI models or services." Read broadly, the phrase could reach a smaller in-house model trained on GPT outputs to take over a task that a company previously sent to OpenAI's API. Read narrowly, it covers competing general-purpose models. The text does not settle which reading OpenAI intends.
Protected reasoning
A single sentence addresses a newer concern. "You must not attempt to extract protected reasoning through unauthorized access, reverse engineering, or circumvention of safeguards," the policy states. The term "protected reasoning" is not defined. Placed inside the training section, it most plausibly refers to the intermediate reasoning that OpenAI's reasoning models produce before an answer and that the company does not show in full, though the policy does not say so.
