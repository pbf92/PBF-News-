TITLE: GTA 6’s Criminal Profile Isn’t a Morality Spectrum – It’s an RPG Class System
OUTLET: IGN Nordic

GTA 6’s Criminal Profile Isn’t a Morality Spectrum – It’s an RPG Class System
Its about what type of criminal you are, not how evil your actions are.
This is an opinion piece from writer Samuel Horti. IGN's opinion columns represent the personal viewpoints of the writer, drawing on their own experiences and research to explore the exciting world of video games.
GTA 6 is a tender love story about a couple clinging to each other through chaos, about the impossible lengths people go for their families, about second chances and forgiveness, about the small moments of romance – watching TV entwined on the sofa, holding hands on the beach, an unexpected compliment – in which relationships bloom.
It is also a game where you can grab a bystander with a digger's claw and squeeze until their head pops.
Modern GTA games have sometimes struggled to square attempts at serious protagonists, or at least protagonists with any depth or humanity, with their inevitable open-world crime sprees. It's hard to take a potentially poignant cut-scene seriously when you've just mowed down 25 pedestrians with a machine gun, and pelted another dozen with RPGs.
GTA 6's main characters, Lucia and Jason, are the deepest, most human protagonists in the series so far. They are relatable and grounded, kind and loyal, silly in spurts. The early goings task Jason with fixing up his leaking, ramshackle house in service of Lucia, so she has something resembling a home to return to from prison – later, they hear a friend is grieving and immediately rush to his side for his favorite activity, a fishing trip.
You'd think, to make that work, Rockstar would need you to be a nice, moral person in the open world. After all, this GTA is clearly more concerned with consequences than ever before: its criminal profile system ranks you from "professional" to "psycho" depending on your aggression, and appears to want to constrain your most perverse instincts. Too much random killing makes you "irredeemable", with permanent narrative and gameplay consequences.
But I've been playing a pair of criminals who, while not savage psychopaths, are violent at the first opportunity. And I've discovered that GTA 6 not only allows this, but wallows in it – and yet somehow steel feels narratively coherent.
See, I don't think criminal profile is a morality system, as others have suggested. I think it's actually a role-playing class system. Its different levels are archetypes, suggestions for you to follow, or to pick and choose your behaviour from – the fact that you can reach psychopathic levels of violence is implicit permission to act out and still rationalize your actions within the game's framework.
The criminal profile's different levels are role-playing game archetypes to pick and choose your behaviour from.
About 35 hours in, my Jason and Lucia are both "violent", although Jason has see-sawed in and out of "psycho". I've got there without rampaging through the streets, and I've even opted for stealth whenever possible (albeit, silent headshots instead of non-lethal chokes).
They are, however, utterly ruthless.
Any spark of trouble is snuffed out by a bullet from one of their pistols. Insults are punishable by death. A man who follows Lucia into an alleyway, leering and asking where the nearest ATM is, gets a bullet between the eyes before he finishes his question. A woman who pulls out a baseball bat after a car crash is promptly deleted. Criminals breaking into a secure van find themselves robbed and murdered.
It's headshots whenever possible, and enemies who are incapacitated by bullets to their limbs are executed when they're on the floor, John Wick style (Rockstar built animations specifically for this).
I've never once felt judged or penalised for my actions. It actually feels like Rockstar gives you both the opportunity and the provocation to be a monster: random NPCs are more aggressive, and some will even start shooting if you crash into them. I've watched somebody break into my car's trunk, I've been hauled off my bike by a pair of criminals, and seen another duo fleeing a robbery, carrying a tempting bag of cash.
In all these situations, and in combat in general, I feel like the fastest, easiest, and most lucrative course of action is a headshot, usually delivered in slow-mo "focus" mode and sometimes followed by a grisly, cinematic close-up of the impact.
I like that Rockstar encourages you to lean into your chosen roleplay by rewarding you with bonus focus. "Professional" or "hardened" criminals gain focus for non-lethal takedowns, violent or psycho players for headshots. Your actions snowball: once you've gone violent, you may as well keep shooting to kill.
And I don't feel a huge disconnect between cutscenes and the open world. If you want them to, Jason and Lucia make sense as hyperviolent people, at least as the story develops. They're a couple who need as much money as they can grab, as quickly as possible. They've been buffeted and bruised by the world around them, wronged at every turn. They know they can only trust each other. Screw everyone else.
Jason's bad temper is hinted at within the first 30 minutes – "dude, you're getting that crazy look in your eye" – and later he says he's more comfortable with a gun in his hand than being social. Lucia doesn't start out violent, but it's feasible that the weight of responsibility and spending time with Jason makes her so.
I can't tell if it's just clever writing, a chameleon script that conforms to whatever imagined backstory you put behind it, or whether I'm getting loads of custom dialogue, but my roleplaying just seems to fit with their chitter-chatter.
I know dialogue adapts to your choices and that the ending alters based on your criminal profile so I'm half-worried that all my murders, and the odd slain civilian, will ruin my story.
But that's a problem for future Lucia and Jason. For now, I've got heads to pop.
