TITLE: Vital to survival, missing from policy: why working donkeys are being overlooked
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

New research from a British Academy-funded project led by the University of Portsmouth in collaboration with Maasai Mara University in Kenya and Haramaya University in Ethiopia, has found that working donkeys in East Africa are routinely overlooked in drought planning, veterinary services and emergency relief, despite their importance in transporting water, food and other essential supplies.
The study was published in the Journal of Rural Studies and included a review of 77 national and international policy documents, of which only 17 mentioned domestic equids, the animal group that includes donkeys, horses, and mules. The researchers found that donkeys were rarely recognised for their contribution to community resilience and were often considered in economic terms rather than in relation to their welfare needs.
Interviews with 41 government officials, veterinarians, staff from non-governmental organisations and community representatives revealed that donkeys are also routinely excluded from veterinary education, health infrastructure and drought relief programmes.
The researchers warn that this lack of recognition leaves animals that communities depend on without adequate support, potentially undermining people’s ability to cope with increasingly severe droughts.
During drought, donkeys can provide an important source of income for families whose livelihoods are under pressure. When water sources dry up, donkeys can travel long distances to collect water for people, crops and other livestock. They also carry animal feed, transport belongings, help people reach healthcare facilities and aid distribution centres, and support access to markets.
East Africa experienced severe drought between 2020 and 2023, bringing widespread crop failures, major livestock losses in some areas, food insecurity and conflict over dwindling resources.
Despite the importance of working donkeys during such crises, drought responses and agricultural policies tend to prioritise animals valued for food production, such as cattle. The study identifies several reasons for this gap, including the limited recognition of donkeys’ contributions, a lack of data on their economic value and livestock systems that do not adequately account for working animals.
The findings build on earlier University of Portsmouth research, published in ‘Anthrozoös’ in June 2026, which explored the wider social and cultural importance of donkeys in drought-prone communities in Kenya and Ethiopia.
That study found that donkeys are far more than working animals. They help sustain nomadic lifestyles, strengthen social networks through sharing and borrowing, and preserve cultural identity and traditions.
Together, the studies highlight how overlooking donkeys can have consequences for both animal welfare and human livelihoods. When donkeys become sick, injured or are lost, the consequences can extend to families who rely on them to transport essential supplies and earn a living.
Professor Leanne Proops, Centre for Comparative and Evolutionary Psychology at the University of Portsmouth, said: “Working donkeys are a lifeline for many communities during drought, helping people access water, transport essential supplies and sustain their livelihoods. Yet their contribution is largely overlooked in the policies and support systems designed to help communities cope with these crises."
Dr Emily Haddy, formerly of the University of Portsmouth, now at the University of Nottingham said: “Recognising donkeys in livestock and disaster planning is not just an animal welfare issue. By including working donkeys in drought preparedness and emergency responses, governments and aid organisations can help protect animal welfare and strengthen community resilience.”
Professor Tesfaheywet Zeryehun, Haramaya University, notes: “Our findings reinforce the fact that donkey welfare cannot be separated from human wellbeing. In drought-prone communities, protecting working donkeys is equally about protecting the livelihoods, resilience and dignity of the families who depend on them. Their contribution must therefore be recognised in policies and emergency preparedness programmes.”
The latest study calls for governments and organisations to explicitly include equids in livestock definitions and in agricultural and disaster risk management frameworks. This would help ensure that working donkeys are eligible for animal health initiatives, feed and other support during drought.
Other recommendations include integrating donkey health and welfare into veterinary training, improving enforcement of animal welfare laws, collecting better data on the contribution working donkeys make to household incomes and community resilience, and investing in drought preparedness before emergencies occur.
Dr David Obiero, Maasai Mara University states: “The lack of recognition of working donkeys extends beyond policy into veterinary training, access to healthcare and vaccination programmes. These animals are vital to community livelihoods, yet the systems designed to support livestock often overlook them. Addressing these gaps is essential to supporting pastoralist communities during time of drought and beyond.”
