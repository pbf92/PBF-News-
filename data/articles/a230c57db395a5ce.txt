TITLE: Live event ad break order
OUTLET: PPC Land

Live event ad break order is a reporting dimension in Amazon Ads unified reporting that records where a commercial break fell within a live event: the first break of a game, the second, the fifth, and so on. Each impression served into live sports or event inventory can be grouped by that sequence number, so delivery, spend and outcomes can be compared break by break. It exists because a live broadcast is not uniform inventory: a break in the opening minutes of a close game and one late in a one-sided contest can carry very different audiences.
Where the field sits
The dimension belongs to the Supply category of Amazon's unified reporting reference, which PPC Land counted at 156 attributes across 11 categories after Amazon updated the page on November 11, 2026. Nine of those attributes describe live events: property, genre, content provider, ID, name, inventory type, ad break order, ad slot order and ad slot type. PPC Land described ad break order as the order of the break within the event. Six of the nine trace their former names to a "Live Event report" in Amazon DSP (demand-side platform), the programmatic buying tool Amazon renamed DVA, for display, video and audio, on September 29, 2026.
Of the 35 Supply attributes, 34 carry the DVA+ marker and only one or two apply to each sponsored ads product, according to PPC Land's count. Live event fields therefore describe impressions bought through Amazon's DVA products, such as streaming TV line items, rather than Sponsored Products or Sponsored Brands. Where a dimension does not apply, Amazon's reference states, "its value appears as null."
How it works
A live stream on Prime Video, Twitch or a third-party sports app is carried as a continuous video feed. When the production truck cuts to commercials, the broadcaster inserts a signal into the stream, usually an SCTE-35 cue (a standard from the Society of Cable Telecommunications Engineers). PPC Land's SSAI explainer describes the mechanism: "A cue-out declares the timestamp and planned duration of a break; a cue-in marks the return." In live streams the cue arrives only seconds before the splice point.
Each cue-out opens a break. Counting the breaks from the start of the event gives the break order. The ads inside one break form an ad pod, and each position within that pod is a slot. Amazon reports the two levels separately: ad break order for the sequence of breaks, ad slot order for the position inside a break.
A worked example shows the grain. Suppose a football game produces 20 commercial breaks, each holding four 30-second slots. An advertiser whose line item ran across that game could pull a report by Live event name and Live event ad break order and see impressions and spend for breaks one through 20. Adding Live event ad slot order splits each row again into slots one through four.
The field reports what happened; it is not a targeting control. Amazon's Live Events Optimizer, a streaming TV media strategy in Amazon DSP launched in late 2025, paces budget across "scheduled event windows based on viewership patterns rather than evenly across calendar days", according to Amazon's launch page. Buyers choose from deals Amazon has certified as live events content. The launch page names no setting for buying a specific break number. The break order dimension shows the outcome of that automated pacing after the fact.
The protocol beneath it
The industry-standard version of the same idea lives in OpenRTB, the IAB Tech Lab's real-time bidding protocol. OpenRTB 2.6, whose base version is labelled 2.6-202210 in the specification's change log, added a set of fields "for pod bidding support" to the video and audio objects. The field closest to break order is podseq, defined as "the sequence (position) of the video ad pod within a content stream." Its values come from the AdCOM 1.0 list "Pod Sequence": 1 for the first pod in the content stream, -1 for the last pod and 0 for any pod.
The slot-level equivalent is slotinpod, with the same three values applied to positions inside a pod: first ad, last ad, or any ad. A shared podid string tells the buyer that several impression opportunities in one bid request belong to the same break. Other fields, such as poddur and mincpmpersec, set a break's fillable seconds and a per-second floor price. A field called rqddurs lists the exact creative durations accepted, and the specification states that it "specifically targets the Live TV use case where non-exact ad durations would result in undesirable 'dead air'."
The OpenRTB field signals only first, last or any. Amazon's reporting field records a full sequence, which is possible because a report sees the whole event after it ends, while a bidder sees one break at a time.
Origin and evolution
Break position predates programmatic: television sales teams have long sold "first in break" spots at a premium. PPC Land's CTV explainer notes that buyers could not bid for a specific position within a pod and publishers could not set floors by slot until OpenRTB 2.6, which was developed from spring 2021 and opened for public comment until February 7, 2022. Digiday has described pod bidding as an OpenRTB standard introduced in 2022.
At Amazon, the history runs through live sports rights. Prime Video began streaming Thursday Night Football exclusively in 2022. In January 2024 Amazon moved all Prime Video subscriptions to an ad-supported default. On July 9, 2024 Amazon opened live event open auction supply in Amazon DSP to self-service advertisers in the United States, Canada and Mexico, with "Live event inventory... selected by default for any newly created video ad line." Reporting then sat under a single "Live event" dimension in the DSP Report Center. Amazon's NBA coverage began with the 2025-26 season, and an Amazon news post dated October 24, 2025 described NBA League Pass inventory as "Amazon's first 100% programmatic sports offering."
Unified reporting entered beta on November 11, 2025 and reached general availability on June 8, 2026. The single live event dimension became nine named fields, ad break order among them, in the reference updated on November 11, 2026.
Why it matters
Live sports carries much of Amazon's premium video revenue. Prime Video's 2025 Thursday Night Football season averaged 15.3 million viewers, according to Amazon citing Nielsen, and Amazon's guide to 23 live events in November 2026 reported an average of 17.7 million through the first six games of the 2026 season. Alan Moss, Amazon Ads vice president of global advertising sales, has said the NFL deal brought 80 net new advertisers, as PPC Land reported when Moss described Prime Video and live sports as one business.
Because audience size moves during a game, break order lets a buyer test whether early, late or overtime breaks delivered differently, and check whether automated pacing concentrated spend in low-value parts of an event. For sellers, it creates the record needed to justify position-based pricing.
Limitations and disputes
Public documentation is thin. Value format, the treatment of halftime and overtime, and whether an unsold break increments the count are not documented publicly.
Comparability is weak across events. Break three in a basketball game, with frequent timeouts, is not the same moment as break three in a football broadcast, so reports grouped only by break order can mislead unless the event is held constant.
The value of position is also contested. TVision, an attention measurement firm, found that first-in-pod ads drew attention about 5 percentage points higher on linear TV, but the benefit was smaller on connected TV (CTV), and attention by break position within a program barely moved: 36.1 per cent at the beginning, 36.0 per cent in the middle and 36.6 per cent at the end. Index Exchange, in an undated explainer video, called the first and last breaks in a show and the first and last slots in a break "premium positions." The two views do not align neatly.
Adoption is the final gap. OpenRTB pod fields are optional for media owners. Rob Hazan, now general manager of product management at The Trade Desk, told Digiday in April 2026 that the company expected its entire publisher supply path to support pod bidding by August 31, 2026, and that "structured" pods, where each slot can be priced, were still coming.
Commonly confused terms
Live event ad slot order. The position of an ad inside a single break. Break order counts breaks; slot order counts ads within one.
Ad break position. A separate Amazon video dimension that classifies a break as beginning (pre-roll), middle (mid-roll) or end (post-roll) of the content. It applies to any video, not only live events, and has three values rather than a sequence.
Live event ad slot type. Defined in Amazon's reference as "the sales model for the live-event ad slot", describing how a slot was sold rather than where it sat.
Ad load. The total quantity of advertising per unit of content, usually minutes per hour, as set out in PPC Land's ad load explainer. Break order describes sequence, not volume.
Recent developments
The nine live event fields became visible in Amazon's reference update of November 11, 2026, and PPC Land's count on November 12 placed them among the 59 dimensions that apply only to DVA+. Legacy Amazon DSP report pages, including the old live event tables, become read-only on December 17, 2026 and shut down on January 31, 2027, according to Amazon's migration notice. Break structure itself is under debate: an IAB Tech Lab member post by InTheGame chief executive Aviram Sharon argued for fewer, shorter CTV breaks.
