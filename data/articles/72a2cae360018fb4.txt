TITLE: Israeli researchers uncover 15,000-year-old clay figurines, the earliest in Southwest Asia
OUTLET: The Jerusalem Post

Natufian clay figurines unearthed from archaeological sites across Israel have pushed back the timeline of the clay figurine-making tradition in Southwest Asia by as much as 3,000 years, according to a new study.
Published by researchers from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, the study analyzed 154 Natufian clay figurines unearthed at Eynan-Mallaha in the Upper Jordan Valley, Hayonim Terrace in the Galilee and el-Wad Terrace on Mount Carmel.
Fifteen of the figurines are recognizable, while the other 139 are cylindrical fragments believed to have once been parts of figures.
The Natufians, who lived in the Levant from roughly 15,000 to 11,650 years ago, were the first people in Southwest Asia to establish permanent or semi-permanent settlements.
As part of the study, researchers carefully re-examined previously overlooked clay fragments found at the sites. Through careful analysis, they identified fingerprints, tool marks, and traces of pigment that revealed deliberate artistic work.
“These discoveries change how we understand the beginnings of artistic expression in this region,” said Dr. Laurent Davin, who led the study alongside Prof. Leore Grosman and Prof. Anna Belfer-Cohen of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. “Thousands of years before agriculture emerged, people were already using clay to create figures, as part of their beliefs and knowledge of the natural world.”
Fingerprints of the young
Perhaps the most notable finding of the study concerns who made the figurines.
Using microscopic analysis to study the fingerprints preserved in the clay, researchers found that both adults and children helped shape and decorate the objects.
Some of the fingerprints found on the clay belonged to children who were most likely younger than 10 years old, offering rare insight into childhood and learning in prehistoric societies.
On one human-shaped figurine, researchers identified two overlapping fingerprints: one left by a young adult or adult woman, the other by a child who had not yet reached puberty.
According to researchers, the children’s marks were no accident.
Rather, the fingerprints suggest children were likely asked to help shape and even color the figurines, which may have been a way to pass on skills and cultural knowledge between generations.
“What is particularly fascinating is the evidence that children were involved in making these objects,” Davin said, “suggesting that creativity and cultural knowledge were shared across generations.”
Animals, beginnings of storytelling
Of the 15 identifiable figurines, the majority (11) depict birds. The rest include a mammal, a gazelle, and two human forms.
Some birds are rendered as simple shapes, while others have carefully marked wings, tails, and feathers. Several appear to be waterfowl, and others may be birds of prey.
Some of the birds are also shown with their heads tilted downward and turned to the left, a posture researchers believe may represent the courtship displays of ducks during breeding season.
If so, there is the possibility that the figurines served as more than decoration and could have been used to convey knowledge about animal behavior and the changing seasons, and may have played a part in storytelling and tradition.
Researchers also found traces of red coloring on several figurines, including evidence of engobe, a technique the involves applying a thin layer of colored clay. One figurine, dated to 15,000 years ago, provides what the study identifies as the earliest known evidence of this coloring technique.

Valla from the CNRS and Center de recherche français à Jérusalem.
