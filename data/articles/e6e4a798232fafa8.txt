TITLE: Tano raises $4M for programmable creator advertising
OUTLET: Quasa.io

Tano raises $4M to turn creator ads into a programmable channel
London-based Tano raised a $4 million seed round led by Cherry Ventures and announced it on November 11, 2026, bringing its total funding to $5 million, according to Tech Funding News’s account of the deal. The larger figure includes earlier backing from Seedcamp and The Players Fund; it is not the size of the new round. Tano plans to spend the capital on computing, engineers and creator strategists.
Founded by Will Caplan and Sagar Shah, Tano asks a brand for a budget and commercial objective, then uses software and people to recruit creators, coordinate their work and connect campaign activity to sales. Caplan told Tech.eu, “Brands should be able to set a commercial objective and invest predictably into creator advertising, just like they invest in Meta or Google.” That is the platform’s ambition, rather than a demonstrated guarantee of predictable returns.
Where Tano automates campaign work
The clearest automation is in the administrative chain between a brief and a published post. In Seedcamp’s description of the platform, agents search more than 280 million creator profiles, assess content and audience comments, recruit prospects, manage contracts and payments, monitor publication, and measure clicks and sales. The investor describes human creator strategists overseeing that work. Tano is offering an operated campaign system, with staff as well as software, rather than a tool brands run entirely on their own.
Product seeding, creator communications and rewards are also part of the workflow. Tracking links, discount codes and commerce integrations can associate posts with purchases; campaign data then informs later creator recommendations. Selected posts can be used in paid advertising when permissions have been agreed. The attraction for a brand is continuity: another campaign can draw on records of which creators responded, posted and generated attributed sales, instead of treating every partnership as an isolated transaction.
Those stages call for different kinds of judgement. Searching profiles and sending routine messages can be repeated at scale, while agreeing terms or approving the reuse of a creator’s work requires attention to the individual deal. A purchase recorded against a code is also a narrower result than an estimate of sales caused by the campaign. Tano’s proposed advertising channel depends on coordinating these stages, but they cannot be reduced to one interchangeable measure of success.
Creator strategists remain in the operation
The plan to hire creator strategists is as revealing as the plan to hire engineers. Creators supply the advertising itself, bringing their own audiences, schedules and negotiating positions. A missed delivery, disputed payment or request to turn an organic post into a paid ad needs a route to someone who can resolve it. The staffing plan points to a continuing service component even as agents take on more repetitive work.
For brands, that distinction affects what can be scaled. Automated outreach could make smaller creator deals less costly to administer, but the volume of invitations alone says little about whether the matches are appropriate or the terms are understood. For creators, the relevant question is who can change a brief or payment arrangement when the standard process does not fit. Human strategists are part of Tano’s stated model; their workload as campaign volume grows will affect its economics.
Two productivity estimates cover different work
The published productivity example concerns a particular programme, and its manual-work estimates use different scopes. Cherry Ventures’ investment note says agents sourced and validated 2,358 creators with two hours of input from a brand expert, against Tano’s estimate of 754 hours for that sourcing work by hand. This compares discovery and validation time, not the entire cost of running a campaign.
Tano’s own announcement uses the same 2,358-creator count and two-hour input but gives a broader comparison: 87 expert hours for the campaign against an estimated 1,751 manual hours. It also names HelloFresh, Skin+Me and Bloom & Wild among customer examples. The shared inputs suggest that the two accounts describe the same programme, while the manual estimates refer to different bodies of work. Neither account publishes an independent audit or the method used to construct its estimated manual workload.
The customer examples describe improvements in conversion, creator-driven revenue and click-through rates, but their public descriptions do not provide full comparison periods, attribution rules or campaign costs. A tracked purchase can be assigned to a creator through a link or code; that record alone cannot show whether the customer would have bought anyway. The distinction matters because predictable advertising requires evidence of additional sales as well as a reliable way to process more partnerships.
What the funding puts to the test
The investment gives Tano resources to expand both sides of its model: the software that searches, communicates and records campaign results, and the people who manage creator relationships. Whether the service becomes more economical at greater scale depends partly on how its computing and staffing needs change as it takes on more campaigns. The funding establishes capacity to pursue that model, while the campaign examples leave its costs and returns open to measurement.
More revealing results would pair sales outcomes with human hours, creator payments and permissions for paid reuse. Those details would show whether a growing programme becomes easier to run while preserving the individual agreements on which creator advertising depends.
