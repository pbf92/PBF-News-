TITLE: Kenyan clinic Nutrihealth wins rehearing of KSh 1.25m CCTV privacy award
OUTLET: PPC Land

Kenya's High Court has set aside a KSh 1,250,000 compensation award that the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC) ordered a health clinic to pay a patient who found a CCTV camera mounted in its examination room and pointed at the table where she had undressed for an ultrasound. In a judgment in Nutrihealth Solutions Limited v G.W., Civil Appeal No. E481 of 2024, Justice W.F. Andayi of the Milimani High Court's Civil Appellate Division found that the complaint had never been heard, declared the Data Commissioner's decision invalid and sent the case back for a fresh hearing. The enforcement notice that ordered the clinic to take the camera down stays in force.
The judgment carries a delivery date of November 12, and a second finding in it reaches well beyond one clinic. Section 65 of Kenya's Data Protection Act entitles a person harmed by a breach to compensation. The Act, the judge wrote, never says who decides whether that harm occurred, and it does not hand that job to the Data Commissioner. The power to order compensation sits in a 2021 regulation, not in the statute, and the court said the validity of that regulation has to be tested in judicial review or a constitutional petition, with the Attorney General heard.
In Short
A Kenyan judge cancelled a KSh 1.25 million payout that the country's data protection regulator had ordered a clinic to pay a patient filmed by a camera in an examination room, because the regulator decided the case without giving either side a hearing. The ruling matters to every company that handles personal data in Kenya, because the judge also said the Data Protection Act does not expressly allow the regulator to decide whether someone suffered damage, a power it had used in 184 compensation orders by January 2026, according to Capital FM. The clinic still has to comply with the order to remove the camera, the complaint goes back to the regulator on January 21, 2027, and the bigger question of the regulator's power to award money is left for a separate court case.
The camera in the examination room
The facts come from the judgment's summary of the record. On May 5, 2023, the respondent, identified in the judgment as G.W., attended Nutrihealth Solutions Limited's health facility for an ultrasound procedure. She had to undress and lie on an examination table. When the procedure ended, she saw a CCTV camera mounted in the room "and pointed directly towards the examination table."
The sonographer told her the camera was not working. A doctor at the clinic then told her it was there "only for monitoring purposes without recording," according to the judgment, and declined her request to see the playback of the room for the time of her examination.
She reported the matter at Parklands police station on May 12, 2023, under reference OD 36/12/5/2023. When she returned with a police officer, the clinic refused the officer access to the footage and to the examination room. The police then went to court. In Kibera Magistrates Court Miscellaneous Criminal Case No. E260 of 2023, a police corporal obtained orders to access, inspect and carry away the clinic's "CCTV DVR KIT and components" and the footage of the examination room between 16:00 and 18:00 on May 5, 2023.
The DVR went to the forensic laboratory of the Directorate of Criminal Investigations. Its report, as the judgment records it, found that the footage for the specific hour she was at the clinic "had been overwritten."
That single forensic finding is read two ways in the appeal. For the clinic, it showed that no data about the complainant was held. Its counsel argued that the storage media had been retrieved and that the forensic report confirmed it "did not hold any of the complainant's data." For the complainant, the point was the camera itself and the "lifelong trauma of knowing that there was a possibility that unauthorized parties may have access to footage she did not consent to," as the judgment summarises her complaint.
What the Data Commissioner ordered
She complained to the ODPC on December 11, 2023, according to the first paragraph of the judgment; the complaint was registered as No. 2556 of 2023. Data Commissioner Immaculate Kassait found the clinic liable, ordered it to pay KShs 1,250,000 as compensation and directed that an enforcement notice be issued. The judgment dates the determination both March 8 and March 13, 2024. The enforcement notice is dated March 8, 2024, and was delivered on March 19, 2024.
The enforcement notice imposed six requirements.
Several of those obligations will be familiar to any data controller working under European rules. The data protection impact assessment and data protection by design duties in items iii and v have direct counterparts in the GDPR, and the "health data" at issue sits in the Kenyan equivalent of what European law calls special category data. Section 2 of Kenya's Act, as the respondent's counsel set out, defines health data to include "data collected in the course of registration for, or provision of health services."
The clinic's appeal
Nutrihealth appealed through an amended memorandum of appeal dated February 13, 2025. The judgment says it rests on seven grounds and lists six. In summary, the clinic argued that:
- the Commissioner wrongly found a breach of the rights of access and erasure when, on the clinic's case, it held no data about the complainant;
- the finding that it processed and stored her data without consent conflicted with forensic reports indicating that no data was collected or stored;
- the finding of liability under the Act was wrong in law and fact;
- the compensation award, made together with the enforcement notice under Regulation 14(3)(e) and section 58, was an error of law;
- compensation is "a judicial function not provided for in the Data Protection Act"; and
- the Commissioner "does not have judicial powers to order compensation" and Regulation 14(3)(e) is "contra statute with the Parent Act and Article 159 (1)" of the Constitution, which vests judicial authority in courts and tribunals.
Both sides filed written submissions dated April 22, 2025. Counsel for the clinic argued that the right of access under section 26(b) applies only where a controller has collected and stored data, that the burden of proof lay on the complainant, and that the regulator "failed to draw a distinction between the mere presence of surveillance infrastructure and the actual processing or misuse of personal data."
On the power to award money, the clinic's counsel drew on two other African statutes. Under Nigeria's data protection law, counsel submitted, section 48 "expressly vests the commission with power to award compensation." Under section 99(4) of South Africa's data protection law, by contrast, compensation is pursued "as a civil remedy before a court of competent jurisdiction." Kenya's Act, the argument ran, contains neither an express grant like Nigeria's nor a court route like South Africa's, and the gap had been filled by regulation.
Counsel for the complainant relied on Article 31 of the Constitution, which guarantees the right to privacy, and on sections 25, 26, 30 and 46 of the Act: transparency, informing data subjects before processing, consent, and processing sensitive data in line with the constitutional right to privacy. On the regulator's power, counsel pointed to section 65, Regulations 14(2) and 14(3), and the High Court's decision in Muthoni v Solpia Kenya Limited t/a Sista Kenya [2025] KEHC 34 (KLR), which held that it was within the Data Commissioner's jurisdiction to grant an award of compensation.
What the court decided
The appeal succeeded in part. The court's orders, and what each left open, break down as follows.
A determination without a hearing
The decisive point was procedural. Quoting the determination itself, the court noted that the Commissioner had written: "This determination is therefore as a result of the complaint as received, the response by the Respondent and investigations conducted by the Office." No hearing of the parties' competing claims on compensation took place.
"I highly doubt that Parliament would have contemplated reposing such judicial or even quasi-judicial authority in the Data Commissioner to be exercised without affording the contesting parties the right to a hearing," Justice Andayi wrote. "Even if a hearing would be conducted, I again doubt that it would be by the same Data Commissioner to be the one investigating, then prosecuting it and then making a determination. That would be against all the rules of natural justice."
The conclusion followed directly. "The failure to follow the above said requisite procedural steps renders the decision of the Data Commissioner invalid," the judgment reads. "Noting that the matter did not proceed through a hearing yet the appellant was condemned to pay compensation, I find that this was a procedural error that needs to be rectified."
Using the appellate powers in section 78(1) of the Civil Procedure Act, which include remanding a case and ordering a new trial, the judge sent the complaint back to the Commissioner "to conduct a hearing of the parties on issues framed and then make a determination."
Compensation: in the regulations, not in the Act
The second strand of the judgment reaches further. The judge walked through the Act's enforcement architecture and described the Commissioner's powers as administrative: investigating complaints, issuing enforcement notices under section 58, issuing penalty notices under section 62, and imposing administrative fines under section 9(1)(f). Section 63 caps a penalty at five million shillings or, for an undertaking, one per cent of the previous financial year's annual turnover, whichever is lower.
Section 65, which grants the right to compensation, comes after the appeal provision in section 64. "This section is silent on who should make a decision regarding compensation," the judgment says. "The Act has no provision for the Data Commissioner to make a 'determination' on infringement of the rights of a data subject." Unlike the sections on enforcement and penalty notices, "there is no express provision under this section donating powers to the Data Commissioner to award the said compensation to an aggrieved person."
The court leaned on a 2026 Court of Appeal decision on statutory jurisdiction, Republic v Retirement Benefits Appeal Tribunal & 2 others [2026] KECA 849 (KLR), which held that a grant of jurisdiction to a tribunal "must, in fact, be an express conferment and not a matter of implication since a tribunal, being a creature of statute, has only such jurisdiction as has been specifically conferred upon it by the statute."
The regulations point the other way. Regulation 14(3) of the complaints regulations lists "an order for compensation to the data subject by the respondent" among the remedies a determination may include, and Regulation 14(5) makes a decision "binding on the parties" and enforceable "as an order of the Court." The clinic's argument that this was ultra vires, the judge found, "is not without merit." The judgment's summary on the point is narrow but pointed: "determination of whether or not a person has suffered damage as a result of the contravention has not been expressly assigned to the Data Commissioner by the Act. The issue needs to be considered further through an appropriate application due to its ramifications."
The judge declined to decide it. The issue had not been before the Commissioner, an appeal is too limited a forum, and a challenge to the validity of the regulations should come "either through a Judicial Review application or as a constitutional petition... where full arguments by parties are made including from the Attorney General."
