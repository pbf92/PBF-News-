TITLE: Fewer yeses, no more noes: EU cookie consent falls to 65.2%
OUTLET: PPC Land

Two in three tracking events from European Union visitors carried full cookie consent in the first nine months of 2026, and the share slid almost every month, according to a benchmark published on November 4, 2026 by TAGGRS, a company that sells hosting for server-side Google Tag Manager. Built on 17.4 billion Google Analytics 4 events from visitors in the 27 member states, the study puts the average full consent rate at 68.8% for January to September, falling from 71.3% in January to 65.2% in September. More people clicking reject did not cause the decline. Active refusals fell.
TAGGRS promoted the study on LinkedIn today. A LinkedIn post by Małgorzata Poddębniak, the TAGGRS content marketer who wrote the benchmark, summarised the headline figures and presented the study as the opening piece of a larger project. "This benchmark is the first part of our State of Tracking Report 2027," the post reads. "The report combines platform data on consent, ad blockers and browser tracking prevention with input from marketers." The company is collecting that input through a five-minute anonymous survey, offering participants early access to the finished report and entry into a raffle for a €200 Amazon voucher.
The figures are vendor data. They come from the traffic of websites that use TAGGRS, are weighted by event volume, and appear on a page that also markets the company's own dashboards and its free Tracking Checker. That does not make them wrong. It does define what they can and cannot say about the EU as a whole.
In Short
TAGGRS, which hosts tracking infrastructure for websites, looked at 17.4 billion Google Analytics events from EU visitors on its own platform and found that 68.8% carried full cookie consent between January and September 2026, with the monthly figure falling from 71.3% to 65.2%. The decline did not come from more people clicking reject, since active refusals fell, but from partial consent, events with no recorded choice and events sent with no consent signal at all, which matters to anyone whose Google Ads and Analytics data depends on those signals. Because two of those three causes often trace back to how banners and tags are set up, part of the gap may sit in a site's own configuration rather than in visitor behaviour, though the figures are one vendor's traffic-weighted data and not a census of EU websites.
Events, not clicks
Consent benchmarks are not new. Most of them come from consent management platforms, the vendors whose banners collect the choice in the first place. TAGGRS cites three. Didomi reports opt-in rates of 55.7% to 67.6% across European regions for 2025, measured against all banners shown. ConsentStack reports that on the median site among its customers, 72% of visitors who make a choice grant full consent. And etracker puts the German average between 40% and 54%, with the lower end on sites where the reject button is as visible as the accept button. On the published page, the etracker reference links to a page on ignite.video rather than to etracker itself.
Those reports count clicks. The TAGGRS benchmark counts something further downstream: the consent state attached to each GA4 event when it reaches the server, which the company describes as what Google Analytics and Google Ads actually receive. "A click on the banner doesn't help if the consent signal isn't passed on, if a tag fires before the consent update is processed, or if the event is sent without any consent signal," the benchmark states.
The difference is not cosmetic. TAGGRS records a denied-by-default share of 8.0%, far below the 21.7% to 27.4% of visitors who make no choice in Didomi's data. The company's explanation is that visitors who browse longer send more events and are more likely to have made a choice. "Because we count events rather than banner clicks, our numbers aren't directly comparable to CMP benchmarks," the page says. A single committed shopper who accepts and then views twenty products contributes twenty granted events; a visitor who bounces from the first page without touching the banner contributes one.
How the benchmark is built
According to TAGGRS, the methodology rests on six elements.
- Data. Every GA4 event from visitors in the 27 EU member states that reached a TAGGRS server-side container between January 1 and September 30, 2026, a total of 17.4 billion events. "The counts are exact and come from aggregate queries only, so no site, customer or visitor can be identified," the methodology section reads.
- Unit of measurement. Every percentage is a share of events, not visitors. Each event gets exactly one consent state, so the five states add up to 100%. Websites with more traffic therefore weigh more heavily on the averages than smaller ones.
- Classification. Each event is placed in a consent state on the basis of two parameters that Google tags send with every hit, gcs and gcd. The information behind them comes from Google's Consent Mode, which passes the visitor's choices from the banner to Google tags. "Google doesn't publish an official list of the consent codes, so their meanings are based on consent platform documentation," TAGGRS notes.
- Geography. The headline figure and the country figures cover EU visitors only. About a fifth of events carry no location and are left out of the EU and country figures. For some sites, the recorded location can over-represent the Netherlands and Germany. Visitors in the Netherlands account for almost half of the EU events; without them, the EU average is 67.2%.
- Worldwide segments. The device, browser, funnel, consent type and purchase figures cover all TAGGRS traffic worldwide, of which about two-thirds comes from the EU. The device and browser data covers only February 14 to September 30, 2026.
- Bots. "Events from bots are not excluded, so some of the events in each consent state may come from automated traffic," the methodology states.
Five consent states

Two qualifications in the benchmark matter for reading every number that follows. Granted does not always mean the visitor accepted: if a site sets consent to granted before the visitor makes a choice, those events still count as granted. And denied by default has different causes from denied. According to TAGGRS, it can happen when a visitor ignores or closes the banner, leaves before making a choice, or when the banner does not load at all.
Nine months, one direction
The headline benchmark is the January to September average: 68.78% of EU events with full consent, 13.52% actively denied, 7.98% denied by default, 5.25% partially granted and 4.47% not set. Counting partial consent as well, 74.0% of events carried at least some consent.
The monthly series is where the story lies. Full consent held at around 71% in the first quarter, then declined every month from March onward, with the fall speeding up in the second half of the year. Two step changes stand out in the series. Denied by default jumped by more than a point between June and July, from 7.6% to 8.7%. Partial consent jumped by two points between July and August, from 5.2% to 7.2%, more than double its entire net rise from January to July. The benchmark does not comment on either shift, and the data cannot say whether they reflect a handful of large sites changing their banners or a broader pattern. Over the full period, TAGGRS calculates a fall of 6.1 percentage points in nine months.
Refusals fell while unrecorded choices rose
When full consent falls, the missing events have to land in one of the other four states. Active denial was not one of them. It went down, from 13.9% in January to 12.6% in September. The three states that grew were partial consent, up 3.0 points from 4.4% to 7.4%; denied by default, up 2.8 points from 6.9% to 9.7%; and not set, up 1.6 points from 3.6% to 5.2%.
"The drop in full consent isn't caused by more visitors declining," the benchmark states. In the LinkedIn post, Poddębniak put the consequence more directly: "The last two often come down to how the cookie banner and the tags are set up, which means part of the drop can be fixed." The two in question are events with no recorded choice and events sent with no consent signal.
There is a subtler shift inside the denied figures. In January, active denial was twice as common as denied by default. By September it was only 1.3 times as common, 12.6% against 9.7%. Combined into a single "denied" figure, the movement would vanish, because the total barely changed: 20.8% in January, 22.2% in September. A dashboard that merges the two states would show a stable refusal rate while the composition underneath changed considerably.
The benchmark also flags that its figures understate the problem. "These figures only include events that reached a server-side container, so the real share is likely higher," it says. Events stopped in the browser before reaching the container, for instance by an ad blocker or a tag that never fires, are simply not counted.
Sixteen countries, from Romania to Lithuania
National rates vary far more than the EU average suggests. Among the 16 EU countries with the most events in the data, full consent ranged from 50.7% in Romania to 96.3% in Lithuania.
Source: TAGGRS platform data, January to September 2026, by visitor location, websites using TAGGRS only.
Lithuania's figure comes with a warning from the authors themselves. Only 2.1% of Lithuanian events were denied. "Such a high rate may come from a small number of large websites or from websites that set consent to granted before visitors make a choice. The data doesn't tell us which," the benchmark says.
What fills the gap below 100% also differs by country, and the differences point in different directions. Active denial is highest in Italy (21.0%), Denmark (19.7%), Sweden (19.2%) and France (18.8%), countries where visitors more often decline in the banner. Denied by default is highest in Romania (29.3%), Hungary (26.9%) and Ireland (20.9%), where a large share of events carries no recorded choice. Not set is highest in Austria (22.1%) and Germany (10.7%). According to TAGGRS, that last pattern "is more likely related to how consent is set up on the sites than to how visitors behave."
The authors attribute part of the variation to national rules and enforcement, noting that each EU country has its own version of the cookie rules and that data protection authorities move at different speeds. The Dutch authority, they write, has warned more than 200 organisations about misleading banners since 2025. That matters for the benchmark directly, since changes on Dutch websites can move the EU average. The Dutch regulator's cookie work has a longer record: PPC Land reported in July 2025 on the final letters from five Dutch cookie banner investigations, which closed between March 24 and 27, 2025 without fines. Country figures, the page adds, cover only sites that use TAGGRS, and in smaller countries a few large websites can have a big effect on the result.
