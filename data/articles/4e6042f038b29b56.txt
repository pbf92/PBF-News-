TITLE: Can Grand Theft Auto 6 Save the Games Industry?
OUTLET: Boston University

Can Grand Theft Auto 6 Save the Games Industry?
Highly anticipated game arrives at a perilous moment for an industry struggling under its own weight, says BU expert and game designer
Forget about Marvel, Avatar, and Barbenheimer. Grand Theft Auto 6 hasn’t even come out yet, and it’s already primed to be the biggest entertainment launch in history.
The first GTA game in 13 years has already reached $1 billion in sales before it comes out on November 19. Movie studios are even preparing to weather the pop culture earthquake created by Rockstar Games’ latest open world game.
But for all the buzz, conversation, and money (not to mention time off requests) that GTA 6 is going to generate, the industry surrounding it is in dire straits, facing a combination of mass layoffs, the closures of legendary studios, corporate consolidation, and rising prices for games and game consoles.
GTA 6 might be on track to be a record breaking success, but can it save the games industry? Margaret Wallace, a veteran game designer and associate professor of the practice of media innovation at the College of Communication, is skeptical.
So much of our hopes and dreams and aspirations are being linked to this game. I think it will evolve the industry forward just clearly not in its current form
“So much of our hopes and dreams and aspirations are being linked to this game,” Wallace says. “I think it will evolve the industry forward just clearly not in its current form.”
The games industry has changed a lot since GTA transformed games forever in 2001 with GTA 3. There, Rockstar handed over an unprecedented level of control to players, setting them loose in a 3D game world of cars, guns, and gang warfare. Open world games like The Elder Scrolls series, The Witcher 3, and even The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, have become bigger and more popular, but they all still follow in the footsteps of GTA.
“You definitely feel like you have more agency as a player in these Grand Theft Auto games that really were unparalleled and stood out compared to a lot of what is out there,” Wallace says.
With each iteration of Grand Theft Auto , Rockstar has created more detailed, immersive, and technically advanced worlds, all while raising the bar for narrative, writing, and character development. GTA is notorious for pushing boundaries when it comes to violence and sexual content in games, and drawing the attention of parents and politicians as a result.
Wallace admits that the series has never been afraid to push buttons, but she says Rockstar has also used its brand of satire to tackle issues that few developers ever dare touch, such as race, class, immigration, and even the American government’s use of violent interrogation tactics.
“It sort of holds up a mirror to our world, not always in the most positive or flattering or impact-minded way,” Wallace says.
In some ways, Rockstar has grown up, she notes. GTA 6 still has some of the franchise’s tongue-in-cheek attitude, but it’s more grounded, subtle, and focused on relationships over bombast, particularly the Bonnie and Clyde-style couple-on-the-run that players control this time around.
The level of both narrative and technical immersion required to pull that off comes with a cost, though. Rockstar is known for its attention to detail, and the dynamic weather system, hair physics, and all the fully written and animated nonplayer characters that fill GTA 6’s Vice City push that to its limits.
Rockstar is “an absolute anomaly” in terms of how much time and money it can invest in the development of its games, Wallace says. However, the studio and its games have come to represent the kind of mega-budget experiences that long sustained the games industry but increasingly have proven unsustainable in an industry that is collapsing under the weight of ballooning budgets and evaporating pospandemic-era growth.
The five-to-seven year development cycles and hundreds of millions of dollars involved in making games at this scale add even more financial risk for the companies releasing them, Wallace notes. What once might have seemed like a successful game launch is now complicated by ever-higher budgets that in turn demand ever-higher profits to justify their existence. In all this new calculus, those who make games have paid the steepest price: Over the last two years, one third of US game workers have been laid off, according to a report from the Game Developers Conference.
At the same time, the price of AAA games has also gone up. What was once a reliable $60 price tag has risen to $70, and in the case of GTA 6, the cost is $80.
With the higher cost of living prices that consumers are paying currently, the vast majority of players are spending less on games and playing fewer of them these days. Wallace is unsurprised that the games industry has pinned its hopes and dreams on a blockbuster like GTA 6. But the arrival of the blockbuster to end all blockbusters might be less of a coronation and more of a funeral for the kinds of games that have long kept the industry afloat but are now in danger of sinking it.
“The games industry is facing a reckoning,” Wallace says. “At least in the current state [of the industry], there are just going to be fewer and fewer of these AAA games and franchises that really have a chance, in part because of price point and accessibility and audience interest.”
And while the GTAs of the world might become less common, Wallace is heartened to see midbudget and even microbudget independent games finding an audience.
“The industry is just going to take a different form,” she says.
