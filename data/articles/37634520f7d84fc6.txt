TITLE: Booking's parent and Agoda must face €965m Dutch consumer claim
OUTLET: PPC Land

The District Court of Amsterdam ruled on October 28, 2026 that it has jurisdiction to hear a Dutch collective action against Booking Holdings Inc. and Agoda Company Pte. Ltd., dismissing challenges from the Delaware-registered parent of the Booking group and its Singapore-based sister platform. The case, brought by Stichting Consumenten Competition Claims on behalf of people living in the Netherlands, accuses the group of using parity clauses that kept hotel prices high and of deploying misleading interface designs on Booking.com and Agoda.com. The interlocutory judgment, published on October 30 under ECLI:NL:RBAMS:2026:10574, settles only where the case will be heard. Whether any of the accusations are true remains open.
In Short
A Dutch court has decided that Booking's American parent company and Agoda, a Singapore travel site owned by the same group, must defend a consumer class action in Amsterdam instead of having it thrown out for being filed in the wrong country. The case says Booking's pricing rules for hotels pushed up room prices and that both sites used design tricks to mislead people, and it asks for €869 million and €96 million in damages for those two complaints. Nothing has been decided about whether the accusations hold, but the ruling treats a website in Dutch, priced in euros and offering a Dutch bank payment method as aimed at the Netherlands, so its design can be judged there wherever the designers sit.
What the court decided
Judge R.A. Dudok van Heel, sitting alone in case number C/13/781273 / HA ZA 26-1, delivered the judgment earlier than scheduled. The published summary describes the case as a collective damages action under the WAMCA, the Dutch law on mass damages claims brought by representative organisations. Two separate applications were before the court. Agoda asked for a declaration that the Dutch courts lacked jurisdiction over every claim brought against it. Booking Holdings Inc. made a narrower request, contesting jurisdiction only over the consumer law claims.
Both failed. "For the reasons set out below, the Court holds that it has jurisdiction," the judgment states at the outset, and it ends with a single line: "The interlocutory applications are dismissed." Agoda and Booking Holdings Inc. were each ordered to pay the foundation's costs of €1,495, payable within fourteen days of written demand. The main proceedings were listed on the procedural roll of November 25, 2026, when the parties are to make written submissions on how the case continues.
The court opened its assessment with an observation that frames everything that follows. Many international collective actions in the Netherlands, it noted, involve conduct carried out largely abroad. "The present case is different. It is closely connected with the Netherlands and is, to that extent, atypical as regards the jurisdictional applications," according to the judgment. The foundation is Dutch; the hotels signed their parity obligations with Booking.com B.V., an Amsterdam company; the booking websites are in Dutch; and the alleged loss is that of Dutch residents.
The quotations in this article come from an unofficial, non-certified English translation of the Dutch judgment. The binding text is the Dutch original published on Rechtspraak.nl.
Thirteen defendants, two kinds of claim
The foundation sued thirteen companies. Three are Dutch: Booking.com Holding B.V., Booking.com B.V., which operates the Booking.com platform, and Booking.com International B.V. The fourth is Booking Holdings Inc., the ultimate holding company of the group, registered in Delaware. Eight more are local support companies in Germany, Belgium, Austria, France, Italy, Portugal, Spain and Switzerland, which the court calls the European Entities. The thirteenth is Agoda, which according to the judgment "offers a platform for Hotel OTA Services, primarily in the Asian market." Booking Holdings Inc. holds all the shares in Agoda, which makes it a sister company of Booking.com.
The claims fall into two groups. The competition law claims target all thirteen defendants and are brought for a class the foundation calls Consumers I: everyone who lived in the Netherlands at any point since January 1, 2013 and booked a hotel room in Europe online at least once, whether through Booking.com, through another online travel agency or directly on a hotel's own website. The breadth follows from the foundation's argument that the parity obligations stopped hotels from offering lower prices through other channels and so caused consumers to pay higher prices, whichever channel they booked through.
The consumer law claims target four defendants: Booking.com Holding B.V., Booking.com B.V., Booking Holdings Inc. and Agoda. They concern what the foundation describes as dark patterns, which the judgment summarises as "patterns and representations implemented on the Booking.com and Agoda.com platforms to mislead consumers." This class, Consumers II, covers Dutch residents who booked a hotel room online at least once on a Booking group website since January 1, 2013. The foundation originally brought these claims against the European Entities too, then withdrew them while the jurisdiction dispute was under way.

The funder is not named.
Behind the foundation stands the Consumentenbond, the Dutch consumer association. According to the Consumentenbond, the foundation started the action against Booking on June 26, 2025 with its support, and more than 270,000 people have signed up. The association describes the practices at issue as fake discounts, incomplete prices and invented scarcity, and says participation is on a no cure, no pay basis with a fee capped at 25% of the result. When the claim was launched, Booking.com rejected the allegations and said it was assessing the claim, according to Dutch news agency ANP.
The parity clause record
Most of the factual background in the judgment concerns parity clauses, the contract terms that stop hotels from offering better deals elsewhere. According to the court, Booking.com included a wide parity clause in its hotel agreements until July 1, 2015. Under it, "hotels were not permitted to offer lower prices or better terms anywhere, whether on other platforms or through their own sales channels, than those offered on the Booking.com platform."
After competition authorities in France, Italy and Sweden opened investigations and accepted commitments, Booking.com switched on July 1, 2015 to a narrow parity clause, under which "hotels were not permitted to offer their rooms on more favourable terms through their own channels." The judgment records that this narrow clause had been removed from Booking.com's general terms and conditions "by 2 December 2024 at the latest." Booking was designated a gatekeeper under the Digital Markets Act on May 13, 2024, as PPC Land reported in its coverage of the Etraveli ruling; the judgment does not connect the removal to that designation. The foundation's case also covers what it calls implicit parity obligations, and its demand for documents asks how Booking.com's ranking algorithm handles "the weighting of external prices."
Germany went further. The Bundeskartellamt found on December 22, 2015 that the narrow clause infringed EU and German competition law, and the Bundesgerichtshof upheld that decision on May 18, 2021. A group of German hotels has also sued Booking.com and its German subsidiary in Amsterdam. In that separate case, still pending, the same court referred questions to the Court of Justice of the European Union on February 22, 2023, received answers on September 19, 2024 in Case C-264/23, and gave a further interlocutory judgment on March 4, 2026. In Berlin, a regional court ruled in December 2025 that 1,099 hotels can claim damages from Booking.com over best price clauses; that ruling was declaratory and awarded no sums.
The scale of the platform explains why these clauses matter. By the European Commission's estimate, Booking held 60-70% of the hotel online travel agency market in the European Economic Area in 2022, a figure that resurfaced when the General Court upheld the prohibition of Booking's Etraveli takeover on September 9, 2026.
One more fact in the judgment links this case to advertising. Since at least 2009, Booking.com has run a "Preferred Partner Program" and a "Preferred Partner Program Plus." According to the court, "To participate, hotels must satisfy certain conditions and pay a higher fee (commission). Participation affects the ranking of participating hotels in search results." On October 21, 2026, a week before this judgment, the European Commission opened Digital Services Act proceedings against Booking.com, examining among other things whether listings boosted in search results in exchange for higher commission are clearly labelled as ads.
How the court reached jurisdiction
According to the judgment, the court's route to jurisdiction ran through seven steps, each tied to a specific legal provision.
- Dutch defendants. Booking.com Holding B.V., Booking.com B.V. and Booking.com International B.V. are domiciled in the Netherlands, so Article 2 of the Dutch Code of Civil Procedure (Rv) gives the Dutch courts jurisdiction over them.
- Uncontested competition claims. Booking Holdings Inc. and the European Entities did not contest jurisdiction over the competition law claims. The court took jurisdiction under Article 26 of the Brussels I Recast Regulation for all of them except Booking.com (Schweiz) AG, which falls under Article 24 of the Lugano Convention.
- The rulebook for non-EU defendants. For a defendant in the United States and one in Singapore, jurisdiction is governed by Articles 1 to 14 Rv. Because the Dutch legislature drew on Brussels I Recast when writing those rules, they "must, in principle, be interpreted by reference to the CJEU's case law on Brussels I Recast and its predecessors."
- Competition claims against Agoda. The court relied on Article 7(1) Rv, which extends jurisdiction over an "anchor defendant" to co-defendants when the claims are closely connected. It did not need to rule on the foundation's alternative ground, Article 6(e) Rv.
- Consumer claims against Agoda. The court took jurisdiction under Article 6(e) Rv, which covers tort claims where the harmful event occurred or may occur in the Netherlands.
- Consumer claims against Booking Holdings Inc. The same provision applied, on the basis of the group liability alleged under Article 6:166 of the Dutch Civil Code (BW).
- Territorial jurisdiction. Article 99 Rv gives the Amsterdam court territorial jurisdiction over the Dutch defendants, and Article 107 Rv extends it to the rest.
Agoda and the parity clauses
Agoda's main argument on the competition claims was that it had nothing to do with Booking.com's contracts. It argued that EU case law leaves no room for what the judgment calls "horizontal liability": holding one subsidiary liable, through a shared parent, for the conduct of its sister company. Agoda described this as an impermissible "two-stage mechanism."
