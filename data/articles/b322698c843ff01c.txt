TITLE: Apple lets iOS 27 apps tell websites not to sell or share user data
OUTLET: PPC Land

Apple's WebKit team on November 18, 2026 documented a programming interface that lets apps which display web pages send the Global Privacy Control signal, a browser-level request asking websites not to sell or share a person's data or use it for cross-context targeted advertising. The interface, available in iOS, iPadOS, visionOS and macOS 27, is off unless an app turns it on, and once it is on it covers every frame and every request a page makes.
In Short
Apple has given app developers a way to make the web pages inside their apps send the Global Privacy Control signal, a request not to sell or share personal data that is defined in a draft web standard. It matters because California and other US states treat that signal as a legal opt-out, so any app that switches it on changes what advertisers and ad tech firms may do with data from those page views. Nothing changes by default: each app must add its own setting, and only navigations where the app enables the signal carry it.
What Apple published
The post, titled "Global Privacy Control (GPC) WebKit API" and signed by John Wilander, is short. "As of iOS, iPadOS, visionOS, and macOS 27, there is now a WebKit API to enable Global Privacy Control (GPC), an emerging web standard," it begins. It is addressed to developers rather than to people who browse: "As a developer using WebKit APIs like WKWebView, or WebPage for SwiftUI, you can add your own user setting to control it."
That distinction is the heart of the announcement. WKWebView is the class iPhone, iPad, Mac and Vision Pro apps use to show web content inside their own interface, and WebPage is its newer Swift counterpart for SwiftUI. The post gives apps built on either a documented switch for Global Privacy Control, in two forms.
For WKWebView, there is a new property on WKWebpagePreferences called globalPrivacyControlEnabled. According to the post, "Setting it to YES on the preferences object handed back from the navigation-policy delegate opts that navigation into GPC. This applies to the whole document tree produced by the navigation." The next sentence sets out how far that reaches: "Once enabled, both navigator.globalPrivacyControl and the Sec-GPC: 1 request header are active for the main frame, its subframes, and their subresources."
For SwiftUI, the same capability sits on a different type. "The Swift WebPage API exposes the same capability through a WebPage.NavigationPreferences property, isGlobalPrivacyControlEnabled," according to the post.
Two things stand out in the framing. The post quotes two outside documents rather than describing the policy goal in Apple's own words: the W3C specification "as of November 10, 2026," and the California Attorney General's page on the California Consumer Privacy Act as of the same date. And it says nothing about Safari, the browser Apple ships on the same devices.
The documentation entries
Apple's developer documentation describes both properties in identical terms. Each is "Whether the Global Privacy Control (GPC) signal is enabled for the navigation." For WKWebpagePreferences, the documentation says "The default value is NO." For WebPage.NavigationPreferences, it says "The default value of this property is false." Both pages repeat the scope in the post: when enabled, the JavaScript property and the request header "are active for the main frame, its subframes, and their subresources."
Both entries list the same five platforms, each from version 27.0: iOS, iPadOS, Mac Catalyst, macOS and visionOS. Mac Catalyst, the route by which iPad apps run on the Mac, appears in the documentation but not in the post. tvOS and watchOS appear in neither.
The new properties sit in the same lists as existing per-navigation settings such as isLockdownModeEnabled, preferredHTTPSNavigationPolicy and allowsContentJavaScript. Apple has, in other words, filed the privacy signal next to Lockdown Mode, among the preferences an app can hand back as a page loads.
How the switch works, according to the documentation
The post includes code samples in Swift for WKWebView, Swift for WebPage and Objective-C. Read together with the two documentation pages, they describe the following sequence.
- The app keeps its own user setting. The post refers to it in the samples as userPrefersGlobalPrivacyControl. The documentation describes no system-wide toggle that apps inherit; the post says developers "can add your own user setting to control it."
- A navigation begins. WebKit calls the app's navigation-policy delegate. For WKWebView, that is the webView(_:decidePolicyFor:preferences:) method, which receives a WKNavigationAction and a WKWebpagePreferences object. For WebPage, it is decidePolicy(for:preferences:), which receives a WebPage.NavigationAction and an inout WebPage.NavigationPreferences value.
- The app sets the property. In the WKWebView sample, the line is preferences.globalPrivacyControlEnabled = userPrefersGlobalPrivacyControl. In the WebPage sample, it is preferences.isGlobalPrivacyControlEnabled = userPrefersGlobalPrivacyControl.
- The app allows the navigation. The Swift WKWebView sample returns (.allow, preferences), the WebPage sample returns .allow, and the Objective-C sample calls decisionHandler(WKNavigationActionPolicyAllow, preferences).
- WebKit applies the signal to the whole page. For that navigation, the main frame, every subframe and every subresource carry the Sec-GPC: 1 header, and scripts reading navigator.globalPrivacyControl see it enabled.
- Without step 3, nothing is sent. The default is NO in Objective-C terms and false in Swift.
Because the value is attached to each navigation, an app could in principle enable the signal for some pages and not others. The post does not discuss that case.
What the signal itself says
GPC is defined by a W3C document. The Working Draft dated 24 September 2026 was published by the W3C Privacy Working Group on the Recommendation track. Its editors are Sebastian Zimmeck of Wesleyan University, Peter Snyder of Brave Software, Justin Brookman of Consumer Reports and Aram Zucker-Scharff of The Washington Post. Robin Berjon, Ashkan Soltani and David Harbage of DuckDuckGo are listed as former editors. The draft carries the usual caveat: "Publication as a Working Draft does not imply endorsement by W3C and its Members."
Its abstract is precise about the purpose: "This document defines a signal, transmitted over HTTP and through the DOM, that conveys a person's request to websites and services to not sell or share their personal information with third parties." The introduction explains why a single opt-out signal was thought necessary. "Requiring that people manually express their rights for each and every site they visit is, however, impractical, and an imposition of 'privacy labor' on people," it states.
The sentence Apple chose to quote comes from the same introduction: "Global Privacy Control is designed to allow users to express a preference to opt out of sharing and cross-context targeted advertising." The draft continues directly with the limits. "GPC is not designed to exercise deletion rights, for example. GPC is also not designed to address data collection or ad targeting within the same context." A publisher showing ads to a reader on its own site based on that reader's activity on the same site is, in the draft's words, outside what the signal is meant to stop.
The mechanics are strict. "A user agent MUST generate a Sec-GPC header field with a field-value that is exactly the numeric character '1'" when the preference is on, and must not send the header at all when it is off. A server that receives any other value "MUST ignore it." HTTP intermediaries "MUST NOT remove a Sec-GPC header set to '1'." The same value is exposed to scripts as navigator.globalPrivacyControl, in both page and worker contexts. The draft's own example of a script check is drily blunt; the code comment reads "// wonderful, we can sell this person's data!" for the case where the property is false.
There is also a rule on timing that maps closely onto Apple's design. "The preference MUST be cached on each top-level navigation," the draft states, and "if the preference changes during or after a top-level navigation, it will not be reflected until the next navigation." Apple's interface is set in the navigation-policy delegate, which runs as each navigation begins.
The draft acknowledges that it relies on concepts developed for Do Not Track, the earlier header that websites were never obliged to obey. The difference sits in the last sentence of the abstract: the standard "is intended to work with existing and upcoming legal frameworks that render such requests enforceable."
The California connection
That is why the post quotes California. The Attorney General's CCPA page, last updated on August 28, 2026, lists the rights the law "secures" for California consumers. Among them: "The right to opt-out of the sale or sharing of their personal information including via the GPC."
The page's frequently asked questions spell out the mechanism in more detail. Under the right to opt out of sale or sharing, it says: "You may request that businesses stop selling or sharing your personal information ('opt-out'), including via a user-enabled global privacy control. Businesses cannot sell or share your personal information after they receive your opt-out request unless you later authorize them to do so again."
The words "user-enabled" fit Apple's design. The post tells developers to add their own user setting, and both properties default to off. The W3C draft describes the wider legal pattern: "Many US states say that a user agent may not send a universal opt-out signal by 'default,' though at least one state has said that selecting a privacy focused user agent is a sufficient indicator of user intent." It also records that "At least four states have specifically identified GPC as a valid means to exercise legal opt-out rights."
For advertisers, the definition of sharing is the operative part. According to the Attorney General's page, "sharing refers specifically to sharing for cross-context behavioral advertising, which is the targeting of advertising to a consumer based on the consumer's personal information obtained from the consumer's online activity across numerous websites." The page adds that "Businesses must wait at least 12 months before asking you to opt back in to the sale or sharing of your personal information."
The California Privacy Rights Act, approved by voters as Proposition 24 in November 2020, amended the CCPA, with new protections starting on January 1, 2023. The Attorney General's office notes that it "typically refers to the law as 'CCPA' or 'CCPA, as amended.'"
A further California deadline sits 44 days after Apple's post, by PPC Land's count. California's amendments include AB 566, under which browsers may not be developed or maintained without opt-out preference signal functionality that is easy for a reasonable person to locate and configure, from January 1, 2027. Apple's post does not mention that law, and the documents do not say whether an app's embedded web view counts as a browser for its purposes.
Safari and the apps
Apple's own browser is moving on a parallel track, according to reports from the week before the post. 9to5Mac reported on November 11, 2026 that the iOS 27.3 beta adds a Safari setting named Use Global Privacy Control, under Settings, Apps, Safari, Advanced. iThinkDifferent reported the same day that the setting is off by default and that iOS 27.3 is expected in December. 9to5Mac reported that Apple's support document for the setting had not yet been published. WebKit's public bug tracker shows a report titled "Add off-by-default support for Global Privacy Control" filed on May 1, 2026.
