TITLE: Daily Iron Cuts Child Anemia, Cochrane Review of 114 Studies Finds
OUTLET: Global Agriculture

21 November 2026, Ithaca, US: Giving infants, children and adolescents a daily iron supplement probably reduces iron deficiency anemia, according to an updated Cochrane systematic review led by researchers at Cornell University, though the authors stress that the quality of the evidence is weaker than the headline finding suggests and that the results do not mean every child needs extra iron.
The review, published on November 17 in the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, pooled 114 studies involving 50,325 children and adolescents aged from birth to 19 years. Iron deficiency anemia is a condition in which the body lacks enough iron to make hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen, leading to fatigue, weakness and, in young children, possible effects on development.
A systematic review gathers and assesses all eligible studies on a question rather than running a new trial. In this case the authors searched medical databases and trial registries up to January 2025, so the newest studies included date from before that point. Most of the research came from Asia (67 studies), and 50 studies focused on children under five.
The largest group of studies, 89 in total, compared daily oral iron given as tablets, capsules or syrup with no treatment or a placebo. Across that evidence, the reviewers concluded that iron alone probably reduces the risk of iron deficiency anemia. Eleven studies compared iron plus folic acid against no treatment or placebo, and the authors judged that combination also probably reduces iron deficiency anemia, although that conclusion rests on a single study for the outcome. A further 38 studies, in two comparison groups of 25 and 13, tested iron given alongside other vitamins and minerals, which the authors said may also reduce the condition.
No effect on growth or common illness
The review found that iron alone made no difference to children’s height or weight compared with peers of the same age and sex. It also found no effect on cough, fever or respiratory illness, a point of interest because concerns have been raised in the past about whether extra iron could worsen some infections in children. Iron combined with folic acid made little or no difference to diarrhoea or respiratory illness.
Side effects were generally mild. Darker stools were more common among children taking iron. Iron alone may make little or no difference to vomiting or diarrhoea, while its effect on constipation was very uncertain. Iron given with other vitamins and minerals probably increased vomiting.
Senior author Saurabh Mehta of Cornell’s Joan Klein Jacobs Center for Precision Nutrition and Health cautioned that the certainty of evidence for several outcomes, including anemia and iron deficiency themselves, was low or very low. Under Cochrane’s grading system, low or very low certainty means further research could substantially change the estimated effects. First author Kripa Rajagopalan said daily iron supplements “do what they are meant to do,” but the authors added that the findings should not be read as a recommendation for universal supplementation.
The review was funded by the Cornell center and the World Health Organization, and two of the authors are or were WHO staff. Many of the underlying studies were supported by non-profit organizations, with UNICEF backing 50 of them. In their conflict-of-interest statement, Mehta disclosed grants for trials of iron-biofortified crops and a former equity interest in a start-up that was dissolved in May 2025. The authors called for more studies on safety in higher-income countries, where fewer trials have been carried out.
A food systems problem as much as a medical one
Anemia affects an estimated 874 million children and women worldwide, according to figures cited by Cochrane, and around 40% of children under five were affected in 2019. About half of all anemia cases are attributed to iron deficiency, which is closely tied to diets low in iron-rich or iron-fortified foods, particularly in communities that rely heavily on staple cereals.
That link is why public health agencies pursue several routes at once. Alongside supplements, governments and research groups use mandatory fortification of flour and rice, and plant breeders have developed biofortified crops, such as high-iron beans and pearl millet, that carry more iron in the edible grain. Supplementation programmes reach children directly, while fortification and biofortification change the iron content of the foods households already buy and grow.
