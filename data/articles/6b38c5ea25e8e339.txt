TITLE: Amazon bans ads whose audio starts or stops abruptly, on every ad product
OUTLET: PPC Land

Audio in Amazon ads must not start or stop abruptly, according to an Amazon Ads Support Centre page updated on November 24, 2026. The line sits in a short list of sound rules on a page titled "Ad specifications overview", which states that it "covers the requirements for ad specifications across all ad products."
The wording is seven words long: "Audio must not start or stop abruptly." It is the last of five bullet points under the heading "Audio quality requirements", next to a requirement for a mute button whenever audio is enabled and a rule that ad audio "Can't be louder than surrounding content." A companion line in the video section says "Video content can't abruptly end or truncate."
Amazon has said something similar before, but more softly and to fewer advertisers. Its undated audio ad specifications page advises audio advertisers to avoid abrupt or startling music cuts at the beginning or end of a spot. The overview page states it as a requirement, extends it from music to audio in general, and places it on a page that applies to every ad product Amazon sells. The page is dated three days before Black Friday, and it does not say when the line first appeared.
In Short
Amazon's ad specifications page, updated on November 24, 2026, says audio in ads "must not start or stop abruptly", one of five sound rules that also require a mute button and bar audio louder than the surrounding content. Because the page covers all Amazon ad products, the rules reach brands and agencies running audio ads on Amazon Music and Alexa devices as well as video ads on streaming TV and the open web. Earlier Amazon guidance only advised audio advertisers to avoid abrupt music cuts, while this page states a requirement, though it does not define "abrupt", set a loudness figure or say when the line was added.
How the page sets out its requirements
The page sits under Policies and guidelines in the Support Centre. Its subtitle describes its purpose: "Learn about Amazon ad specification requirements to build compliant ads across video, audio, and display formats." A highlighted note sends readers elsewhere for detailed figures: "Please reference our supply specific guide for additional information about specific image sizing, aspect ratios, and bit rate requirements for each supply type."
According to Amazon, the requirements fall into six blocks, in this order.
- General quality and performance requirements. Eight lines covering production value, broken ads, load times, interference with surrounding content and language. Ads "Must be high production value, with no grammatical errors or typos" and "Must not impact surrounding content performance."
- Accessibility requirements. Three lines: text "must be legible and high contrast", "Video captions must not overlap user interface elements", and interactive elements "must be clearly identifiable and distinguishable from inactive elements."
- Video ad quality requirements. Five lines on aspect ratios, bit rates, animation length, endings and silent video.
- Audio quality requirements. Five lines on clarity, harsh sounds, loudness, the mute button and abrupt starts or stops.
- Image-sizing requirements. Two lines: images "Must fill the entire designated space" and "Meet all layout and safe zone requirements for the respective placement."
- QR codes. Four lines on restrictions, destinations, Amazon's own generator tool and data tracking.
That is 27 requirements in all, by PPC Land's count. None carries a date, a market or a named ad product.
The five sound rules
The audio block reads, in full:
- "Audio must be clear and easy to understand"
- "No high-pitched, disruptive, or shocking sounds"
- "Can't be louder than surrounding content"
- "A mute button is required when audio is enabled"
- "Audio must not start or stop abruptly"
Each line describes how sound behaves for the person hearing it rather than how a file is encoded. There is no sample rate, no file format and no loudness target in decibels. The page's pointer to a supply-specific guide mentions image sizing, aspect ratios and bit rates, not sound levels.
The abrupt-start rule targets a familiar problem. An ad whose audio begins at full level the instant it loads, or ends mid-word when the slot closes, jolts the listener in a way a faded or properly padded spot does not. The page does not describe the problem in those terms. It states only the outcome: no abrupt start, no abrupt stop.
Which ads the rules reach
The page does not limit the audio block to any ad product, and its scope sentence covers "all ad products." On that reading, the audio rules apply wherever an Amazon ad carries sound.
That is a wide footprint. Amazon's audio ad specifications page lists delivery on the ad-supported tier of Amazon Music and through Amazon Publisher Services, on desktop, mobile, tablet, connected TV and smart speakers, including Echo, third-party Alexa-enabled devices and Fire TV. Video with sound runs on Amazon's own Streaming TV supply and across the open internet. Amazon's demand-side platform has added audio supply through partners, connecting Pandora and SoundCloud inventory from SiriusXM Media in September 2025 and joining Triton's programmatic audio marketplace, which spans more than 80 countries, on July 15, 2026. On June 11, 2026, audio became a supported inventory type in Amazon DSP's campaign management APIs, with podcast inventory still excluded at the time.
The same platform has since changed its name. On September 29, 2026, Amazon put a four-step DVA+ campaign builder into US closed beta, a product that buys display, video and audio from one objective and one budget. Rules written for "all ad products" apply to audio bought that way as well.
The page does not separate formats with screens from those without. A mute button "is required when audio is enabled", yet an audio spot on a speaker without a display has no screen on which to show one. Whether the mute requirement applies to screenless placements, or is meant for display and video units that carry sound, the page does not say.
Video specifications on the same page
The video block carries the page's only numerical specifications. It pairs the sound rules with an endings rule of its own and a disclaimer requirement for silent video. In the page's terms, CTV refers to connected TV, STV to Streaming TV and OLV to online video outside television screens.
The 16:9 requirement for CTV and Streaming TV reflects the shape of a television screen. For OLV, the page also allows 9:16, the vertical format of phone video. The bit-rate floor of 4 Mbps applies to both, with 8 Mbps recommended.
Read together, the video and audio blocks set the same expectation at both ends of an ad. Sound must not cut in or out abruptly, and the picture must not end before the message does.
What earlier Amazon guidance said
Amazon has published audio guidance in several places, none of it dated. Comparing those pages with the November 24 overview shows how the wording has hardened, though not when.
Amazon's audio ad specifications page, which covers standard and interactive audio ads, advises advertisers to avoid abrupt or startling music cuts at the beginning or end of a spot. It also asks for voiceovers that are easy to hear with even pacing and appropriate loudness, for disclaimers read at roughly the same volume and pace as the rest of the ad, and for no shrill or jarring sounds, giving air horns as its example. Its file specifications call for spots of 10 to 30 seconds, files of up to 3MB in WAV, MP3 or OGG format, at least 192 kbps, with RMS loudness normalised to -14 dBFS and peaks to -0.2 dBFS. Audio ads meant for Twitch must run 12 to 15 or 27 to 30 seconds to fit its ad slots, and interactive audio ads are limited to the US and UK.
Amazon's Sponsored Ads policies set rules for Sponsored Brands video, which autoplays on mute and plays sound only when a shopper clicks. Those policies prohibit distracting, shrill, unexpected or jarring sounds, naming sudden changes in volume and unusually high-pitched voices, and poor audio such as static or crackling. They also bar videos that end by cutting off a voiceover, speech or text in mid-sentence, and accept silent videos as long as it is clear to the shopper that there is no audio.
Amazon's creative acceptance policy, an older undated document, requires all audio to be started by the customer with a click, with a mute button, and requires videos longer than 15 seconds to be started by the customer with play, pause and stop controls. Its section on Amazon DSP repeats that audio must be initiated by the customer.
Three differences stand out. The abrupt-audio wording moved from "avoid" to "must not." Its subject widened from music cuts to audio of any kind. And its reach widened from audio ads to every ad product. One element of the older text is absent: the overview page requires a mute button but does not repeat that audio must be started by the customer. It does not say autoplay audio is now allowed, either.
Loudness rules without a number
"Can't be louder than surrounding content" is a relative test. It compares an ad with the programme, song or page around it rather than with a fixed level, which is how regulators frame the same question. In the United States, the CALM Act has long required television commercials to match the volume of the programmes they accompany. California extended that principle to streaming when Governor Gavin Newsom signed SB 576 on October 6, 2025, barring video streaming services serving California consumers from playing ads louder than the content they accompany, from July 1, 2026.
Industry standards bodies put figures on it. The IAB Tech Lab released CTV ad format guidelines for public comment on December 11, 2025, with a loudness target of -24 LKFS in the US under ATSC A/85 and -23 LUFS in Europe under EBU R128. Amazon's overview page cites none of these measures and names no law. The audio-file figure Amazon publishes elsewhere, -14 dBFS RMS, sits on its audio ad specifications page and uses a different measurement.
The rest of the page
Most of the page restates general creative hygiene, but a few lines carry more weight than the rest.
The language rule is the only line on the page that names a consequence. Ads "must be in a language understandable to the customer viewing the ad", and the page adds: "It's the advertiser's responsibility to ensure the ad's creative, settings, and placement match the language of the ad to the audience, landing page, and locale. Amazon will remove ads that don't adhere to this." No other requirement, the audio rules included, says what happens when an ad falls short.
The QR code block sets a data rule. "QR codes must not track any customer data such as identity, device identifier, or geographical data." Codes must lead "to a website or mobile app/app store compatible with a smartphone", and "On-Package ads and Lockers require creation using Amazon's QR code generator tool." The page adds that "Use of QR codes may be restricted on some supply or placements", without listing them.
The general block also says "Interactive elements must not cover or obstruct elements such as text, logos, disclaimers, or product information and they can't obstruct product shots", and that ads "Must meet specific load time and text-to-image ratio requirements." The load times and ratios themselves are not on the page.
