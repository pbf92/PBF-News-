TITLE: Instagram chief denies microphone spying amid targeted ad concerns
OUTLET: Indian Television Dot Com

MUMBAI: Think your phone has been eavesdropping on your conversations? Instagram says it has a much better way of getting inside your head. The platform’s chief, Adam Mosseri, has denied that Instagram listens to users through their phone microphones or cameras to serve targeted advertisements, arguing that a combination of user behaviour, social connections and online tracking can explain why certain ads appear to know exactly what people have been discussing.
In a video statement on Tuesday, Mosseri sought to address a long-running suspicion surrounding social media platforms: that they secretly listen to everyday conversations and use the information to make advertisements more relevant. The theory often resurfaces when a product discussed with friends suddenly appears in an Instagram feed, sometimes despite the user never having searched for it.
Mosseri offered three explanations for the seemingly uncanny timing. The first, he suggested, may have less to do with technology and more to do with what users have already seen.
According to him, people may have encountered an advertisement several times while scrolling through Instagram without consciously registering it. The product or brand could nevertheless have stayed somewhere in their minds, making them more likely to bring it up in a later conversation.
“You might have actually seen that ad in Instagram a couple of times before, and so it was actually in the back of your mind, which made you more likely to talk about it in the first place,” he said.
The second explanation is rooted in the way human attention works. Once people discuss a particular product, they are more likely to notice it when it appears again. An advertisement that would otherwise have passed unnoticed can suddenly stand out, creating the impression that Instagram has picked up on the conversation.
“After you talk about something, after it’s top of mind, you’re more likely to notice it when you see it in the real world or on Instagram,” Mosseri explained.
The third reason, however, brings the discussion back to the extensive information social media platforms collect about their users and the people around them.
Mosseri said Instagram does not rely only on an individual’s activity when deciding which advertisements to display. It also considers information about their friends and people with similar interests and behaviour. This means a user may never have searched for a product, visited its website or interacted with an advertisement, but someone in their social circle might have.
That connection can help the platform identify products and services that could be relevant to the user, even without any direct search or conversation-related information.
There is a substantial amount of information available to Meta without its having to switch on a phone’s microphone. Instagram’s own data policy describes how the platform collects information about users’ connections, the accounts they interact with, the content they view, how frequently they use the service and how much time they spend on it.
The company’s advertising ecosystem also draws on information from businesses, websites, applications and other partners. This can include websites visited, purchases made, advertisements viewed, devices used and interactions with services outside Meta’s platforms.
The information helps Instagram build a picture of users’ interests and activities, which can then be used to personalise advertisements. Meta also combines information across devices, allowing activity on one device to influence the advertisements displayed on another.
One of the technologies supporting this system is the Meta Pixel, a tracking tool that businesses can install on their websites. It records actions taken by visitors, such as viewing a product, adding an item to a shopping basket or completing a purchase.
Advertisers can use this information to create Custom Audiences and reach people who have previously visited their websites or interacted with their applications, subject to Meta’s advertising rules and privacy controls.
The company also offers Lookalike Audiences, which help advertisers identify potential customers who share characteristics with an existing customer group. In effect, a person does not necessarily have to show direct interest in a product for an advertisement to reach them. Their online behaviour, shopping patterns or similarities with other customers may be enough to make them part of a target audience.
Put together, these tools offer advertisers several routes to reach potential customers without relying on microphone recordings.
Mosseri was categorical in rejecting the suggestion that Instagram uses audio or video recordings for this purpose.
“But no, we did not listen to you via your microphone or your camera or anything, any other recording device. I want to be very, very clear about that,” he said.
The explanation also highlights the commercial importance of Meta’s advertising machinery. For the company, understanding what people watch, buy, search for and engage with is central to its business model.
Meta reported advertising revenue of $196.17 billion in 2025, accounting for almost all of its total revenue of $200.97 billion. Its Family of Apps, which includes Facebook, Instagram, Messenger and WhatsApp, averaged 3.58 billion daily active people in December 2025.
Advertising impressions across the Family of Apps increased by 12 per cent during 2025, while the average price per advertisement rose by 9 per cent.
The figures underline the scale of the audience and advertising market that Meta serves. The more accurately platforms can match advertisements with users, the more valuable their inventory can become to businesses looking to reach specific audiences.
For advertisers, this means the targeting process can go well beyond someone explicitly searching for a product. Browsing activity, previous purchases, interactions with content and connections with other users can all contribute to the picture.
For users, however, the same system can feel considerably more intrusive, particularly when an advertisement appears moments after a private conversation.
There is also a psychological explanation for why these experiences can feel more mysterious than they are. The phenomenon is commonly known as the frequency illusion, or the Baader-Meinhof phenomenon.
It describes the tendency to notice something more frequently after it has caught one’s attention. A person who has just discussed a particular car, for instance, may suddenly begin noticing that model everywhere, even though its actual presence on the road has not changed.
Selective attention plays a role here. Once something becomes relevant to a person, the brain is more likely to pick it out from the surrounding information. The result can be a heightened sense that the product has suddenly appeared everywhere.
Another related concept is the mere-exposure effect. This psychological phenomenon suggests that repeated exposure to something can increase familiarity and, in certain circumstances, make people more inclined to like it.
A 2017 meta-analysis published in the journal Psychological Bulletin examined 268 exposure-response curves drawn from 81 research papers. It found that repeated exposure generally increased familiarity and positive evaluations of visual stimuli. However, the relationship followed an inverted-U pattern, meaning that the positive effect could weaken or reverse when exposure became excessive.
When these psychological effects meet sophisticated advertising algorithms, the result can be an advertisement that feels almost impossibly well timed. A product may have already appeared in a user’s feed, their friends may have shown interest in it, and their own attention may have become more sensitive to it after a conversation.
The combination can create the impression of being listened to, even when the explanation lies elsewhere.
The debate over Instagram’s microphone is part of a wider concern about how much technology can infer from people’s everyday activities. Digital services are increasingly designed to understand users’ preferences, remember interactions and anticipate what they might need next.
Apple, which has repeatedly positioned privacy as a central part of its business, is also developing features for its Apple Watch that can help users recall conversations. Its Audio Intelligence features include Live Rewind, which can display the last 15 seconds of a conversation as text, and Siri Recap, which can summarise conversations and help users remember important points.
The features are intended to help users catch information they may have missed and recall discussions without having to take notes. Apple presents them as part of a broader effort to make its devices more useful, with privacy safeguards built into Apple Intelligence.
Yet the contrast highlights an increasingly important question about the relationship between technology and personal information. The ability to understand someone’s interests, habits, relationships and preferences can be useful when a digital assistant is organising tasks or retrieving information. The same capability can feel uncomfortable when it is being used to decide which advertisement should appear next.
For Meta, the challenge is therefore not simply to convince users that their microphones are not being used to listen in. It is also to explain how the enormous amount of information collected across its platforms and advertising partners shapes what people see.
The phone may not need to hear what users are saying to make an educated guess about what they want. And, as Instagram’s explanation suggests, that may be precisely what makes the whole experience feel so uncanny.
