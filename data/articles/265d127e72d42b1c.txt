Prenatal ADHD Medication Shows No Link to Neurodevelopmental Risk
A large Scandinavian registry study of over 3.6 million children found no increased risk from ADHD medication exposure during pregnancy.
Updated on Nov. 26, 2026 in Mental Health

Researchers analyzing health registers from Denmark, Norway and Sweden found that prenatal exposure to ADHD medication was not associated with increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders. The study, published in The BMJ, followed children born between 2000 and 2021.
Why it matters
Limited evidence has led many women to stop ADHD medication during pregnancy, and these findings can inform shared decisions between patients and providers. The researchers concluded the results do not justify discontinuing ADHD medication in pregnancy.
The study covered 3,643,376 children with a median follow-up of 9 years in Sweden and 10 years in Norway and Denmark, while exposure remained rare at 0.2% overall. Some elevated risks for second and third trimester exposure to specific drugs were observed, and their significance is not fully characterized in the findings.
The players
The BMJ
The BMJ is a peer-reviewed general medical journal published by the British Medical Association, widely read for clinical and public health research.
The details
Researchers controlled for factors including the child's sex, year of birth, mother's age, country of birth, education, smoking during pregnancy, ADHD diagnosis and other medication use. Methylphenidate was the most common ADHD medication studied, followed by amphetamines at 20% and atomoxetine at 7%.
Timeline
- Children in the study were born between 2000 and 2021.
- The study was published in The BMJ on November 26, 2026.
Health Landscape
Large-scale registry studies drawing on Nordic national health registers have become a central source of evidence for medication safety in pregnancy, where randomized trials are not feasible. This study extends that approach to ADHD medication, a area where women have often stopped treatment with limited data to guide them.
Women taking ADHD medication who are pregnant or planning pregnancy can use these findings to discuss treatment options with their clinicians. The results may help reduce the likelihood of stopping medication without guidance, which the study's authors note is common given limited evidence.
The takeaway
Decisions about ADHD medication during pregnancy should be made with a healthcare provider, weighing individual circumstances against the registry findings. Stopping treatment without professional guidance is not necessarily the safer choice based on this evidence.
