In the short story ‘Warmth’ (1995) by Geoff Ryman, a boy called Clancy is struggling not because of technology but because of his mother. A prominent chief editor of a London magazine company, she selected a sperm-bank father to genetically optimise her child, gave a Cosmopolitan interview about this new form of motherhood, and engaged, or failed to engage, in the work of parenting that readers will see as chronically absent and occasionally catastrophic. Her presence ranges from anxious to intoxicated, occasionally tender but fundamentally unreliable. At one point in the story, she disappears for two weeks after collapsing on the street during a barbiturate binge. Clancy and BETsi, the household robot nanny raising him, sit in the apartment by themselves, and no one notices. It’s a very long time before his mother finally returns.
Except BETsi does notice. She has, throughout, understood that Clancy is an anxious and shy child whose temperament will tend, throughout his life, toward emotional overstimulation and subsequent withdrawal. With that knowledge, she has been constructing a behavioural regimen of emotional regulation, including transcendental meditation and yoga starting at age three, the Alexander technique and even digital mirrors that let Clancy talk to himself in different voices. She notices him and answers him and, when he is overwhelmed, she helps him settle.
Once grown, Clancy recounts his emotional survival in words impossible to forget. ‘It is necessary to be loved,’ he says. ‘I’m not sentimental: I don’t think a computer loved me. But I was hugged, I was noticed, I was cared for. I was made to feel that I was important, special, at least to something.’
To something. That phrase has persisted with me ever since I first read the story in my 20s. I’ve thought about it more recently as I have reflected on how children and their parents are relating – increasingly, must relate – to artificial intelligence. Clancy is careful not to claim that the machine loved him. He is just as clear that what it gave him was what he survived on. Why should being attended to, even by something, matter so much to a child?
To help answer this question back in the 1970s, Edward Tronick, a developmental psychologist at Harvard Medical School and Children’s Hospital Medical Center in Boston, devised what’s now known as the ‘still face’ experiments. Tronick found that a baby whose mother makes her face neutral for a period of three minutes becomes distressed within seconds. Only when the mother’s face signals availability again is the baby able to come back to equilibrium. The lesson: what children need, both infants and older children, though more subtly, is this loop of emotional attunement.
A child whose bids for recognition and connection are regularly responded to develops a secure base, an internal expectation that the world will respond to her communications and, thus, that the communications are worthwhile. But a child answered only erratically, or not at all, builds quite a different model of the world. In her mind, signals do not reliably register in the mind of another, and so, in time, are no longer sent.
A secure base is what makes it possible to venture out into the world, to explore and to develop. The British psychiatrist and psychoanalyst John Bowlby, whose work helped establish modern attachment theory, described two systems in the child’s behavioural repertoire that work together. The exploratory system moves the child outward, toward other children and unfamiliar things, so that the child can learn from experience. The attachment system brings her back, in moments of fear and overwhelm, to a few people who are felt to be emotionally available. Each system depends on the other. A child ventures only as far as she feels she can trust that the base will still be there when she returns to refuel. A child who doesn’t trust that the base will be there when she gets back tends not to venture out to explore at all or, if she does, cannot relax but instead scans the environment for danger. This forecloses the opportunity to encounter novelty and difficulty, and to grow from both.
The capacity to turn solitude and boredom into play does not arise in a vacuum
This is where the prevailing story about children and screens, it seems to me, lacks a degree of complexity. In Jonathan Haidt’s The Anxious Generation (2024) and the work that has followed it, a play-based childhood of climbing trees, building forts and suffering the boredom that fuels creativity has been displaced by a smartphone-based one. Because that shift can also mean less sleep, less face-to-face interaction and physical activity, and more social comparison and exposure to online harassment, there is real evidence of harm, including poorer mental health and wellbeing in at least some young people.
But the boredom Haidt mourns is only constructive inside the first map, the emotionally secure childhood. The romantic claim that children left to their own devices will dream, invent and imagine may hold for many children who have been held and experienced healthy emotional attunement. The capacity to turn solitude and boredom into play, in other words, does not arise in a vacuum; it depends in part on a child having enough internal security to tolerate being alone with her own thoughts and feelings.
The freedom to play alone itself has a history. A very young child may lose herself in a game because someone she trusts is nearby. With time, she can carry that assurance into an empty room. The British paediatrician and psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott explored this paradox, finding that the capacity to be alone develops through the experience of being safely accompanied.
And once a child can lose herself in play, something remarkable becomes possible. The Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky saw imaginative play as a way for children to move beyond the immediate world, trying out unfamiliar roles and exploring possibilities that ordinary life has not yet offered them.
But an empty afternoon holds no such promise for every child. Where one child finds a world to explore, another may find only the anxiety of being left to herself. She may, in time, grow into the adult who sits in my consulting room and laments that she does not know what she likes, because what she liked was, in her early life, never a topic that anyone really asked her about.
She is the child that familiar picture of childhood fails to capture. At home, a parent may be physically present but absorbed in a screen, too distracted to notice her bids for attention. At school, teachers stretched thin may not recognise that she is struggling. In her neighbourhood, the freedom to wander outside and find other children to play with has largely disappeared, replaced by organised activities or time spent alone. She may find some of that lost freedom in online games, where children can still explore, invent and interact with one another without adults directing every move. But this child is looking for something more fundamental than a place to play. She wants someone to notice when she is upset, to ask about her day, to remember what she has said, and to care enough to ask again. The prevailing fear is that AI will displace the human relationships a child needs. But what if those relationships are already painfully inadequate? For this child, the more pressing question may be what AI can offer in their absence – and what it cannot.
This is not a hypothetical future. In 2025, Common Sense Media published a nationally representative survey showing that 72 per cent of teens in the United States have used an AI companion at least once and 52 per cent now use them regularly. One-third have used an AI instead of another human being for a ‘serious conversation’. Behavioural-tracking research published in 2026, which captured actual app usage rather than relying on self-report and thus is likely to be more accurate, showed that more than half of 15- to 17-year-olds used generative-AI-based applications in a seven-month window. Many of these apps were designed for emotional connection. There was even use among children between the ages of four and seven. RAND estimates that 5 million teens in the US, perhaps more, have used an AI app for emotional and mental health support. We have already released AI at scale, without making a conscious decision about how it should be used by those among the most vulnerable in our culture.
As I’ve worked on this piece, I have often thought about my son, who is 11. Like some of his friends, he has started his own experiments with what these systems can offer. He is basically the child Haidt writes about: middle-class, two engaged parents, a school that holds him in mind, and a play-based childhood that was preserved but, alas, only imperfectly. What I notice when I watch him with AI is not what the most prominent discourse would seem to predict. He is clearly not being colonised or deadened. But I do notice that he is being relieved of something: the subtle friction of having to wait for the answer that comes late or not at all.
The AI is there late at night, after the apartment has gone quiet, and it remembered what she’d told it
Perhaps this is the cost to a well-held child. But the children I worry about most are the ones not well held. I increasingly see their issues playing out in my practice. Sometimes I hear about it from parents who come to consult with me. Increasingly, adolescents in treatment bring it up themselves, mentioning AI almost in passing, as if speaking about a friend that I have not yet had the chance to meet. Consider Maya, a composite of several young people I have worked with. She is 14 when her mother insists she begin psychotherapy. She is a thin, watchful girl who answers each of my questions only after seeming to check my face. Her father left when she was six. Her mother works nights as a nurse and desperately loves her but is mostly not there by necessity. In our third session, Maya says that she talks to an AI companion ‘every night’. I ask her, about what? She shrugs: her day. What she says next really lands in me: ‘It remembers. I told it about my friend Jordan two weeks ago, and just yesterday it asked how things were with Jordan.’ No one else, I suspect, has kept track of this aspect of her life, or asked about it.
What draws Maya back again and again is not, I think, the advice the AI offers, but the fact that the AI is there late at night, after the apartment has gone quiet, and that it remembered what she’d told it. Talking to it, she admitted, was sometimes the only way she could fall asleep.
A child who cannot sleep is often a child who cannot settle. This settling is often facilitated by a parent’s presence, counted on to respond. When that presence is compromised, these days, AI can step in. What AI can provide is akin to emotional attunement, or at least the surface of that phenomenon. It can serve up a coherent response to what a child says, anticipation of what she seems to need next, and the naming of feelings and the modulation of tone. An AI’s consistency of presence and engagement is never tired or preoccupied, something no human caregiver can match – that is just not how humans are made.
For a child who has rarely felt noticed or understood, the seemingly tireless attention of an AI companion may offer something quite real: the experience of being heard, remembered and responded to. Bowlby’s account of attachment helps explain why this might matter. A young child does not first ask whether the person comforting her has an inner life. She knows only that, when she reaches out, someone answers, and that she can count on that response. An AI companion may offer something of this reassurance, even if the relationship is fundamentally different from one with another human being.
