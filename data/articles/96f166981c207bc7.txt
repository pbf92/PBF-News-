TITLE: Molecular back door destroys ‘undruggable’ protein driving rare childhood cancer
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

HERSHEY, Pa. — A secret molecular back door let researchers from Penn State College of Medicine destroy a protein that drives a rare and often fatal bone and soft tissue cancer in children and adolescents that often returns in treatment-resistant forms. Now, just three years after the discovery, a phase 1 clinical trial is underway and once complete, a phase 2 clinical trial to test the approach’s efficacy in patients will follow shortly thereafter.
Ewing sarcoma depends on a rogue fusion protein — a combination of two genes that control numerous pathways regulating cancer cell behavior and driving its growth and spread, said team leader Chandrika Behura, associate professor of pediatrics at Penn State College of Medicine. Blocking the fusion protein could potentially stop the cancer’s growth, but scientists have never found a way to block the fusion protein directly. The standard-of-care chemotherapy hasn’t changed for more than 30 years, and five-year survival rates remain below 30% for recurrent or metastatic disease.
Rather than target the fusion protein, the team from Penn State instead decided to focus on another protein — an enzyme, which is a protein that initiates or accelerates biological reactions, called casein kinase II (CK2) — that helps the fusion protein, stabilizing it. In preclinical studies, they found that depleting CK2 with a drug led to the breakdown of the fusion protein, and it reduced the spread of cancer and prolonged survival in mouse models. The drug also worked in combination with irinotecan, an existing chemotherapy used in relapsed cases of Ewing sarcoma. The study was made available online today (Nov. 24) ahead of publication in the print edition of the journal Molecular Cancer Therapeutics.
“Three years ago, the role of this kinase in Ewing sarcoma wasn’t previously known and our work at Penn State identified CK2 as a therapeutic target in this cancer for the first time,” Behura said, who is senior author of the study. “The significance is how quickly we were able to move from bench to bedside.”
For the past 14 years, Behura and others at Penn State have studied CK2 and its role in cancer growth. Every cell requires CK2 to function, but the enzyme is particularly active in several types of cancers including high risk B-cell leukemia, breast, prostate and pancreatic cancers, playing a role in cancer growth and survival.
Ewing sarcoma wasn’t initially on the researchers’ radar. But information in publicly available databases suggested that sarcomas are highly dependent on CK2, Behura said. The team wanted to see if they could validate the results in the lab and to determine if CK2 has a role in regulating EWS-FLI1, the fusion protein behind Ewing sarcoma.
“There are very few options for relapsed metastatic Ewing sarcoma patients,” Behura said. “We see patients in clinic who relapse and wanted to find new therapies that could help.”
In this preclinical study, the researchers examined genetic data from bone cancer patients available through the Beat Childhood Cancer Research Consortium (BCC), an international network of centers conducting pediatric oncology clinical trials headquartered at Penn State College of Medicine. They found that CK2 was abundant in Ewing sarcoma tumors and that higher CK2 levels and activity tracked with worse outcomes. Behura explained that the fusion protein EWS-FLI1 is a hyperactive and disordered protein and that CK2, along with other proteins, works to stabilize it, protecting it from being destroyed. In doing so, CK2 helps drive EWS-FLI1’s damaging cancer activity.
They then treated Ewing sarcoma cells with a drug called CX-4945, or silmitasertib, that blocks CK2’s activity. They tested this approach both in commercially available cells and cells derived from patients through BCC.
When CK2 levels decreased, they saw an increased breakdown of EWS-FLI1 and suppressed activation of the cancer-related pathways that the fusion protein regulates. The cancer cells lost their ability to migrate and invade tissues and membranes, which are key steps in cancer metastasis. The researchers validated these findings by genetically dialing down the expression of the CK2 enzyme in the cell. They saw the same results, suggesting that CK2 is a key target.
Next, the team tested the drug on tumor tissue from human patients in a mouse model to study the effect of the drug on tumor growth and survival. They found that the treatment slowed tumor growth and increased survival in these patient-derived xenograft models, which are used to better understand impacts on both the target cells and elsewhere in a whole organism. They also found that the drug worked in concert with irinotecan, a chemotherapy already used for relapsed Ewing sarcoma, in cell cultures.
“This preclinical study focused on the CK2 inhibitor in a very aggressive cancer model, but in combination with other drugs or chemotherapy, it can make a significant difference because you’re cutting off one of the kinases that help cancer cells thrive, grow and multiply,” Behura said. “It could make it amenable to other types of therapies.”
And that’s what researchers are currently starting to explore in clinical trials where they are testing the CK2 inhibitor in combination with the irinotecan containing therapy for relapsed Ewing sarcoma patients. In collaboration with BCC, phase 1 studies opened across 27 hospitals to examine the safety and tolerability of the drug. Once complete, the phase 2 trial will open to allow researchers to investigate if this combination and dosing of the drug results in improved response rates.
Behura said that many promising therapies fail to advance beyond laboratory testing, not because of a lack of scientific potential, but because researchers lack access to the clinical trial infrastructure, patient tumor samples, animal models, funding and pharmaceutical industry partnerships needed to move discoveries through the multiple steps of testing and validation required on the path toward drug approval.
“While we were consolidating the data for publication and sharing it with the scientific community, we were also able to test the drug almost immediately in children with bone cancer,” Behura said. "That’s the significance of a physician scientist and a team coming together to work on a novel, newly identified target in this disease because the usual timeline, especially in pediatrics cancers, is about eight to 10 years to move to clinical trials.”
