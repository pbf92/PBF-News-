TITLE: How ‘Good Burdens’ Bring You to the Lord’s Table
OUTLET: Christianity Today

In Philippians 4:8, we read, “Whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is commendable, if there is any excellence, if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things” (ESV throughout).
Notice what is missing from this list of worthy pursuits: whatever is quick, easy, effortless, fast, or purely productive.
Paul’s words of encouragement to the church in Philippi focuses on permanent, enduring things. Nevertheless, so many of the devices and gadgets in our home and kitchen are about making things quick, easy, effortless, and fast. And with good reason: Who would ever choose to make dinner the hard way? Who would ever want kitchen tools that take more time and effort?
Albert Borgmann would. Borgmann, a philosopher of technology and author of Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life, suggests that there can be such a thing as “good burdens.” Modern technology—especially household gadgets and devices—seeks to eliminate any and every burden imaginable. Having to walk across the room to change the radio station is a burden, so Amazon gave us Alexa. Having to keep a grocery list is a burden, so refrigerator companies gave us smart refrigerators with AI kitchen assistants.
Borgmann, however, thinks there can be such a thing as a good burden—a task that is not only burdensome, but also good for us. Preparing “the great meal of the day” and engaging in “the culture of the table” are, according to Borgmann, good burdens. Making and eating a meal with others takes time, effort, practice, discipline, and focus. And yet it is one of the greatest goods in life. The work is hard, but it is good.
Not only does a shared meal around a table gather, but it also unfolds. The subtle interplay between the dish and the cook, the ingredients and the location, play against each other to create a unique experience. A meal with others has a structure and courses that unfold over time. Even a single-course meal takes on a structure of greeting and gathering, pouring drinks and setting plates, sitting and blessing, commencing and conversing, enjoying and departing. All the while, we may not even be aware of how we are engaging in the ancient disciplines of conversation, social engagement, and table manners while we eat.
In contrast, time-saving foods like fast food, snacks, convenience foods, and premade meals reduce food to a factory-sealed commodity to deliver calories. The packaging of these foods facilitates scattering, not gathering. Technological food is packaged to make it easy for us to consume while we are scattered around the house or around town.
No wonder we feel cluttered and distracted as we hurriedly eat factory-made protein bars in a traffic jam while five minutes late for soccer practice. These sorts of meals compress all the courses into one object and distill table manners down to mere grabbing and going. The hands—or machines—preparing the meal are anonymous. There is no who or where behind these meals, just a what.
Is it burdensome to make a meal and eat it with others? Yes.
Is it faster and easier to grab a premade meal and eat it on the run? Yes.
Both provide calories. Yet the former provides so much more. Along with the nourishment, a meal made together and enjoyed around a table involves generosity and gratitude, relationship and community, experience and tradition.
You may be considering some objections as you read these words: It costs too much to prepare a meal. It takes too much time. Only wealthy or retired people can live like this. I’d rather buy bread instead of tending to a sourdough starter like it’s an angry Tamagotchi living on my kitchen counter.
In reality, incorporating a few intentional “good burdens” into your life may be cheaper and easier than you think. Good burdens like preparing a meal or brewing pour-over coffee are often cheaper than premade meals and K-Cup coffee. According to research published in Public Health Nutrition, a peer-reviewed academic journal, “homemade meals have better nutritional characteristics, are cheaper and have lower greenhouse gas emissions, especially those that are plant-based.” And single-serve digital coffee makers cost far more—both initially and per serving—than analog forms of brewing coffee. It’s really not the cost that holds many people back; it’s convenience and time.
A meaningful shift away from hurry can happen in small and incremental ways. Nobody is asking you to destroy your crockpot, coffee maker, or any other kitchen appliance. Instead, this is a call to replace a few devices aimed at speed, ease, and consistency with tools that support gathering, community, and hospitality. Just a few.
Swap a prepackaged dinner on the couch with a meal at the table with others just once a week. Swap an automatic kitchen device out for a tool that invites you to engage with gratitude and awareness.
As you welcome a few good burdens into your home and kitchen, you will eventually find yourself asking: Is it worth it? Is it really worth the extra time and effort to make a meal rather than getting it out of a box or bag? Is it really worth it to turn on a stove rather than push a button? Is it really worth it to put on pants, clean the house, and invite someone over to join in breaking bread?
When you realize that Jesus has joined you at your table, you will find how worthwhile it is to add a few good burdens to your life.
It’s easy to overlook how something as simple as making a meal and eating it at a table with others can be a place where we encounter the presence of Jesus. Yet this is exactly what happens—Jesus is revealed in the breaking of bread together, in the gathering around a table, and in drinking from a shared vessel. From the first miracle to the empty tomb, the Gospels are a veritable grocery store of food and drink, eating and breaking bread with others in the presence of Jesus.
Of all the miracles Jesus performs—raising the dead, walking on water, calming storms—the miracle at Cana (John 2) has a certain strangeness to it. In his inaugural miracle to set the tone of his public ministry, Jesus helps an embarrassed bride and groom who have run out of wine at a wedding? Really? The Son of God chose saving a wedding party as his opening act?
Reynolds Price, a literary critic at Duke University, sees this as evidence for the historicity of the Gospels. In his book Three Gospels, Price argues this is proof that the Gospels are accurate and trustworthy. Price describes this miracle as a “homely kitchen-wonder” that was so inexplicable that it simply had to have happened. Who would choose a “kitchen-wonder” as a fabricated miracle to dupe people into thinking Jesus was a miracle worker?
From Cana onward, Jesus continued to break bread, gather at tables, and make time for hospitality. Jesus gathered at tables with tax collectors and sinners with such regularity that he was accused of being “a glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners” (Luke 7:34). Jesus shared meals with Pharisees (7:36), friends (Luke 10:38–42; Matt. 26:17–30), and the masses (Matt. 14:13–21). Even after his death and resurrection, Jesus went right on eating, drinking, and gathering people around tables.
At a table somewhere near Emmaus, Jesus broke bread with two disciples and opened their eyes to his presence while giving them a weirdly wonderful kind of heartburn (Luke 24:30–35). Immediately after that, Jesus appeared to more of his disciples and dined with them while they “disbelieved for joy and were marveling” (v. 41). Some of the earliest post-resurrection words that Jesus said to his disciples were, “Come and have breakfast” (John 21:12).
After destroying death and paying the full price for human sin, what is top of mind for Jesus to do next? Make a big ol’ brunch.
But there is a reason why breakfast is so important to Jesus after the cross and the empty tomb. Saying “Come and have breakfast” is simply another way of saying “Death is now behind us.”
Isaiah 25 proclaims God’s plans to one day swallow up death forever. When God swallows up death forever, feasting will follow: “On this mountain the Lord of hosts will make for all peoples a feast of rich food, a feast of well-aged wine, of rich food full of marrow, of aged wine well refined” (v. 6, ESV). With death behind us, we can truly eat, drink, and be merry.
Unlike the hopeless hedonism of “eat and drink, for tomorrow we die” (Isa. 22:13, emphasis added), this is a hope-filled eating, drinking, and merriment that comes with knowing that death is behind us. Because of the resurrection of Jesus, we can “eat, drink, and be merry” for death is behind us.
Jesus did exactly this for Lazarus and Peter’s mother-in-law. Lazarus had died and was even rotting in the tomb for four days when Jesus called him out (John 11). As a portent of resurrections to come, Jesus called Lazarus out of the tomb and brought him back to life. After, “they gave a dinner for [Jesus] there. Martha served, and Lazarus was one of those reclining with him at table” (John 12:2). With death behind him, Lazarus broke bread with Jesus.
The same thing happened with Peter’s mother-in-law. While not dead, she was dying with a fever (Matt. 8:14–17). Jesus put death behind her as he touched her, and the fever left her. What did she do next? She served Jesus, most likely by making a meal: “And she rose and began to serve him.” With death behind her, she broke bread with Jesus.
Saying “Come and have breakfast” or “Come and make a meal with me” is an invitation to eat, drink, and be merry because death is behind us. Inviting others to share a cup of coffee, enjoy a stack of pancakes, or make dinner together is an opportunity for Jesus to be revealed among us.
Gathering around a pot of coffee prepares us to gather around the common cup that Jesus has prepared for us for the forgiveness of sins (Matt. 26:28). Gathering around a platter of pancakes teaches our hearts and minds, souls and stomachs to anticipate the breaking of bread in the Lord’s Supper. Gathering around a kitchen table is a short step away from gathering around the Lord’s Table.
Before inviting people to church, we might start by inviting them to breakfast. We may be surprised by how easy it is to find Jesus sitting among us at our kitchen table.
