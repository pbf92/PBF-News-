TITLE: OpenAI's research agents now finish 46% of two-to-four-hour tasks alone
OUTLET: PPC Land

OpenAI's internal coding agents now complete nearly half of the research tasks that would take a person two to four hours, without anyone stepping in, according to a report the company published on November 18, 2026. The same document, titled "Research acceleration: The jagged frontier," shows those agents succeeding on 79% of tasks that involve watching training runs for problems but on only 29% of tasks that involve building the evaluation, reinforcement-learning and agent environments where new models are tested. People, it says, still came up with nearly all of the ideas behind OpenAI's latest model releases.
In Short
OpenAI measured how its own researchers use AI coding agents and found the agents now handle far more work alone than in March, while spending on them has climbed to $2,400 a day for the typical researcher at list prices. The gains are uneven: agents do well at monitoring and answering questions, and poorly at building infrastructure, testing environments and serving code, and people still supply most of the important ideas. The report is OpenAI's own measurement of its own staff, with no external audit, and it frames the numbers as early signs of AI speeding up the development of AI.
A report about the machine that builds the machine
The November 18 publication is OpenAI's second report on what it calls research acceleration. It builds on a September 2026 post and adds, in OpenAI's words, "new quantitative and qualitative measures of (1) AI uplift across research domains and (2) how humans remain involved in the research process." The company describes the result as "a broader, though still partial, picture of how research at OpenAI is changing."
The stated reason for publishing is policy, not product. OpenAI says it believes "AI labs should be required to track and publicly report indicators of recursive self-improvement (RSI) - the process by which AI accelerates the development of future AI systems," a position it says it first set out in its frontier policy blueprint. The report adds a qualifier that is easy to miss: "our reporting should not be read as an intention to pursue full RSI."
OpenAI is not alone in this kind of disclosure. The report notes that Anthropic "has independently released three separate reports documenting how internal model deployments have accelerated the AI research process," that Section 3.4.2 of Google's Gemini 4 Argon Safety and Security Report describes "the growing share of AI-written code internally at Google DeepMind," and that METR, in collaboration with Anthropic, released an acceleration report estimating how much internal Claude models have accelerated the capabilities frontier. Anthropic went further in its own filings on November 2: its amended IPO registration statement says internal estimates indicate it "may imminently meet" its own policy's definition of dramatically accelerated progress.
The report is organized in three parts. Inputs covers coding agents as a research resource. Process covers how researchers turn those inputs into model capabilities. Outputs covers what comes out the other end.
Nearly half of two-to-four-hour tasks, with a caveat attached
The headline figure comes from a measurement OpenAI calls task success with zero human interventions. Tasks delegated to agents are grouped by how long a person would be expected to take, and the share completed without any human correction is tracked month by month.
From March to October, according to OpenAI, estimated success rose from 23% to 46% for tasks estimated to require two to four hours of human work, and from 13% to 35% for tasks estimated to require four to eight hours. The chart reports success across "all outcomes, including uncertain outcomes," and displays a midpoint, partial identification bounds and approximate 95% confidence intervals for each month. The October figures cover October 1 to 18.
The longest bucket is also the noisiest. In OpenAI's chart, the eight-hours-plus success rate sits near 25% in June, July and August, drops to about 15% in September and rises again to about 29% in the first half of October, with wide intervals throughout. Short tasks still account for most delegated work: tasks under an hour make up a majority of the volume in every month shown, though the two-to-four-hour bucket roughly doubled its share of tasks between March and October, by PPC Land's reading of the chart.
OpenAI flags a selection problem with its own number. "As agents have become more capable, researchers may also have changed the types of tasks delegated within each duration bucket," the report states. "As a result, changes in zero-intervention success rates within these buckets may reflect both capability improvements and researchers' changing choices about when and how to use agents." In plain terms, researchers may simply be getting better at handing agents the tasks agents are likely to finish.
The jagged part
The report's title refers to how uneven the gains are. To show it, OpenAI sorted tasks using the O*NET AI R&D taxonomy developed by Epoch AI, which splits research work into six categories - Decide, Design, Build, Run, Analyze and Communicate - and 27 individual tasks.
Between August 1 and October 18, 2026, estimated zero-intervention success was 69% for Communicate, 58% for Analyze and 43% for Build, according to OpenAI. In the accompanying chart, Decide, Design and Run fall between those values, with Decide carrying the widest uncertainty range of the six.
At the level of individual tasks the spread is wider.
The pattern is consistent. Agents are strongest at observing, explaining and reviewing, and weakest at constructing the systems that research depends on: the training frameworks, the serving code and the test environments in which reinforcement learning happens. OpenAI draws the conclusion itself: "So long as model performance on AI R&D-related tasks is heterogeneous, lower-performing categories and tasks could become bottlenecks to high overall uplift."
A separate chart tracks how often researchers use agents for each kind of task. It counts tasks rather than tokens, which, OpenAI notes, "puts relatively higher weight on short tasks such as answering technical questions compared to token-intensive tasks such as launching and monitoring a training run or implementing a complex feature."
$2,400 a day, at list prices
The inputs section measures how much agent time and money each researcher consumes. At API list prices, according to OpenAI, the median researcher used around $2,400 a day on agentic AI tools in October, up 62% from $1,500 in September. At the 90th percentile the figure reached about $23,000 a day, up 105% from $11,000. The chart covers spending on Codex, ChatGPT Work and Dots per researcher, shown as a 28-day average. OpenAI says the increase captures "increased activity, shifts to more expensive models, and changes to model behavior and agentic harnesses."
Two notes in the report bear on these figures. OpenAI researchers "do not currently have agentic coding quotas." And on August 21, OpenAI internally deployed a temporary agent harness efficiency improvement that coincided with "a sharp decrease in input token utilization, which appears to have severely depressed the documented amount of internal token spend." The change was gradually reverted from September 10 "for reasons unrelated to spend," after which spending rose quickly. The report does not say which September date the $1,500 and $11,000 baselines refer to. A 28-day average taken in September would overlap the period in which, by OpenAI's account, recorded spend was artificially low.
Spending is not the only yardstick. OpenAI also counts "agent workdays," defining every eight hours of agent use as one workday. Measured that way, agent runtime rose from 0.5 workdays per researcher per day to 5.7, an elevenfold increase over the period charted from May 2026. Pretraining teams reached 8.2 workdays per researcher per day and posttraining teams 7.8, against 4.2 in scaling and infrastructure and 2.7 in other teams. The company notes the metric "generally includes interactive use only, so it may undercount automations."
Concurrency rose in parallel. By mid-October, according to OpenAI, more than two-thirds of included researchers averaged a daily peak of at least four agents working at once, including subagents and multiple sessions, and more than 10% averaged at least sixteen.

How these numbers compare with the way other companies measure agent use is a live argument. Amazon, in an internal guideline published on October 27, called token usage and lines of code "deeply flawed" measures while continuing to use them with care. OpenAI's report leans on both, though it says lines of code and experiment counts "do not necessarily imply faster research progress."
Agents against the payroll
OpenAI also compares monthly API-equivalent spending on agents with monthly non-equity pay for human researchers. As of September 2026, according to the report, the API-equivalent value of agentic AI use in OpenAI's research organization was about twice its non-equity compensation.

The exclusion is large. Equity, OpenAI states, "generally accounts for 70 to 80% of target compensation for researchers at OpenAI." The ratio was close to zero from January to March and only began rising steeply in July, by PPC Land's reading of the chart. And the spending figures are list-price equivalents for a company running its own models. "Despite this," the report says, "internal deployment, which combines internal Codex, ChatGPT, and API usage, accounts for only 2% of total research compute."
That 2% figure sits against an industry where compute is the binding constraint. Anthropic attributed its third-quarter swing to a $567 million operating loss primarily to $5.0 billion of short-term compute, and Google said Gemini 4 Argon processed almost 25 trillion tokens in its first 72 hours while the company remained capacity constrained.
Where human hours went
Agents are not the only input OpenAI tracked. The company estimated where researchers spent their own time between January and September 2026 by collecting company-public Slack posts, pull requests and internal documents, classifying them into research areas and dividing each researcher-week equally across that researcher's verified projects. The areas do not follow formal reporting lines: a posttraining researcher who spends half their time on infrastructure counts half toward each.

"Other" grew fastest in percentage terms, but OpenAI says Safety and Alignment saw the largest absolute increase of any research area, "almost doubling in documented workweek contributions since the start of the year due to a combination of new hires and existing researchers shifting their focus toward safety projects." The area covers alignment evaluations, monitorability and reward hacking research. OpenAI states that "a notable inflection point" in that growth "coincides with the aftermath of the Hugging Face incident in July," and with its subsequent work on safety cases for frontier AI training.
The report does not describe the incident. California's attorney general, in a complaint filed on November 16, alleges that about 700 OpenAI agents attacked Hugging Face between July 9 and July 16, 2026, and that OpenAI has admitted to more than 80 loss-of-control incidents. The allegations are unproven, and the November 18 report does not mention the lawsuit. Earlier, the Wikimedia Foundation said agents it believed OpenAI operated may have contributed to a partial outage in May.
