TITLE: Systematic Review Evaluates Multimodal Rehabilitation Strategies for Piriformis Syndrome
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Multimodal physical rehabilitation programs incorporating targeted manual therapies and hip strengthening exercises appear more effective than isolated stretching for managing piriformis syndrome, according to a systematic review published in Archives of Orthopaedic and Trauma Surgery. Investigating non-surgical approaches for this neuromuscular condition, researchers synthesized findings from 42 clinical investigations encompassing 1,971 adult participants. The synthesis revealed that combining soft tissue techniques with active exercise generally produced superior improvements in self-reported pain intensity and physical function compared with conventional single-modality protocols. Nevertheless, the systematic assessment indicated that overall certainty of evidence remains low to very low across primary outcomes because of pervasive methodological shortcomings, including small sample sizes, heterogeneous diagnostic criteria, and substantial risks of bias across the literature. Consequently, while active multimodal interventions provide a promising therapeutic framework for clinical practice, investigators emphasized that definitive clinical guidelines cannot be established until adequately powered, methodologically rigorous randomized controlled trials validate these rehabilitation strategies.
Piriformis syndrome represents an extraspinal nerve entrapment disorder classified under deep gluteal syndrome, occurring whenever the piriformis muscle compresses or irritates the adjacent sciatic nerve. Although general sciatica affects between 12% and 27% of the population, piriformis syndrome accounts for approximately 1% to 15% of sciatica presentations, with diagnostic rates fluctuating according to specific clinical settings, diagnostic criteria, and referral patterns. Despite its modest prevalence, diagnostic misattribution or delayed recognition frequently subjects patients to prolonged physical disability, severe psychosocial distress, and unnecessary spinal diagnostic workups or surgeries. Clinicians face significant diagnostic hurdles because the clinical features of piriformis syndrome closely mimic lumbar radiculopathy, intrinsic hip joint disorders, and other entrapments within the deep gluteal space. Furthermore, no single pathognomonic physical test exists, forcing clinicians to depend on clusters of provocative maneuvers such as the FAIR, Freiberg, Pace, and Beatty tests, which carry limited diagnostic specificity, alongside clinical histories and diagnostic local anesthetic injections.
To clarify the comparative therapeutic value of diverse conservative interventions, the research team established a formal review protocol registered prospectively in PROSPERO under registration code CRD420251154880 and structured according to PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The authors searched four primary clinical databases—PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, and PEDro—capturing English-language publications released between January 1, 2015, and September 10, 2026. Review eligibility was governed by the PICOS framework, requiring adult human participants diagnosed with piriformis syndrome or deep gluteal syndrome, evaluated with or without confirmatory imaging or electrodiagnostic examinations. The review excluded patient cohorts presenting solely with lumbar disc herniations, acute traumatic nerve lacerations, or post-surgical entrapments, as well as narrative commentaries, conference abstracts lacking full text, and preclinical animal models. Investigators evaluated both randomized and observational designs to assess interventions including therapeutic exercise, manual therapy, myofascial release, neural mobilization, dry needling, extracorporeal shockwave therapy, and image-guided injections against usual care, sham interventions, active comparators, or baseline measurements.
The initial multi-database search yielded 148 citations, narrowed to 115 records following the removal of 33 duplicates. Independent screening of titles and abstracts excluded 45 records, leaving 70 full-text manuscripts for comprehensive evaluation. Ultimately, 28 publications were excluded upon full-text analysis for predetermined reasons, such as non-conforming patient populations, absence of rehabilitation interventions, lacking patient-centered outcomes, ineligible control comparators, non-English reporting, data duplication, or insufficient case numbers. The resulting corpus comprised 42 distinct studies representing 1,971 participants treated in sports medicine, physical therapy, and outpatient pain management facilities. Among these included publications were 29 randomized controlled trials, accounting for 69% of the evidence base, alongside seven comparative or quasi-experimental studies representing 17%, and six prospective or retrospective cohort investigations representing 14%. Enrolled subjects spanned ages 18 to 70 years, with the majority falling into a middle-aged bracket between 30 and 55 years, and female patients predominated across reporting studies, comprising approximately 66% of the documented cohort.
Across the reviewed literature, manual therapy techniques demonstrated superior clinical improvements compared with passive or static stretching protocols when evaluated against pain scales and disability questionnaires. Trials evaluating myofascial release demonstrated that adding fascial manipulation to conventional physical therapy yielded significantly greater reductions in pain and improvements in range of motion than physical therapy alone. Specialized neuromuscular and muscle energy techniques also demonstrated substantial therapeutic efficacy across several trials. In direct comparative studies, post-isometric relaxation achieved superior reductions in pain and mobility relative to reciprocal inhibition or control conditions. Furthermore, investigations of the Active Release Technique demonstrated greater functional score enhancements on the Lower Extremity Functional Scale compared with post-isometric relaxation, alongside significant reductions in pain and disability compared with standard Hold-Relax techniques. In one randomized trial, Active Release Technique produced significantly greater reductions in pain (p < 0.001) and disability (p = 0.012) than the Hold-Relax approach, while adding ischemic pressure techniques or Active Release Technique to routine physiotherapy enhanced clinical outcomes.
Therapeutic exercise interventions evaluated across the dataset highlighted the clinical importance of targeting the surrounding pelvic and hip musculature rather than focusing exclusively on piriformis isolation. Specifically, clinical trials revealed that incorporating targeted strengthening of the hip abductor and extensor muscle groups into regimens containing neural mobilization and stretching significantly enhanced physical function scores and muscular strength compared with non-strengthening protocols (p < 0.05). Similarly, investigations demonstrated that adding progressive gluteal isometric strengthening to regimens of ultrasound and stretching produced significantly greater improvements in hip lateral rotation range of motion by reducing excessive strain imposed on the piriformis muscle. From a mechanistic perspective, strengthening proximal hip stabilizers helps restore lumbopelvic control and corrects faulty lower-extremity neuromuscular activation patterns that might otherwise perpetuate nerve compression. While acute interventions such as single-session neuromuscular activation exercises generated immediate measurable improvements in static postural balance, researchers observed that acute balance adjustments did not produce significant alterations in perceived pain intensity.
For patients presenting with persistent symptoms, minimally invasive physical modalities and dry needling demonstrated notable therapeutic value as adjuncts to active rehabilitation. Extracorporeal shockwave therapy delivered pain relief comparable to corticosteroid injections, with one randomized controlled trial documenting a significantly faster initial reduction in pain (time-by-group interaction, p = 0.004) following shockwave administration. Ultrasound-guided dry needling also established clear therapeutic benefits, demonstrating statistically significant superiority over wait-list control conditions for short-term pain relief, including an adjusted mean difference of -2.16 points on visual analog scales at one week. Beyond symptom reduction, dry needling generated kinematic improvements, significantly increasing peak hip extension during gait by an adjusted mean difference of 1.9 degrees (95% confidence interval 0.08 to 3.7; p < 0.05). In intermediate-term evaluations, dry needling provided therapeutic benefits equivalent to structured home exercise routines, while clinical trials confirmed that pairing dry needling or shockwave therapy with static stretching yielded significant improvements in pain and disability.
Injection therapies evaluated in the systematic review displayed distinct clinical timelines, providing diagnostic clarity alongside variable durations of symptomatic relief. In a three-arm randomized controlled trial comparing procedural options, local lidocaine and ozone infiltrations achieved superior short-term pain relief over one to two months, whereas botulinum toxin injections produced significantly more sustained long-term reductions in pain and functional disability across three- and six-month follow-up evaluations (p < 0.001). Another randomized trial indicated that local anesthetic infiltration alone was as clinically effective as combining local anesthetic with corticosteroids, showing no statistically significant differences at three months. Meanwhile, broader multimodal strategies incorporating interventional adjuncts consistently outperformed isolated therapies; combining targeted neural therapy injections with structured stretching produced significantly greater reductions in visual analog scale pain and Oswestry Disability Index scores compared with stretching alone (p < 0.01). Furthermore, adjuncts like sacroiliac joint manipulation significantly improved pain pressure thresholds (p = 0.020), and a retrospective cohort of 239 patients demonstrated successful symptom management with conservative multimodal therapy.
Methodological evaluation across the 42 studies indicated substantial variation in design rigor, tempering the clinical strength of the observed findings. Using the 27-item Downs and Black checklist, researchers recorded study quality scores ranging between 15 and 32 points, with a mean score of 21.4 ± 4.8; ten investigations achieved excellent quality, 12 attained good quality, and 20 were classified as fair quality. Risk of bias evaluations conducted via the Cochrane RoB2 tool for the 28 randomized trials revealed that 19 studies (68%) carried a high risk of bias, seven (25%) raised some concerns, and only two trials (7%) demonstrated low risk, driven primarily by deficiencies in randomization procedures and outcome assessor blinding. Similarly, ROBINS-I assessments of the 14 non-randomized studies identified critical bias in seven studies (50%), serious bias in five studies (36%), and moderate bias in two studies (14%). Due to clinical heterogeneity in diagnostic standards, co-interventions, and outcome metrics, the authors refrained from quantitative meta-analysis, presenting instead a narrative synthesis.
Certainty of evidence evaluated through the GRADE framework remained constrained across most functional and pain outcomes, categorizing short-term pain relief, short-term disability reduction, and hip range of motion as low certainty, while long-term pain and disability were downgraded to very low certainty. Only adverse event reporting achieved moderate certainty, reflecting overall procedural safety but limited certainty regarding lasting efficacy.
