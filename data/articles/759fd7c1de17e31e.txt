TITLE: GTA 6′ s Tenebrae Lux Is A Hilarious Parody Of Video Game Auteurs, And A Surprisingly Good Puzzle Diversion
OUTLET: Kotaku

“[Jonathan Zuch] is such a genius,” gushes Cal as he sets up a JoySystem in Jason’s living room. “He’d love to work with other people, but he’s too fuckin’ good at everything. It’d just slow him down. You know, the time it takes him to explain something, he could just make shit quicker. You can see it on the screen, man, it’s so fuckin’ raw.”
Jason, ever-stoic, doesn’t seem fully convinced, mostly going along with things for the enjoyment of spending time with his buddy. I wasn’t really convinced either when GTA VI forced me into the short intro of Tenebrae Lux. But despite the entire thing mostly existing as a completely unsubtle barb directed at one indie game auteur in particular, Tenebrae Lux hooked me immediately by showing off a series of surprisingly mind-bending puzzles. While some (Cal) might expect such things from a genius like Zuch, I wasn’t at all prepared to bust out my puzzle notebook while playing Grand Theft Auto VI!
Like most in-universe media in GTA VI, Tenebrae Lux is a giant, heaving mass of satire. Rendered in black and white and dripping with angst, it follows a Man Who Won’t Shut The Fuck Up who has washed up on an island decorated by some crumbling shacks and a single lighthouse whose beam swings rhythmically ’round the dark landscape as you explore. It’s moody as hell: swaying grass, dark and ominous music, too-bright lights in the distance indicating where you might want to poke around, and a haunting crying sound of some sort in the background. Hell, before you even get to any of that, you’re shown what I’m sure is intended to be a very deep and meaningful quote scrawled across the opening screen: “Every tale begins with an unwritten page quietly awaiting the ink of destiny…” -R.T. Gravenhurst, The Teachings of Twmffat. Apparently, “Twmffat” is a Welsh way to call someone an idiot. Excellent.
The majority of the segment you play with Cal is focused on hammering home the satire. As your character noisily broods his way down the beach, introductory credits roll, attributing every single bit of the game—from the leading voice performance to the writing, music, and “mise-en-scene”—to Johannes Zuch. The initial moody atmosphere and beach themes, as well as the single auteur name on all the credits, made me think at first that the game was lampooning Hideo Kojima, and there’s a little bit of Mark Danielewski going on here too. But as I got into the actual gameplay (and read the name on the credits a few more times) I clocked the actual joke here. “Johannes Zuch” is not-so-subtle when you say it out loud with a short u in Zuch.

All of this is quite funny, and works pretty well for a 10-minute-long-or-so joke sequence that you then cut off to go snuggle in a bed with your bestie and get your girl out of prison. But you gotta dive back in once you’ve left. That’s where the good stuff is. It starts with a couple of basic grid-based puzzles that involve moving pieces around a board to clear all of them. I assumed, wrongly, that the game would just be a bunch of harder variations on that. But then I crossed the beach and entered what seemed like some sort of war bunker, and everything changed.
Or, rather, it stayed…eerily the same.
Tenebrae Lux features what I would consider two major puzzles, with two smaller ones bookending them, but boy, those two major puzzles are doozies. One involves looping through the same room, over and over, trying to figure out a six-digit code needed to proceed while eerie sea shanties wail in your ears and a crazed man yells at you from the other side of a door. The other involves a bridge covered in glowing symbols requiring a specific pattern to cross, found by navigating a nearby ruin and deciphering a deceptively simple poem. In both cases (but especially in the looping rooms), the solutions require a bit of lateral thinking and very close observation to fully work out. I spent a good couple hours on these last night!
What I found most clever about the looping rooms in particular is that the puzzle seems to be different for every player. Checking online, it sounds like the various objects in the room (which are key to solving the puzzle) change depending on your playthrough. Some people had a painting that never appeared in mine, and I had record albums that others never saw. This helps the solution dodge easily researchable answers online but also immediately confused my efforts at information-sharing as I texted friends who had already seen the room for help. It’s a cool trick!
The finale of Tenebrae Lux was as silly and pretentious as the start, though I did find the aesthetics of its final puzzle to be pretty cool in spite of that. The in-universe idea of Tenebrae Lux, by design, says nothing about anything. There’s no deeper meaning to its constant prattling about darkness and light and fate and horror and whatnot. But in saying nothing, the existence of Tenebrae Lux within the context of GTA VI manages to land a pretty stellar blow on a very specific and recognizable kind of auteur culture, while simultaneously providing a few excellent hours of puzzling. Allegedly there’s some sort of secret ending if I go back and collect a bunch of notes, and I find myself…weirdly compelled to do this?
So: inscrutable and entirely meaningless thematically, up-its-own-ass creatively, but with some pretty excellent puzzling despite that? Yup. That’s a Johannes Zuch game.
