TITLE: Daily briefing: How vampire bats survive on blood alone
OUTLET: Nature News

An analysis of vampire bats is offering clues as to how the animals sustain themselves on blood alone. Researchers found that the stomachs of all three vampire-bat species act as a stretchy, pH-neutral storage sac lined with thick layers of connective tissue that help it to balloon after a feast. These ‘stomachs’ contain high levels of fluid-transporting channels that help to shed excess water so the bats can fly home. Genes that encode metabolic pathways that convert blood proteins into fats and sugars are also upregulated in vampire bats, compared with non-vampire species.

A genetic variant that seems to reduce the size of human brain areas involved in visual processing was inherited from Neanderthals. Scraps of Neanderthal DNA have been found in the genomes of many modern humans, but this variant seems to have an unusually powerful effect on brain anatomy, and people who carry the variant also have a reduced risk of schizophrenia. “Its effect is really one of the most beautiful and strong that I’ve ever seen,” says computational biologist Davide Marnetto.
Reference: Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA paper
The US Supreme Court has announced that it won’t intervene in a landmark legal battle over the cancellation of research grants by the US National Science Foundation (NSF) and other federal agencies — at least for now. The decision leaves in place the rulings of several lower courts, which required the reinstatement of hundreds of cancelled awards while a lawsuit brought by a group of scientists against the government proceeds. The Supreme Court’s refusal to hear the case is a relief to scientists whose work was left in limbo, and leaves protections for their reinstated funding in place.
At the UN Climate Change Conference (COP31) in Turkey, ten countries have pledged to reduce the environmental footprint of AI and use the technology to cut emissions elsewhere — for example, by accelerating the development of clean technologies. “The trillion-dollar question is whether or not those savings will outweigh those impacts we’re seeing now,” says Eric Masanet, a mechanical engineer who studies decarbonization. Germany, Brazil, South Korea and Turkey have endorsed the pledge, as have some large tech companies — but none of the frontier AI firms.
The researcher who led an experimental gene-editing trial that resulted in the death of a child has been dismissed from his position by Chinese authorities. An investigation into the trial by the Shanghai municipal government found that the researcher and his team failed to follow regulations and overlooked warning signs in a safety report. The institutions involved in the trial — Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine and Xinhua Hospital — have also been reprimanded and other researchers involved in the trial sanctioned.

The first footage released from the ultraviolet camera on a European-Chinese spacecraft called the Solar wind Magnetosphere Ionosphere Link Explorer (SMILE) shows the full ring of northern lights around Earth’s north pole on 24 July. The bright flashes on the left side of the video are distant stars, which seem to move because of the movement of the orbiting spacecraft. To get the best possible picture of the brain’s inner workings, scientists need to study living, active tissue. Neuroscientists around the world are increasingly teaming up with surgeons to access this tissue, then racing to probe it to capture how neurons communicate. Studying this tissue requires mastery of a complex technique called patch clamping, and quick work — living neurons begin to degrade after a few days in the lab. “As much as we like mice, I don’t want to understand the mouse brain,” says neuroscientist Lucy Palmer. “I want to understand the human brain.”
In The Imagination Scientist, developmental psychologist Paige Davis delves into the reasons why children create imaginary friends. A make-believe buddy can offer children comfort and encouragement when needed, and give them a way to practise dealing with conflict, she writes. The book is “a deeply researched and highly entertaining look at the world of imaginary companions”, writes science journalist Josie Glausiusz in her review, who fondly recalls her own two childhood imaginary friends — the inventively named Boy and Girl.
