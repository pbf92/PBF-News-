The Residency Report: Does donor diabetes affect DMEK outcomes?
NYU Langone’s Casey Zhang, MD, and Andrea Blitzer, MD, host Iowa’s Mark A. Greiner, MD, on corneas from donors with diabetes.
In this installment of “The Residency Report,” NYU Langone ophthalmology resident (PGY3) Casey Zhang, MD, moderates an Ophthalmology Times–NYU Langone Department of Ophthalmology journal club discussion with guest Mark A. Greiner, MD, professor of ophthalmology and the Beulah and Florence Usher Chair in Cornea/External Disease and Refractive Surgery at the University of Iowa Carver College of Medicine. Andrea Blitzer, MD, assistant professor of ophthalmology at NYU Grossman School of Medicine, joins Zhang in hosting.
The discussion centers on 2 companion papers from the Diabetes Endothelial Keratoplasty Study (DEKS), a randomized clinical trial of whether donor diabetes affects Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK). One reports 1-year graft success, and the other reports endothelial cell loss and morphometry at 1 year.1,2 Greiner is an author on both papers.
Episodes in this series
Why donor diabetes raised concern
Zhang notes that diabetes is increasingly prevalent in the US and in the donor cornea pool, and because it affects basement membranes elsewhere in the body, it could plausibly affect Descemet membrane. The discussion traces the practical concern to graft wastage. Almost from the start of eye bank tissue preparation, eye bankers and surgeons recognized that tissue from donors with diabetes tore more often, a finding reported in more than 1 publication and a key driver for the study.
How the trial was designed
DEKS enrolled 1421 eyes at 28 clinical sites, with donor corneas from 13 eye banks. Tissue was assigned in an approximate 2:1 ratio of donors without diabetes to donors with diabetes,1 which the discussion notes reflects the distribution in the US donor pool.
The discussion highlights how rigorously donor diabetes was characterized. A retrospective medical history, gathered from family members shortly after a death, is described as unreliable, so, according to the discussion, DEKS used a postmortem hemoglobin A1c of 6.5% or greater as a quantifiable marker. Some donors without a history of diabetes were reclassified on that basis, which the discussion describes as having public health relevance.
What the trial found
The 1-year cumulative probability of graft success was 96.3% with tissue from donors without diabetes and 97.1% with tissue from donors with diabetes, and donor diabetes severity did not appear to influence success.1 Mean endothelial cell loss at 1 year was 28.3% and 28.0%, respectively, and cell morphometry did not differ between groups.2
Given preclinical evidence that diabetes affects the posterior cornea, Descemet membrane, and corneal endothelium, the results are described in the discussion as surprising. With no separation in cell loss between the groups at 1 month or 1 year, the discussion notes greater confidence that the graft success findings are backed by the biology. Most cell loss appears to occur early, which the discussion attributes to perioperative stressors from donor recovery through surgical handling, after which the tissue appears to reach a new equilibrium. For now, that early pattern does not change day-to-day postoperative practice, according to the discussion.
Tissue preparation and what comes next
One difference the discussion highlights is a higher rate of failed graft preparation with tissue from donors with diabetes. The discussion attributes this not to a learning curve but to increased adhesion between the posterior stroma and Descemet membrane, a long-standing observation the study quantified.
The practical message, according to the discussion, is that successfully prepared tissue from a donor with diabetes can reasonably be expected to function like tissue from a donor without diabetes, which may lower the barrier for eye banks. If 1 cornea from a mated pair tears during preparation, the second can be diverted to a different preparation or technique, such as Descemet stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty, which does not require peeling, or full-thickness keratoplasty.
DEKS has been extended to 3- and 5-year follow-up with continued National Eye Institute funding. A central question is whether cell loss in the 2 groups diverges over time. An ancillary study looked at the frequency of the transcription factor 4 (TCF4) repeat expansion, a genetic risk factor for Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy, in DEKS donors and, according to the discussion, found rates similar to those in the general population; the extension will allow that risk factor to be followed.
