TITLE: Research finds culturally relevant nutrition interventions engage Black adults more effectively
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

November 25, 2026 – A recent study in the Journal of Nutrition Education and Behavior (JNEB), published by Elsevier, explored how researchers can better engage Black adults with hypertension in digital nutrition interventions designed to improve dietary habits and reduce cardiovascular risk.
Researchers interviewed 30 Black adults (16 male) with hypertension about their interest in a digital nutrition program and their preferences for research recruitment. Participants expressed strong interest in an intervention that could help them improve their overall health, build healthy eating and cooking skills, and receive guidance and accountability from a registered dietitian.
The findings highlighted the importance of culturally relevant recruitment materials featuring Black adults across ages, genders, and body sizes, along with appealing healthy foods and the technology used in the intervention. Participants also emphasized that dietitians should understand Black cultural food traditions and lived experiences, noting that cultural competence was more important than the dietitian’s race alone.
“Engaging Black adults in nutrition research requires more than simply sharing information about a study,” said lead investigator, Loneke T. Blackman Carr, PhD, RD, Department of Nutritional Sciences, University of Connecticut, Storrs, CT. “Recruitment materials should reflect people’s lived experiences, and researchers should consider the trusted community spaces and digital platforms people use in their daily lives. Just as importantly, nutrition professionals must understand the cultural and social contexts that shape food choices to help participants make meaningful changes to their health.”
Participants identified both digital platforms, such as social media and YouTube, and trusted community spaces, including churches, as valuable places to share recruitment materials. Preferences varied somewhat by sex: women placed greater emphasis on traditional community settings, while men prioritized digital platforms.
These findings suggest that combining culturally relevant messaging with recruitment through familiar community spaces and digital channels may help researchers better engage Black adults, resulting in their greater representation in nutrition research.
