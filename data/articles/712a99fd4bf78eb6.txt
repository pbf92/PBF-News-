TITLE: California fines Hertz $1.55m over unprocessed browser opt-out signals
OUTLET: PPC Land

California's privacy regulator today made public a decision ordering The Hertz Corporation to pay a $1.55 million administrative fine and to rebuild how the Hertz, Dollar and Thrifty websites and apps respond when a visitor's browser asks them not to sell or share personal data.
In Short
California's privacy agency fined Hertz $1.55 million after finding that, for part of a three-year period, the Hertz, Dollar and Thrifty websites kept passing visitor data to advertising technology companies even when browsers signaled an opt-out. The case matters well beyond car rentals, because the order spells out in technical detail how tracking tags, tag managers and consent tools have to be tested before and after someone opts out. For the next three years Hertz must scan and test its trackers every quarter, check for the browser signal before any ad tracker loads, and publish how many opt-outs arrived through those signals.
The decision
The Board of the California Privacy Protection Agency, which calls itself CalPrivacy, adopted a 15-page Stipulated Final Order in case number ENF25-001-V-HE on November 6, 2026. The Order of Decision, signed by Board chair Jennifer M. Urban, states that the decision "shall become effective immediately." Hertz and the agency's Enforcement Division had both signed the stipulation on October 16, 2026, 21 days before the Board acted. The agency announced the outcome today.
The order is a settlement, not a contested ruling. Hertz agreed to the terms, waived any hearing or appeal, and accepted a fine of $1,550,000 under Civil Code section 1798.199.55. Payment is due "no later than sixty (60) days after the effective date of the Board's decision," which, by a PPC Land calculation, falls on January 5, 2027.
The money is the smaller part of the document. Section VII.B sets out a compliance program that runs for three years from November 6, 2026 and reads, in places, like a test plan for an ad operations team. It covers tracker inventories, vendor contracts, the timing of tag loading, testing in staging and production environments, the behavior of server-side technology, and the reliability of consent vendors.
Who Hertz is in this case
According to the order, Hertz is a Delaware corporation with its principal place of business at 8501 Williams Road, Estero, Florida, and the respondent includes the Hertz, Dollar and Thrifty car rental brands. The order says the company has "over 600 locations and 4,000 employees" in California, rents "tens of thousands of vehicles per month," and collects and processes the personal information of "hundreds of thousands of Californians" who interact with its digital properties and visit its locations each month, with activity "increasing substantially during the summer season."
Hertz meets the CCPA's coverage tests on two counts, according to paragraph 5: annual gross revenue above $26.625 million, and selling or sharing the personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households a year. The order quotes Hertz's own description of itself as "one of the most recognized brands in the world."
The digital properties at issue are hertz.com, dollar.com and thrifty.com and their mobile apps. The relevant period, unless otherwise stated, runs from July 1, 2023 to August 25, 2026, which by a PPC Land calculation is 1,151 days.
Thirteen ad tech names in one paragraph
Paragraph 32 is the part of the order that most directly concerns the advertising market. It lists the companies whose tracking technologies Hertz installed on its digital properties: "Meta, Google, Pinterest, LinkedIn, Microsoft, TikTok, the Trade Desk, LiveRamp, Adobe, Amazon, Sojern, Criteo, Wunderkind, and other AdTech companies, some of which are registered data brokers." The order does not say which of them are registered data brokers.
According to the order, these technologies made personal information such as "personal identifiers and car rental dates" available to the companies "for purposes that include cross-context behavioral advertising." Paragraph 27 adds that the trackers collected "unique user identifiers and rental search parameters." None of the 13 named companies is a party to the case, and the order makes no finding against any of them.
The agency also addressed what happens after the data leaves Hertz's pages. Paragraph 33 describes cookie syncing as "a process in which they match their respective tracking identifiers so they can recognize the same individual across their systems." According to the order, syncing allows ad tech companies to "construct an even more detailed profile of the consumer across the AdTech ecosystem," which deepens "the consumer's exposure to pervasive tracking, targeted advertising, and intrusive inferences about their behavior and interests."
Three findings
The Enforcement Division's factual findings fall into three groups. Hertz neither admits nor denies them, a point covered below.
All three findings are limited to "a portion of the relevant time period." The order does not say which portion.
1. Opt-out signals that were not honored
The central finding concerns opt-out preference signals, the browser-level instruction that California's regulations require businesses to treat as a valid request to opt out of sale or sharing. The order names Global Privacy Control as the example and describes it as "a browser-based technical standard that allows a consumer to communicate or signal to every website they visit that they are exercising their right to opt-out of the sale/sharing." It works, according to paragraph 35, "by sending a standardized HTTP header and setting a JavaScript property that websites can detect."
The agency is blunt about how long the obligation has existed. "California law has required businesses to honor opt-out preference signals for more than six years," paragraph 36 states. The order traces the requirement through three versions of the regulations: section 999.315(c), effective August 14, 2020 to May 4, 2022; section 7026, effective May 5, 2022 to March 28, 2023; and section 7025, effective March 29, 2023 to the present.
Against that history, the finding is short. Although Hertz "installed numerous tracking technologies on its digital properties that facilitated the sale and sharing of consumers' personal information, for a portion of the relevant time period, it failed to configure its digital properties to properly detect and honor consumers' opt-out preference signals." As a result, according to paragraph 39, Hertz "continued to sell and share this personal information with multiple AdTech companies and data brokers."
Once a business receives an opt-out, including through GPC, Civil Code section 1798.120(d) bars it from selling or sharing that consumer's data unless the consumer later gives explicit consent. The order treats every sale or share after an ignored signal as a separate violation. It does not count them.
2. A privacy policy that said nothing about signals
The second finding is narrower. California's regulations require a business operating online to explain in its privacy policy "how an opt-out preference signal will be processed" and how consumers can use one. According to paragraph 42, for part of the period Hertz's privacy policy did neither, and so the company "failed to provide notice to each consumer who visited Hertz's digital properties."
3. The cookie window that reset itself
The third finding comes with screenshots. Figures 1 and 2 of the order show the "Manage Cookies" windows on Dollar.com and Thrifty.com. On the Dollar window, every optional category (functionality, performance and advertising cookies) carries a toggle marked "REJECTED," with "Accept All," "Decline All" and "Save Changes" buttons. The Thrifty window looks the same, with an extra "Unclassified" category.
The toggles showed advertising cookies as already off. According to paragraph 46, "if the consumer closed out of the window, such as by using the 'X' at the top right of the window, without selecting 'Save Changes,' the settings would not hold. The settings reverted to the default settings, which allowed advertising cookies."
The agency's language here is more cautious than in the first two findings. Paragraph 49 says that by "requiring an additional and non-obvious step for consumer choice to take effect, Hertz used interactive elements and choice architecture that may have been confusing which would violate section 7004(a)(3)-(4)." The regulation cited requires that methods for submitting requests avoid confusing interactive elements and that toggles or buttons "clearly indicate the consumer's choice."
What the order requires
According to Section VII of the Stipulated Final Order, Hertz's obligations break down into the following steps. They apply for three years from the November 6, 2026 effective date, which by a PPC Land calculation means until November 6, 2029.
- Fine. Pay $1,550,000 to the agency within 60 days of the effective date, following written instructions from the Enforcement Division (paragraph 54).
- Quarterly tracker inventory. Scan the digital properties "at least quarterly, to maintain a full and current inventory of tracking technologies that sell or share personal information" (paragraph 56). The scope is every Hertz.com, Thrifty.com or Dollar.com domain, including subdomains, plus the mobile apps. Pages that Hertz has taken offline are excluded "even if they are temporarily accessible through caching or Internet archival services."
- Contracts. For each tracker in the inventory, keep contracts that comply with 11 CCR 7053(a)(1)-(6) with every third party to which Hertz directly sells or shares personal information (paragraph 56(a)).
- Configurations that act on opt-outs. Configure the properties to "recognize and give full effect" to opt-outs, whether they arrive "through (1) an opt-out preference signal, such as the Global Privacy Control ('GPC'), or (2) through another method" (paragraph 56(b)). Two sub-requirements follow. On timing: "Hertz must check to see if it has received the consumer's GPC signal before loading tracking technologies that sell or share personal information." On vendors: Hertz must implement "documented instructions provided by any relevant service providers, contractors, or third parties, such as tag managers or consent management platforms." The order gives an example: Hertz "may need to send a restrictive or limited data usage flag to instruct the entity to only use personal information for service provider purposes."
- Testing. Test the configurations "in both staging and production environments at least quarterly, and whenever a new tracking technology is added" (paragraph 56(c)). The tests "should include, but not be limited to, reviewing network activity, tracking technology execution, storage writes, and system behavior before and after an opt-out request." The order lists six conditions the tests must cover: all digital properties; "all tag management containers or profiles"; different browsers and mobile device platforms; "the full inventory of tracking technologies, including server-side and client-side technologies"; "both known and unknown consumers"; and different circumstances, such as a GPC opt-out versus a cookie banner opt-out, first-time visitors "without an existing consent state," and consent preference changes.
- Onboarding review. Run an internal review process before onboarding any third party that receives personal information through a sale or share, covering both the contract and the configuration needed to honor opt-outs (paragraph 57).
- Request channels. Test the methods for submitting CCPA requests at least quarterly (paragraph 58).
- Compliance review. Review CCPA practices within 180 days of the effective date, to the extent not already done (paragraph 59).
- Vendor checks. Assess service providers and consent tools independently, at least twice a year (paragraph 60).
