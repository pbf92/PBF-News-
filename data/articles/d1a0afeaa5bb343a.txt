TITLE: Hospitalized Medical Patients Treated by Female Physicians Show Lower Mortality Rates
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Adult patients hospitalized with common medical conditions experienced lower rates of death and hospital readmission when treated by female physicians compared with those treated by male physicians, according to an observational study published in the October issue of JAMA Network Open. Conducted by researchers at the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences (ICES) and led by an investigator from the University of Toronto, the extensive population-based analysis examined clinical outcomes across hundreds of thousands of inpatient admissions.
The investigation significantly broadens the existing medical literature surrounding clinician demographics and patient care. While earlier studies examining the potential link between physician sex and clinical outcomes concentrated primarily on surgical procedures and focused narrowly on the single admitting physician, the current study expands the inquiry into broader medical disciplines. According to Dr. Angela Jerath, an ICES scientist and Associate Professor in the Department of Anesthesiology and Pain Medicine at the University of Toronto’s Temerty Faculty of Medicine, past research has largely focused on surgical patients and the admitting clinician alone. Dr. Jerath explained that the new findings add to a growing body of scientific research by widening the investigative lens beyond surgical care to a diverse range of common medical illnesses and accounting for multiple attending physicians.
To evaluate these associations on a system-wide scale, the research team analyzed data curated by ICES, an independent, not-for-profit analytics and research institute that uses population-based health information to evaluate health policy, delivery, and population outcomes. The study encompassed 958,150 adult patients admitted to the hospital between January 1, 2008, and December 31, 2022. Each patient included in the dataset had been hospitalized for one of seven common acute medical conditions, which included serious illnesses such as acute myocardial infarction and pneumonia. Within the overall study cohort, 259,759 patients were cared for by female physicians, while 698,391 patients were managed by male physicians during their inpatient stays.
Because care is frequently transferred between multiple physicians during a hospital stay, the researchers designed their evaluation to account for care transitions over time. The investigative team analyzed whether inpatient clinical outcomes varied based on the sex of the designated most responsible physician. In addition, they evaluated the cumulative extent to which patients received direct medical care from female physicians throughout the full duration of their hospitalization.
Patients cared for by female physicians experienced a lower 30-day mortality rate of 12.3 per cent, compared with 13.7 per cent among those cared for by male physicians. Readmission rates back to the hospital within 30 days were also slightly lower for patients treated by female clinicians, recorded at 18.6 per cent compared to 18.9 per cent for those treated by male doctors. Furthermore, when evaluating overall exposure across a patient’s stay, greater exposure to care from female physicians was associated with a seven per cent reduction in the odds of dying within 30 days. The researchers noted that similar protective patterns persisted over extended follow-up intervals, remaining apparent at 90 days and at one year following hospital admission.
The study authors explored several potential behavioral and clinical mechanisms that may help explain why patients cared for by female physicians achieved better outcomes. Clinical literature suggests that female physicians may be more likely to engage in patient-centred communication and spend more time directly interacting with their patients. In addition, female physicians may be more inclined to follow established evidence-based protocols by ordering guideline-directed medical treatments. They may also more consistently consult professional colleagues when managing complex clinical scenarios and engage in shared decision-making with patients and multidisciplinary care teams.
The observational design of the study benefited from a structural feature of hospital medicine that helps strengthen the validity of its comparisons. As Dr. Jerath highlighted, an important methodological strength of studying non-surgical medical admissions is that patients hospitalized for acute medical diagnoses are generally assigned to physicians based on predetermined, rotating on-call hospital schedules rather than personal preference or selective referring patterns.
The researchers emphasized that the core objective of their study is not to foster competition or favor one group of practitioners over another, but rather to pinpoint concrete clinical behaviors, communication strategies, and management practices that can be adopted to elevate the standard of care across the entire healthcare system. Because the current evidence is observational, the authors recommended further research to evaluate the precise ways in which clinical care delivery differs by physician sex. They also emphasized the need to determine whether these observed associations can be generalized and extended to medical institutions in other geographic regions, practice environments, and countries.
