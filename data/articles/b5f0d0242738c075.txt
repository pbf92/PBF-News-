TITLE: Orthodontic Artificial Intelligence Demonstrates High Imaging Accuracy but Lacks Prospective Clinical Evidence
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive narrative review evaluating the clinical translation of artificial intelligence in orthodontics indicates that computational systems have achieved advanced technical precision in automated image analysis but remain unproven in prospective patient care. Synthesizing evidence across diagnostic imaging, treatment planning, remote monitoring, patient education, and automated robotics, the assessment revealed that no orthodontic artificial intelligence application has satisfied predefined criteria for higher clinical maturity. Although machine learning and deep learning architectures have demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy when identifying anatomical landmarks on radiographs and segmenting anatomical structures on three-dimensional volumes, the vast majority of published studies remain retrospective, technical investigations conducted on benchmark datasets. Investigators determined that current literature has prioritized computational performance over clinical utility, leaving major gaps regarding whether algorithm-assisted recommendations alter clinical decision-making, enhance treatment efficiency, reduce procedural errors, or improve long-term therapeutic outcomes for orthodontic patients.
To investigate the clinical maturity and translational readiness of digital technologies across orthodontic workflows, the review team conducted a structured literature search across PubMed, ScienceDirect, and the Cochrane Library for peer-reviewed studies published between January 1, 2000, and August 31, 2026. The initial electronic query retrieved 2,022 records, comprising 1,699 records from PubMed, 204 from ScienceDirect, and 119 from the Cochrane Library. Following the removal of 169 duplicate entries in Zotero, two independent reviewers screened 1,853 unique records against strict eligibility criteria, resolving discrepancies through structured discussion and third-reviewer consultation. Ultimately, 45 database publications and three additional records identified through reference screening—comprising one book chapter and two peer-reviewed conference proceedings—formed the core evidence synthesis of 48 studies, contextualized by 17 authoritative reporting and regulatory frameworks.
To appraise translational progress systematically, the authors established a structured clinical-maturity evaluation hierarchy incorporating four progressive evidence levels. The first level evaluated task-specific technical performance against designated reference standards in development and internal validation datasets. The second level assessed external validation using independent clinical cohorts, distinct acquisition hardware, or separate clinical institutions. The third level examined prospective clinical effectiveness, requiring direct investigation of algorithm performance during real-world patient care to determine effects on decision-making, procedural safety, or clinical workflow. The fourth level assessed demonstrated clinical or patient benefit through comparative evidence showing measurable improvements in patient-centered, clinician, or health-system outcomes. Based on these thresholds, technologies were categorized as possessing higher clinical maturity, being emerging, or remaining experimental. Commercial availability was documented strictly as an implementation characteristic rather than proof of therapeutic efficacy, and no reviewed application met the threshold for higher clinical maturity.
Diagnostic imaging represents the most extensively researched and technically mature domain within orthodontic artificial intelligence, led by automated cephalometric landmark detection. Convolutional neural networks applied to two-dimensional lateral cephalograms have been evaluated across dozens of retrospective cohorts. In a featured systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 studies encompassing algorithms developed between 2017 and 2020, investigators established that 79.9 percent of anatomical landmarks were detected within the accepted two-millimeter clinical acceptability boundary, with a pooled mean prediction error difference of minus 0.581 millimeters relative to reference tracings. However, approximately one in five automated landmark detections fell outside acceptable clinical tolerance. Furthermore, critical appraisal revealed that most underlying studies exhibited high risk of bias, substantial applicability concerns regarding data selection, and an overreliance on public benchmark datasets rather than diverse clinical cohorts, underscoring that benchmark precision under retrospective conditions cannot guarantee diagnostic equivalence in clinical practice.
Three-dimensional image analysis has demonstrated similarly sophisticated computational capability, particularly for automated tooth segmentation on cone-beam computed tomography and digital dental casts. An external validation study examining 190 cone-beam computed tomography scans captured across five distinct imaging systems evaluated 879 tooth segmentation maps generated by cloud-based commercial software. The automated tool produced acceptable segmentations without manual refinement in 90.1 percent of instances, recording Dice similarity coefficients between 0.96 and 0.97, Intersection over Union values from 0.92 to 0.95, and precision exceeding 0.97. However, the presence of fixed orthodontic brackets independently increased the likelihood of requiring manual refinement nearly fourfold, while endodontic obturation and adjacent high-density metallic artifacts elevated refinement odds by 4.43-fold and 7.88-fold, respectively.
Machine learning regression models trained on serial lateral cephalograms achieved reported linear prediction accuracies of 97.87 percent and angular accuracies of 94.45 percent for craniofacial growth at age 13, yet the underlying training cohort was restricted to 59 children from a single population. In contrast, an independent comparative trial assessing an artificial intelligence web platform against manual three-dimensional measurements across 95 craniofacial computed tomography examinations revealed automated tracing failures in 16.8 percent of patient scans. Agreement between the platform and manual assessment for facial asymmetry was exceptionally poor, yielding intraclass correlation coefficients below 0.30 and failing entirely on the asymmetry rate index, despite manual measurement repeatability demonstrating excellent consistency with intraclass correlation coefficients exceeding 0.947.
Artificial intelligence systems formulated for orthodontic treatment planning and extraction decision-making remain classified as emerging technologies characterized by substantial methodological heterogeneity. A systematic review of 19 planning systems noted expert concordance rates surpassing 80 to 90 percent; however, these trials relied on subjective clinician opinion as the gold standard rather than verified long-term clinical correctness. Methodological quality assessments classified most studies as carrying moderate to serious risk of bias due to single-center designs and lack of prospective follow-up, confirming that current decision models serve as adjunctive aids rather than autonomous treatment planners.
Remote monitoring systems and smart sensor appliances represent another expanding domain that lacks robust prospective verification. Automated monitoring tools designed to evaluate clear aligner seating, oral hygiene, and appliance integrity between clinical appointments rely predominantly on retrospective photo evaluations and feasibility demonstrations. Although direct force-sensing platforms featuring flexible three-dimensional sensors have been successfully validated in laboratory environments to record force direction and magnitude, their practical clinical integration remains in early prototype development. Wireless Internet of Things devices proposed to deliver real-time intraoral data have yet to demonstrate tangible clinical improvements in treatment speed, patient compliance, or long-term therapeutic stability during daily clinical orthodontic practice. Consequently, published monitoring frameworks represent conceptual engineering pathways whose promised efficiency gains and clinical benefits remain unconfirmed by controlled prospective clinical trials.
The integration of conversational artificial intelligence and large language models for patient communication and education has yielded highly variable results across recent dental literature. In a preliminary randomized controlled trial evaluating 100 orthodontic patients allocated to either conventional educational leaflets or an artificial intelligence chatbot over five weeks, both groups exhibited significant periodontal deterioration; however, chatbot users experienced a significantly smaller increase in modified gingival index scores compared to controls. Comparative assessments of commercial models such as ChatGPT-4.0, Microsoft Copilot, and Gemini revealed contradictory findings across general orthodontic and clear aligner inquiries, largely reflecting discrepancies in evaluation timing, prompt wording, retrieval architectures, and interface configurations. Crucially, the reviewed chatbot investigations lacked adherence to standardized reporting guidelines like CHART, and authors emphasized that benchmark performance on simulated patient inquiries does not demonstrate clinical safety during unsupervised patient triage.
Experimental applications involving robotic wire-bending systems, autonomous bracket placement devices, and integrated augmented reality environments face substantial translational hurdles before routine implementation can occur. Automated wire-bending devices frequently reflect conventional computer numerical automation rather than genuine artificial intelligence learning models, and evidence showing improvements in chairside efficiency or tooth-movement precision remains sparse. Furthermore, the review highlighted pervasive ethical, legal, and operational challenges, including automation bias among practitioners, data privacy risks associated with facial photographic records, algorithmic bias originating from homogeneous training sets, and the lack of dedicated orthodontic regulatory frameworks. The authors concluded that future progress requires moving beyond computational accuracy toward prospective, multicenter clinical trials adhering to established frameworks such as TRIPOD-AI, STARD-AI, and DECIDE-AI, ensuring algorithms function as supervised decision-support tools that demonstrably benefit patient care.
