TITLE: The Evangelicals Bringing Hope to Climate Advocacy
OUTLET: Christianity Today

As a student at Wheaton College studying environmental science with a minor in theater, Bradley Lauber staged a production of The Tempest set in the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, a floating island of plastic between California and Hawaii.
The stage was strewn with fishing nets, plastic jugs, cans, and milk crates to represent the garbage swirling in the Pacific. Lauber envisioned Shakespeare’s Ariel as the spirit of the coral reef, one of the habitats most critically endangered by oceanic pollution. He reimagined Caliban, a character native to the remote island in the original play, as a representative of Indigenous tribes impacted by climate change. And the lords shipwrecked on Shakespeare’s island were depicted as representing the fishing industry and nations that contribute the most to pollution.
With the help of Second Glance Arts, an artistic ensemble made up of Wheaton College graduates, Lauber directed a weekend of performances in March at All Souls Anglican Church. The audience consisted mostly of Wheaton students and the cast’s family or friends. But the show also attracted some environmentally minded community members receptive to the message about stewardship.
“It was my hope … to put on theater productions wrestling with being good stewards of the earth,” Lauber said.
The production was the centerpiece of Lauber’s year as a college fellow with Young Evangelicals for Climate Action (YECA). The nonprofit developed the program in 2014 to help undergraduate students gain leadership skills and engage their classmates on the topic of climate change.
Overall, YECA helps young people see that they can advocate for the environment with faith, hope, and love. The organization has three leadership programs, all aimed at Christians under age 30. The college fellows program comes with a small stipend and requires participants to commit five to seven hours each week to climate-related work. Fellows receive training and can implement that training however they best see fit.
“We have a very clear why in our mission, which gives people a lot of room to have many different hows” said Lauren Kim, YECA’s national organizer and spokesperson. “The umbrella messaging creates room for diversity because the organization has a shared identity and theological vision rather than set policy.”
The American Psychological Association reported in 2020 that roughly two-thirds of US adults experienced some concerns about climate change and its effects.
According to the same poll, nearly half of young adults (ages 18–34) claimed their stress about climate change impacted their daily lives. If climate issues worsen, most young people say it will play a factor in how they travel, what they purchase, and where they live. Some even say it might impact whether or not they have kids.
In an advocacy field that Kim noted feels “urgent, dark, and filled with panic,” YECA trains its fellows to develop projects with small, measurable goals and to ground their work in the scriptural message of caring for God’s creation.
“What sets YECA apart from other organizations is we are faith-based and our hope is in Jesus,” she said. That hope can protect against burnout and, if embraced, relieve advocates of the pressure to essentially save the earth.
Kim said some other climate activists have been attracted to the hopeful attitude even if they don’t share the Christian convictions that anchor that message. Colleagues at non-Christian environmental organizations, like the Earth Island Institute, have highlighted the organization’s work. YECA has also received attention from secular media outlets like The Walrus and The New York Times.
Hope-filled creation care was a major theme of Rinah Fiol’s year as a fellow.
In a year, Fiol spearheaded a program to save about 5,000 pounds of leftover food from the Calvin University dining hall and donate it to local community centers, homeless shelters, churches, and youth programs.
Separately, she developed creation care lessons for preschool and elementary students centered on the theme “small things tell big stories.” The process helped her embrace the challenge of tailoring a message to different audiences.
“It’s easy to get caught up in what I’m passionate about, but when presenting a prickly topic like creation care, it’s not about how right I am,” Fiol said. It’s about “translating my passion into language my audience can understand.”
Fiol’s faith infuses her studies in plant and animal ecology with wonder for God’s creation. Her understanding of Christ’s return and a renewed heaven and earth gives her further hope and fuels her work in the here and now.
“Creation will be restored, as we will be in a state of no sin and eternal fellowship with God,” she said. “That’s what allows me to do this work without this burden on my shoulders and why I see this discipline as such a wonderful channel for sharing the hope of Christ.”
After speaking at more than 100 Christian colleges, universities, and seminaries—including Biola University, Wheaton College, and Covenant Theological Seminary—Calvin DeWitt is encouraged by how many young Christians see creation care as a component of faithful Christian living.
DeWitt, an evangelical climate scientist who teaches at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, has been researching and advocating on this issue for more than 50 years. The faith-based Au Sable Institute of Environmental Studies, which he once led, calls him the “modern day father of Christian environmentalism.”
DeWitt said he has noticed evangelical students are excited about environmental stewardship and “looking to make a difference in their lives.” Naturally, he encourages them to get involved at Au Sable Institute, which provides students with environmental science field classes at its locations in northern lower Michigan, Washington state, and Costa Rica.
DeWitt said the animating principle behind his work is love for creation, the Creator, and his fellow image bearers. He will talk to anyone about this work, but those conversations aren’t always friendly.
Skepticism toward climate change and environmentalism creeped up in the early 1990s among evangelicals closely aligned with the Republican party.
By 2013, Scott McConnell, the executive director of Lifeway Research, noted that the opinions of pastors on the topic of “global warming” reflected their own political beliefs. Lifeway’s survey at the time showed 43 percent of pastors believed that global warming was real and man-made. By 2019, that number rose to 53 percent.
DeWitt said his speeches over the years were occasionally met with jeers from climate change skeptics. When he’s publicly opposed by Christians, he said he sometimes talks about Revelation 11:18, where the apostle John prophesies a time “for destroying those who destroy the earth.”
For DeWitt, this verse demonstrates that God will hold people accountable for how they treat his creation and that Christians should not ignore the warning.
“You don’t have to white it out with a pen; you can white it out with your mind,” he said. “And you shouldn’t do that with anything in Scripture.”
Joshua Groeling has encountered students who, while not taking Wite-Out to Scripture, certainly did not take time to see how Christians should care for the environment.
During his year with YECA, he designed a research study to find out why some fellow students at Taylor University seemed hesitant to discuss climate change. He discovered that one of the primary reasons people didn’t talk or seem to care much about it was their interpretation of Scripture.
“Their short answer responses often cited the Rapture and how God won’t let large-scale disaster happen,” Groeling said.
In response, Groeling tried to engage students with action. He sought to bring more visibility for Taylor’s environmental club, called Stewards of Creation. He created trash cleanup, invasive species removal, and Earth Week events. He also revived the Randall Fall Festival, the school’s annual environmental festival.
By quantifiable metrics, the events were successful. Over 200 people attended the festival, and many also participated in other events. But the magnitude of the problem still causes Groeling to feel overwhelmed. He said he understands why some of his fellow students give in to apathy—it is easier.
“I have to pray a lot about it,” Groeling said. “It’s centering to pray and receive the reassurance that you know who is in control.”
Studying environmental science in school and training with YECA have also helped.
Both have given him the tools to resist the feeling that climate change is too overwhelming for one person to make a difference. He also leans on a few other virtues to help keep things in perspective: gratitude for the beauty of God’s creation as well as curiosity for the created order and for other people—including climate change skeptics.
“I’ve prayed that God would help me understand them, hear their story, and have a positive impact on them so the Lord can work through me more subtly, in a humble way, rather than trying to turn them” 180 degrees on this issue, he said.
Groeling’s third virtue, which ties together all others, is love.
“You must have love in your actions as you’re trying to create change.”
