TITLE: Character.ai moves to keep the law firm it owes $146,382 on three lawsuits
OUTLET: PPC Land

The bankrupt company behind the Character.ai chatbot wants to keep paying the Los Angeles law firm that defends it in three lawsuits over its product, even though that firm is one of its 20 largest unsecured creditors. In a sworn declaration filed on November 24, 2026, in the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, Munger, Tolles & Olson LLP states that it is owed $146,381.99 for work done before the bankruptcy and that it will go on defending Character Technologies, Inc. against the Commonwealth of Kentucky, Pennsylvania's State Board of Medicine and a girl identified only as A.D.
In Short
Character.ai's parent company filed a sworn statement from Munger, Tolles & Olson on November 24 so it can keep the firm on three lawsuits about its chatbots, brought by Kentucky, Pennsylvania's medical board and a minor known as A.D. The firm is owed $146,381.99 from before the bankruptcy, and it lists past or current work for 66 parties tied to the case, among them Google, OpenAI, Disney and the Federal Trade Commission. Anyone who objects has until December 15 at 4:00 p.m. ET, and any fees for the firm's new work come out of the money that creditors, including tech and marketing vendors, are waiting to share.
What the declaration says
The filing is Docket No. 246 in case 26-11603 (BLS), before Judge Brendan L. Shannon. It runs to 14 pages: a three-page declaration, a five-page list of parties connected to the case (Exhibit 1) and a six-page table of the firm's links to those parties (Exhibit 2). It is signed by Seth Goldman, a partner in the firm's office at 350 South Grand Avenue in Los Angeles, and dated November 24, 2026.
Its purpose is set out in the first paragraph: "The Debtor wishes to retain the Firm to provide non-bankruptcy related litigation defense of the Debtor during this chapter 11 case, and the Firm has consented to provide such services."
Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11 on October 12, 2026, and has since run its business as a debtor-in-possession, meaning it stays in control of its affairs under the court's supervision instead of handing them to a trustee. The declaration confirms the firm "has provided services to the Debtor prior to October 12, 2026 (the 'Petition Date')."
Munger, Tolles & Olson is not being hired as bankruptcy counsel. That role belongs to Wilson Sonsini Goodrich & Rosati. Instead, the firm is being kept on as an ordinary course professional: an outside adviser the company already used in its normal business before the bankruptcy, such as a trial lawyer or an accountant, and which it may keep using under a lighter procedure set by the court. The declaration states that it "is submitted in compliance with" the order that created that procedure, Docket No. 162, and adds: "I have reviewed the Ordinary Course Professionals Order, and I understand the limitations on compensation and reimbursement thereunder."
The rest of the declaration covers the questions a court asks of any adviser paid from a bankrupt company's funds. The firm says it has not agreed to share its fees with anyone outside the firm. It says it has received no promises about pay "other than in accordance with the provisions of the Bankruptcy Code." It did not hold a retainer when the case began, and it had no indemnification agreement with the company. Paragraph 13 says the firm will file a supplement if it finds new facts bearing on these points.
The caption sets the objection deadline as "December 15, at 4:00 p.m. (ET)" without a year. By PPC Land's count, December 15 falls 21 days after the filing.
The three lawsuits
Paragraph 4 lists the work. The firm will "Defend the Debtor in civil litigation brought by" three plaintiffs, "including but not limited to engaging in motions practice, responding to discovery requests, producing documents, negotiating with opposing parties, and handling other litigation tasks." Discovery is the stage where each side can demand documents and data from the other before trial, and much of it now runs as eDiscovery over email, chat and other electronic records.
Kentucky's case was the first of the three. The state sued Character and its co-founders in Franklin Circuit Court on January 8, 2026, according to the chief executive's first-day declaration in the bankruptcy. Four months later, on May 1, Pennsylvania's State Board of Medicine sued the company over a chatbot that posed as a licensed psychiatrist, with the Department of State bringing the petition in Commonwealth Court.
The third case has had less attention. According to the federal court docket, A.D. v. Character Technologies, Inc. was filed on August 13, 2026, as a personal injury product liability case and is assigned to Judge Charles R. Breyer. The complaint names five defendants: Character Technologies, its co-founders Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas Adiwardana, Google LLC and Alphabet Inc. It says the girl and her mother, who acts for her in the case, live in Jefferson County, Kentucky. According to the complaint, the app reached her when she was 12, without her parents' knowledge and with no age check, and drew her into simulated sexual encounters and toward self-harm. Those are allegations, and none has been tested in court. The complaint also states that Google and Alphabet paid $2.7 billion to Character Technologies, a reference to the August 2024 licensing deal.
A.D. is not a stranger to the bankruptcy. The Delaware docket shows lawyers appearing in the case for A.D., described as a minor acting through her guardian ad litem, K.H., on October 29 and again on November 19. The November 19 appearance lists Steve Lopez and Anna J. Katz, two of the Gibbs Mura LLP lawyers who signed the California complaint. Gibbs Mura also appears in Exhibit 1 under "Counsel to Litigation Counterparties."
A creditor defending the company
The petition Character Technologies filed on October 12 lists the 20 creditors with the largest unsecured claims, meaning debts with no collateral behind them. Munger, Tolles & Olson is No. 8, with $136,807.61 for professional services, according to the petition. The declaration now puts the figure at $146,381.99. By PPC Land's calculation, that is $9,574.38, or about 7%, more than the petition showed. The declaration does not explain the difference.
Paragraph 9 reads: "The Debtor owes the Firm $146,381.99 for fees and expenses incurred before and unpaid as of Petition Date, the payment of which is subject to the limitations contained in the Bankruptcy Code." In plain terms, that old bill waits in line with other unsecured debts. New work after October 12 is paid separately, under the ordinary course order.
The same petition list ends with three litigation entries of unknown value, each marked contingent, unliquidated and disputed: the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania at No. 18, the Commonwealth of Kentucky at No. 19 and a minor plaintiff, name redacted, at No. 20. The petition does not name that minor. So the firm sits on the same list as at least two of the parties it is defending against.
Law firms hold five of the 20 places on the petition list.
Exhibit 2 shows Munger, Tolles & Olson has in the past represented three of those four other firms, White & Case, Skadden and Quinn Emanuel, in unrelated matters.
Who else the firm works for
Exhibit 2 is the firm's conflict check. Counsel to the company handed Munger, Tolles & Olson the list of parties in Exhibit 1, and the firm marked which of them "are either current or former clients of the Firm." The table has 53 entries covering 66 named parties, by PPC Land's count. Each carries one of three status lines:
- "MTO currently represents this entity in matters wholly unrelated to the debtors' chapter 11 cases" (3 entries: Fireworks.Ai, Rippling and Zayo Group LLC)
- "MTO currently represents and/or has in the past represented this entity and/or certain affiliates or subsidiaries in matters wholly unrelated to the debtors' chapter 11 cases" (27 entries)
- "MTO has in the past represented this entity and/or certain affiliates or subsidiaries in matters wholly unrelated to the debtors' chapter 11 cases" (23 entries)
The wording refers to "debtors" and "cases" in the plural, although there is one debtor and one case.
Several names stand out because of their role in this bankruptcy.
The 15 attorney general offices in Exhibit 2 are those of Arizona, California, Delaware, Florida, Kansas, Michigan, Mississippi, New York, North Carolina, Ohio, Oregon, South Carolina, Utah, Virginia and Washington. Exhibit 1 lists 50 such offices in all, by PPC Land's count, including those of Kentucky and Pennsylvania. Neither Kentucky, Pennsylvania nor the Pennsylvania Department of State appears in Exhibit 2, so the firm reports no client ties to the two governments it is defending against.
The rest of Exhibit 2 is the ordinary furniture of a large firm's client list: insurers (AIG, AXA XL, Beazley, Berkley, Berkshire, RSUI, Vantage, Lloyd's London through CRC Wholesale Group), the broker Arthur J. Gallagher & Co., banks (Silicon Valley Bank, JPMorgan Chase Bank, Morgan Stanley), utilities (AT&T, Comcast, Pacific Gas & Electric), tax agencies in California, New York, Florida, Michigan and Virginia, and LinkedIn, DoorDash, Apple Distribution International, Anthem Blue Cross, the University of Washington and 33 Irving Tenant LLC, a WeWork entity listed among the company's contract counterparties.
On adverse interests, paragraph 8 states that no one at the firm, "insofar as I have been able to ascertain, holds or represents any interest adverse to the Debtor or its estate with respect to the matter(s) upon which this Firm is to be employed."
How the ordinary course route works
According to the filings, the procedure has run in these steps:
- The motion. On November 3, 2026, the company asked the court for authority to retain and pay professionals it uses in the ordinary course of business (Docket No. 104), with objections due November 10 and a hearing set for November 17, according to the notice of motion.
- The order. The court entered the order on November 13, 2026 (Docket No. 162), according to the docket. The declaration refers to it as "Ref. Docket No. 162."
- The declaration. Each professional files a sworn statement. In this one, the firm covers its services, what it is owed, any retainer, any indemnification, fee sharing, and its links to every party on the list supplied by the company's lawyers.
- The objection window. The declaration sets December 15, 2026, at 4:00 p.m. ET as the deadline for objections.
- The limits. Pay is subject to the "limitations on compensation and reimbursement" in the order. The declaration does not restate them.
- Updates. The firm commits to supplement the declaration if it "should discover any facts bearing on the matters described herein."
Exhibit 1 itself shows how the list has been reused. Its cover page carries two docket stamps: Docket No. 246-1, filed November 24, and Docket No. 189-1, filed November 18. Docket No. 189 is the declaration supporting the employment of Alex Feerst as an ordinary course professional. Under the heading "Ordinary Course Professionals," the list names six: Minami Tamaki LLP, Alex Jonathan Feerst, KPMG, Frank Rimerman, FBT Gibbons LLP and Jaymie Scotto & Associates. Munger, Tolles & Olson is not under that heading. It appears only among the holders of the 20 largest unsecured claims.
Two more filings on November 24 concern names from that heading. Docket No. 243 is a similar declaration for FBT Gibbons LLP, a Louisville firm working on the Kentucky case. Docket No. 244 is a separate application to retain KPMG LLP for tax work.
