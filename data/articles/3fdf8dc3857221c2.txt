TITLE: Anthropic Researcher Claims AI Has Solved Math’s White Whale
OUTLET: Gizmodo

Mathematicians have been reeling since OpenAI announced in September that it had solved the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, a feat that had eluded the field for decades. For some, it was the first tantalizing glimpse of a new era, one where humans and AI would collaborate to solve math’s thorniest mysteries; for others, it was an unsettling harbinger of a jobs apocalypse.
Now another, perhaps much more momentous event is poised to revolutionize the field.
Levent Alpöge, a Turkish-American mathematician currently employed by Anthropic, claimed Friday morning to have solved the infamous Riemann hypothesis. First formulated in 1859 by the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann, the hypothesis concerns the distribution of prime numbers and is widely considered to be the field’s most formidable unresolved puzzle—“the great white whale of mathematical research,” as the author John Derbyshire wrote in his book Prime Obsession, which outlines the origins of the hypothesis and its impact on modern mathematics. Like Navier-Stokes, the Riemann hypothesis is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s seven “Millennium Prize Problems,” each carrying a $1 million prize for a solution that satisfies the institute’s requirements.
Alpöge says he used Claude to formulate his proof, which he published in the programming language Lean and has yet to be confirmed by the Clay Mathematics Institute, Anthropic, or any other credible third party. In a statement published on his website, he writes about the experience of working with the chatbot to chase math’s “white whale” like one who has just glimpsed some kind of divine revelation. “It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light, but what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater,” he wrote. “Claude and I have had a great time together with such breadth in recent months, but this time Claude far outpaced me near the finish line. No, Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.”
If his proof is confirmed, it would be a momentous achievement not only for mathematics but also for the AI industry, which has thus far justified its monumental spending with claims that the technology will soon advance scientific discovery across just about every domain. “We now have models that are clearly, unequivocally capable of expanding the frontier of knowledge in a way that we—humanity’s smartest minds—have not been able to do on our own,” OpenAI CEO Sam Altman told Fortune editor-in-chief Alyson Shontell in an interview shortly after the company announced its Navier-Stokes solution. “And this is an amazing time. That same technology will work for discovering lots of other science. I think we are going to have an incredible golden age of scientific discovery.”
OpenAI’s claim to have solved the Navier-Stokes problem—which it said it achieved with the help of around 10,000 AI agents—was largely overshadowed by an ensuing controversy after Alpöge and another mathematician, the NYU professor Tristan Buckmaster, claimed their work on a related problem had been used by OpenAI without credit, in part due to Alpöge’s affiliation with Anthropic.
An AI-assisted solution to the Riemann hypothesis would also be a win for Anthropic as it continues to try and win over investors following its record-breaking IPO on Thursday. In his statement, Alpöge wrote that he arrived at the solution Sunday night, at which point he texted his boss, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, to discuss a potential announcement. (He didn’t mention how Amodei responded.)
Alpöge admitted that the timing was “horrible,” referring to Anthropic’s upcoming IPO. Companies frequently make grand, unsubstantiated proclamations right before a stock market debut to drive up share prices and investor interest—recall Elon Musk’s bluster about making humanity a multiplanetary species in the period leading up to the SpaceX IPO—but Alpöge insists that wasn’t the case here. “There is an obvious cynical view, to do with the timing, but it is false,” he wrote.
