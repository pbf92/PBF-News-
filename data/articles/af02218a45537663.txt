TITLE: The value and impact of correcting anaemia before cardiac surgery
OUTLET: Open Access Government

Professor Paul Myles explores how treating anaemia before cardiac surgery could support recovery, drawing on the ITACS trial’s findings on intravenous iron, blood transfusions and healthcare costs
At least 313 million surgical procedures are performed each year worldwide, and demand for surgery continues to grow. (1) More people die after surgery worldwide than from HIV, TB, and malaria combined, making perioperative mortality the world’s third-leading cause of death. (2)
Detecting and optimising underlying medical conditions and supporting patients awaiting surgery to stop smoking, eat a healthy diet, and undertake regular exercise are central tenets of perioperative care. Poor physical fitness, anaemia, malnutrition, sarcopenia, obesity, diabetes, and other modifiable conditions are potent risk factors for surgery. Put simply, preoperative optimisation aims to improve functional reserve before surgery. This article focuses specifically on managing anaemia before surgery.
In a study performed in 79 hospitals across 20 countries, 31.7% of patients undergoing major surgery had anaemia preoperatively, and this was associated with an increased adjusted risk of admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), postoperative complications, and a fivefold increase in the risk of death. (3)
Why anaemia matters in major surgery
Anaemia (low haemoglobin in the blood) is common in people undergoing major surgery, but its impact on risk and recovery after surgery is often underappreciated. Numerous studies have found that there is a strong correlation between the extent of preoperative anaemia and the risk of major complications and unplanned readmission to hospital after surgery. Reducing anaemia is likely to improve overall health before and after surgery. (3-5) Anaemia also increases the need for blood transfusions, which themselves are also associated with an increased risk of complications.
Advances in anaesthesia and perioperative medicine have enabled older, sicker patients to safely undergo surgery. A key part of this success story is optimising chronic health conditions in the weeks or months before elective surgery. Anaemia is a common, identifiable and modifiable risk factor in this regard.
Patient recovery from major surgery usually takes weeks or months. During this time, many patients experience discomfort, tiredness, poor sleep, and reduced physical and mental wellbeing. Older patients are particularly vulnerable to a prolonged and tenuous recovery – what Krumholz described as ‘post-hospitalisation syndrome’. (6) Some patients need frequent visits to their local doctor after being discharged from hospital, or are readmitted, because of infection, excessive fatigue, shortness of breath, confusion, or falls.
Blood loss during major surgery can reduce the body’s iron stores and cause or worsen anaemia. Therefore, reducing anaemia and increasing haemoglobin levels before surgery could help patients recover better, both physically and mentally. Complications after surgery will often lengthen the hospital stay and also increase healthcare costs, and are strongly linked with being readmitted to hospital and with losing independence.
Why intravenous iron?
Treating anaemia is one of the most straightforward ways doctors can potentially improve recovery after surgery. Intravenous iron is already used successfully to treat anaemia in some conditions, including heart failure, kidney disease and pregnancy. However, until recently, there has been insufficient evidence of its benefits in most types of major surgery, including cardiac surgery. Modern intravenous iron preparations are considered safe and can deliver a full treatment dose in about 15 minutes. (7) This gives patients a dose equivalent 12 months of tablets and means that iron-deficiency anaemia can be treated in the clinic.
What have recent studies shown?
Several small, single-centre trials have demonstrated that giving a single dose of intravenous iron before elective cardiac surgery reduces red cell transfusions. (8,9) My group conducted the ITACS study (Intravenous Iron to Treat Anaemia Before Cardiac Surgery) across 33 hospitals in 10 countries, recently published in the BMJ. (10) We found that giving patients a single iron infusion in the weeks before their cardiac surgery led to a correction of anaemia in a majority of cases, reduced the need for blood transfusions during and after their surgery, and was associated with an increase in the number of days patients were alive and at home in the first three months after surgery. It also reduced disability scores in the first month after surgery. It may have improved the chance of surviving without significant disability for up to 90 days after surgery (although this finding was not statistically conclusive). The iron infusion was cost ‘dominant’ in this setting, meaning it improved patient outcomes and reduced total healthcare costs.
These results further support current best-practice guidelines that, until recently, offered a provisional recommendation to use intravenous iron in patients with anaemia before cardiac surgery, particularly when they are iron deficient. The patient benefits and demonstrable cost-effectiveness of this approach demand action. Pre-admission care pathways need to be modified to ensure that all patients being considered for cardiac surgery have their haemoglobin checked and, if they are found to be anaemic, undergo further testing (iron studies) to determine whether this is due to iron deficiency. If iron-deficiency anaemia is identified, arrange an iron infusion well before the planned date of surgery. These essential steps can be instigated by the referring cardiologist or local family practitioner. This process should otherwise be integrated into a physician- or nurse-led preoperative anaemia clinic.
Why might the benefits take time to appear?
Interestingly, the benefits of intravenous iron do not seem to happen immediately after surgery. For example, the PREVENTT study looked at patients undergoing colorectal surgery. (11) Patients received intravenous iron or placebo between 10 and 42 days before surgery, but this did not reduce the need for red cell transfusions, complications, or length of hospital stay. Similar patterns have been seen in other studies, including ITACS, in which the full benefit of pre-surgery iron supplementation became apparent mostly within the first three months after surgery, consistent with the usual recovery period after major surgery. The full benefits of intravenous iron may take time to develop.
Future research
At this point, we do not know whether intravenous iron provides comparable benefits in other types of major surgery. Future research is needed to investigate whether the benefits of an iron infusion seen in elective cardiac surgery also extend to people undergoing inpatient (urgent) cardiac surgery, and other types of major surgery. There is also the possibility of treating anaemia in the postoperative period before discharge.
The task now is to implement what we know to be cost-effective. Detecting and optimising underlying medical conditions remains a crucial component of high-quality perioperative care. Preoperative care pathways need to be adapted to ensure anaemia is detected and treated before cardiac surgery.
