TITLE: CNIL fines unnamed life insurer €5m over 1.8m passwords stored in plain text
OUTLET: PPC Land

France's data protection authority today published a €5 million fine against a life insurer that kept the passwords of more than 1.8 million customers in clear text, readable by anyone with access to its databases, and that let an eight-person team share a single login to a database holding the records of 1.3 million policyholders. The decision does not name the company.
The restricted committee of the Commission nationale de l'informatique et des libertés (CNIL), the body that rules on sanctions, adopted decision SAN-2026-016 on November 12, 2026. It appeared on Légifrance today with the insurer called "company X" throughout and every detail that could identify it replaced by an ellipsis in square brackets. The committee found two breaches of Article 32 of the GDPR, which requires security appropriate to the risk, and two breaches of Article 5(1)(e), the rule that limits how long personal data can be kept. The rapporteur had first proposed €9 million, then €7 million. The committee settled on €5 million, issued no compliance order because the company had already fixed the problems, and agreed to publish without the company's name.
The amount is modest next to the CNIL's recent cookie cases. The reasoning behind it is less so. The committee calculated the fine on the turnover of the wider group to which the insurer is affiliated, over the company's objection that no capital link connected it to that group, and it measured the group's turnover as gross written premiums rather than the IFRS 17 "insurance revenue" figure the company argued for. For any insurer that belongs to a larger group, the decision is a written account of how the French regulator sizes its exposure.
In Short
France's privacy regulator fined a life insurance company €5 million because it kept more than 1.8 million customer passwords in readable form, let eight employees share logins to customer data, and held contact-form messages and login records for many years longer than it needed. The case matters to any business that runs customer accounts, because the regulator described password storage and individual logins as basic security duties and sized the fine on the revenue of the whole group, not just the company. The insurer had already fixed the problems, so it faces no order to change anything, and the decision was published without its name.
The company behind the "X"
What the decision reveals about the insurer is limited. It specialises in life insurance and sells savings products in the wealth sector, retirement savings plans and products used in estate planning, through its agencies or by phone. In 2024, 640 employees worked for an entity whose name is redacted, 522 of them on the insurer's life insurance business. The insurer is wholly owned by another company, which is affiliated to a group whose statutes, according to the decision, "organize common governance and financial solidarity" among its member companies.
Turnover, net result, the number of reception points in France and the group's revenue are all redacted. So is the name of the database, the access tool and the team at the centre of the case.
The quotations in this article are translated from the French text of the decision.
How the case reached the committee
The CNIL was alerted on December 27, 2023 about how the insurer kept passwords and answers to security questions, and about how long it retained data that users entered in the personal space of its website. The table below records the procedure as the decision sets it out.
The panel was chaired by Philippe-Pierre Cabourdin, with Vincent Lesclous as vice-president and Didier Kling and Éric Thiers as members. At the hearing the company was informed of its right to remain silent and had the floor last. Among the texts the decision relies on is a ruling of the Constitutional Council, decision No. 2025-1154 QPC of August 8, 2025, alongside the general principle guaranteeing the rights of the defence to anyone facing a punitive procedure, "which includes the right to be silent."
Nobody contested who was responsible. The insurer lists itself in its register of processing activities as the data controller for managing insurance contracts, from subscription to execution, and did not dispute that status in either set of written observations.
Passwords anyone with database access could read
Article 32 obliges a controller, "taking into account the state of knowledge, the costs of implementation and the nature, scope, context and purposes of the processing as well as the risks," to implement "appropriate technical and organizational measures in order to guarantee a level of security appropriate to the risk."
The committee began from first principles. "The safe storage of passwords is a basic precaution in the protection of personal data," the decision says, and it traced that expectation back more than a decade.
From those sources the committee drew a working definition: a mathematically proven, deliberately slow hash function, with a random salt of at least 128 bits for each password, so that attackers cannot use tables of passwords and their fingerprints computed in advance.
The insurer was nowhere near it. According to the rapporteur's findings, it kept the passwords of more than 1.8 million customers in clear in the table that stores customer login data, and 730,289 of those passwords were also kept in clear in a log table. Where it did transform passwords, it used ORA_HASH, a hash function built into Oracle's database software, which the decision calls obsolete, without a cryptographic salt.
The sequence of fixes is set out in unflattering detail. Only after the inspection, in an email of July 18, 2024, did the company say it had applied a first hash function - ORA_HASH. Only on April 25, 2026, after the sanction report had been served, did it move to a function the committee accepts as state of the art: Argon2 with a salt, according to the company's defence. By PPC Land's calculation, that left a little over 23 months between the inspection and the switch, a period during which, in the committee's words, the risk of compromise of personal data accessible with those passwords continued.
The company did not dispute any of this. It stressed how quickly it had reacted and pointed out that no security incident had compromised the data. Its stronger objection concerned what the passwords protected. The rapporteur had argued that they gave access to health and banking data. The company replied that a table collecting contact forms sent to account managers sat entirely outside the customer's personal space and could not be reached with a customer's login, and that bank details shown in the personal space were truncated to the last three digits of the IBAN.
The rapporteur accepted the point on health data and dropped it. On bank data, the committee found something more awkward: in an extract of the contact-form table supplied during the inspection, several customers had typed their full IBAN into forms sent from the "My letters" section of their personal space. It also accepted that this table could not be reached with the password protecting the personal space.
That did not save the company. Storing passwords in clear, the committee held, made all personal data in customer areas vulnerable, data "which may contain financial data, no matter whether this vulnerability has been exploited or not by an attacker." The breach was established for the period from the inspection until the sanction report was served.
Eight people, one password
The second Article 32 finding concerned two accounts that several people used under one set of credentials.
The first was a generic application account through which the company reached a database holding all information on its 1.3 million customers with a life insurance contract in force. In effect a service account, an identity meant for software, it was being used by people: its access was shared by the eight people in one of the company's teams. The second was the administrator account of a tool the company used to view every table in its database. On the day of the inspection, its password was shared by the same eight people.
The committee set out three reasons why shared accounts are, as the decision puts it, "in principle contrary to Article 32 of the GDPR." They undermine an authorisation policy designed to limit each category of user to the data it needs, the logic of least-privilege access. They destroy traceability, since only individual accounts record who did what, which makes a fraudulent access, a deletion or a corruption of data much harder to attribute. And a password shared among several people is no longer secret or individual.
It also repeated language from an earlier sanction, SAN-2021-008 of June 14, 2021: "the assignment of a unique identifier per user and the prohibition of shared accounts are among the essential precautions to ensure effective traceability of access to a database." Without that, the committee noted, there is no reliable audit trail to identify fraudulent access or the author of a deletion carried out through the shared account.
The company's defence was partly about timing and partly about representativeness. It said it had created named accounts in November 2025, each with a unique identifier and a strictly personal password, and had revised the application account's permissions so that only application processes could use it, with a password generated at deployment and never stored or shared. The committee accepted that the team had a genuine need for access to the database, for technical data analysis and business questions, but found that until June 2026 the company had not proved that the shared access had been deactivated or deleted.
On the administrator account, the insurer argued that the generic login used during the on-site inspection did not reflect how access to the tool was normally managed, since team members had always had individual accounts. It deactivated the generic account on April 22, 2026. The committee held that "only individual accounts, including a unique identifier per user, guarantee a level of security adapted to the risk," and found the breach established for the past.
The password rule that passed
One complaint did not survive. The rapporteur had also attacked the insurer's password policy: when customers reset a password from their personal space, the site accepted eight to fifteen characters, with at least one uppercase letter, one lowercase letter and one number.
Measured against the CNIL's 2022 recommendation, which the decision describes as not mandatory but a "relevant light" on what security requires, that policy only passes with an extra safeguard. The company showed that one existed on the day of the checks: customer accounts were blocked automatically after several failed login attempts. The rapporteur dropped the complaint, and the committee found it was not established in any case.
Thirteen years of messages, 21 years of logins
Article 5(1)(e) of the GDPR requires personal data to be "kept in a form that allows the identification of data subjects for a period not exceeding that necessary for the purposes for which they are processed." Once that purpose is achieved, the decision says, data must be deleted, anonymised, or moved to intermediate archiving for a fixed period where a legal obligation or potential litigation requires it.
The first retention breach concerned the contact form in the customer area. Since November 2010, the insurer had kept in one table the details customers entered, their messages and any attachments. The oldest message was dated November 27, 2010, more than 13 years before the inspection; the rapporteur counted 14 years. Bank details were among the data. The retention was all the more excessive, the rapporteur noted, because each form was also archived as a PDF in an electronic document management tool once an account manager took up the request.
