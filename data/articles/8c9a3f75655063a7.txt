TITLE: Caregiver Health Education May Reduce Pediatric Urinary Tract Infection Recurrence
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Educating caregivers about pediatric urinary tract infections may help lower infection recurrence while strengthening parental knowledge and preventive care practices, according to a systematic review published in Frontiers in Public Health. A research team led by Xuqiong Tan evaluated educational interventions aimed at caregivers of children with or without a prior infection history. Their narrative synthesis of available clinical evidence indicates that structured instructional programs delivered to mothers contributed to measurable improvements in prevention behaviors and notable reductions in repeat infections confirmed by laboratory culture.
To assess the effectiveness of these caregiver-focused instructional programs, the authors performed comprehensive literature searches across eight medical databases, including PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, SinoMed, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP, tracking records from inception through May 27, 2026. The search targeted studies specifically evaluating health education directed at caregivers to prevent pediatric urinary tract infections.
The screening process identified four eligible trials, all conducted in Iran, comprising one randomized controlled trial and three quasi-experimental designs. Across these four studies, the interventions enrolled a cumulative total of 520 mothers, divided evenly into 260 participants in intervention cohorts and 260 participants in control groups. Theoretical frameworks guided the instructional design of the evaluated programs: three of the included studies structured their educational curricula around the Health Belief Model, while the fourth intervention was structured using the Theory of Planned Behavior.
The educational interventions generated broad improvements across parental cognitive and behavioral measures. Three trials documented statistically significant increases in maternal knowledge regarding urinary tract infections, and three studies reported marked gains in parental self-efficacy and perceived disease susceptibility. In addition, two studies showed significant increases in perceived benefits of preventive action. All four included studies observed statistically significant enhancements in the daily preventive hygiene and care practices carried out by participating mothers.
Two trials evaluated clinical outcomes by tracking urinary tract infection recurrence confirmed through diagnostic urine cultures. In an analysis of data from Rakhshani and colleagues, caregiver education achieved a risk ratio of 0.08 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning 0.03 to 0.18, reflecting a risk difference of minus 0.80 with a 95 percent confidence interval between minus 0.90 and minus 0.70. Similarly, data reported by Mosavi and colleagues yielded a risk ratio of 0.07 with a 95 percent confidence interval ranging from 0.01 to 0.49, corresponding to a risk difference of minus 0.23 with a 95 percent confidence interval from minus 0.35 to minus 0.12.
Despite these favorable numerical results, the systematic review identified critical methodological limitations that temper overall confidence in the findings. The authors conducted a qualitative narrative synthesis rather than a quantitative meta-analysis due to significant clinical heterogeneity and the small number of published trials. Methodological reporting inconsistencies were also present; for example, the analytical approach in the trial by Rakhshani and colleagues remained ambiguous because five participant dropouts were noted yet the final analytical denominator remained unchanged at 75 individuals.
The review team emphasized that the existing evidence base remains constrained by short post-intervention follow-up windows, variable outcome measurement scales, and an exclusive geographical focus on maternal participants in Iran. Consequently, the investigators concluded that while caregiver health education represents a promising non-pharmacological strategy for pediatric care, extensive high-quality research across varied global settings is required before routine adoption into standardized clinical practice guidelines can be justified. They added that future educational initiatives must be tailored carefully to distinct cultural settings, caregiver needs, and available local healthcare resources.
