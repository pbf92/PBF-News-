Wednesday, November 18, 2026 was a quieter day for announcements and a revealing one for where advertising money is flowing. A new estimate put global spending on creators at $116bn this year, with the fastest growth coming from brands that hire creators to make their ads rather than simply post for them. Some creators are earning more by syndicating old YouTube videos to free streaming channels than from YouTube itself. Microsoft Advertising called its latest changes the biggest upgrade to its bidding models in a decade, a media agency described how AI agents help Zillow separate real renters from daydreamers, and Digiday tried to define the "unified media platforms" that promise to put budgets under one roof. Google pressed on with folding its travel campaigns into search and loosened its election ad rules for candidates who also run businesses. At the DealBook Summit, Sam Altman said he did not know whether Anthropic was building AI safely, and Elon Musk said he felt "pretty foolish" about his record midterm spending. France's privacy regulator fined a life insurer EUR5m for storing more than 1.8 million passwords in readable form, and Amazon warned that ads on its Fire tablets can keep counting impressions after a campaign ends. The day's thread was production: who makes the ads, who decides where they run, and who keeps the records straight.

Creators become the production line
The creator economy has a new headline number, and its most interesting part is not the posts. Digiday reported that brands booking creators to make their ads are pushing up a $116bn global influencer market. Research firm Madison & Wall estimates that marketers spent $116bn on creator activity in 2026 and forecasts growth of 12% a year through 2030, double the 6% it expects for advertising overall. The biggest slice, 44% or $52bn, goes on paid amplification, the practice of boosting a creator's post with media money. Sponsored posts and ambassador deals took 28%, and agencies, platforms and talent representatives took 8%.
The fastest-growing segment is smaller but more telling. Creator-made assets for connected TV, display and paid social, which include whitelisting, licensing, production and user-generated content fees, made up 12% of spending but are growing 16% a year. In other words, brands increasingly treat creators as a production house for their own ads. "The reason we see brands engaging with this is just because it's cheaper," said Luke Stillman, managing director of Madison & Wall. Agencies see the same trend in their clients' books: one estimated creator spending among its clients rose 20% to 30% in 2026, and another put the rise at 50%. Nicholas Spiro, chief creative officer of Viral Nation, said creators are becoming the production engine for paid media. "Creator-made assets simply connect best with potential customers on a human level," he said.
The categories are widening beyond fashion and beauty into healthcare, automotive and finance. Carmen Graham, chief marketing officer of the children's medicine brand Genexa, described using parent creators: "An ad can make a claim. A parent can prove it." By market, the US accounted for 43% of spending last year and is projected to grow 15% this year, followed by the UK at 5% and Germany at 4%. India is the fastest-growing market, at 20% a year. The speed of the format also shapes the economics: Unilever's chief executive, Fernando Fernandez, said at the company's Capital Markets Day that a social video now lasts about four days, which helps explain why brands need so much more of it.
Old videos are turning into new money. Digiday reported that some creators are turning YouTube back catalogs into sizable paychecks. An anonymous true-crime creator earned $154,000 across Spotify, Tubi and Facebook in September, more than 1.8 times what the channel made from the YouTube Partner Program that month, said Chanel Simmons, managing director of Motion Society. Her company, which has syndicated creator content since 2022, works with more than 650 creators and rights owners and distributes more than 30,000 videos a month to over 20 platforms and TV services. The work is closer to television packaging than uploading: videos are grouped into seasons, re-edited to strip sponsor reads, calls to action, watermarks and YouTube references, checked for brand safety, given platform-specific artwork and tested.
Free ad-supported streaming channels are hungry for that programming. Kudzi Chikumbu, vice president of creator partnerships at Tubi, said the service looks for distinctive voices in genres such as horror, young adult and reality, and offers creators a revenue share. Roku, Samsung TV Plus and Pluto TV are other outlets, with Spotify suited to podcast-style video and Facebook to long-form clips. "The focus is shifting from audience growth to maximizing the value of the content creators already own," said Jonathan Chanti, chief executive of Reign Maker Group, who also warned that creators risk giving up control of their work or future earnings. Keith Bendes, chief strategy officer of Linqia, raised a question brand marketers will soon face when a sponsored video ends up on television: "Who owns the extra reach?" He expects brand deals to move towards television-style pricing as a result. "The big bonus is that the destinations now pay," he said.
The year's biggest entertainment launch showed the opposite approach to brand partnerships. Adweek reported on six ways brands are capturing buzz around GTA 6's release. Rockstar Games' Grand Theft Auto 6 launches on November 19 after years of delays, with Sensor Tower data showing more than $1.5bn in preorders, almost 17 million copies. Economists estimate $1bn in productivity is at risk on launch day as workers take time off. The franchise is "notorious for rejecting in-game brand integrations," preferring parody businesses, so brands are working around it: Logitech gave staff an official day off and Popeyes created a "Spice City" sandwich. Rockstar itself is marketing in the physical world. Adweek reported on seven ways Rockstar is bringing GTA 6's Vice City to life, turning well-known Miami locations into landmarks from the game, with hidden details for fans. Its predecessor, GTA 5, came out 13 years ago and has sold nearly 235 million copies.
Machines that buy
Microsoft is rewriting the engine behind its automated campaigns. Search Engine Roundtable reported that Microsoft Advertising announced the biggest upgrade to its bidding models in a decade. "We're rolling out the biggest upgrade to bidding models in a decade," Microsoft said. The changes fall into three areas. On consistency, bids should hit cost-per-acquisition and return-on-ad-spend targets more reliably without losing volume. On speed, bids adjust nearly twice as fast after an advertiser changes a target. On delivery, budgets pace more evenly through the day and campaigns reach full budget sooner.
Microsoft offered three results from its testing. Campaigns using Maximize Conversion Value or Target ROAS saw on average 10% more conversion value per click at a similar return. Moving from Target CPA to Target ROAS delivered 8% more conversion value, and moving from enhanced CPC to Maximize Conversions with a cost target delivered 18% more conversions at a similar cost. Microsoft calls this a first stage and says it will "continue to roll out improvements automatically to more accounts over time," into 2027. The figures are Microsoft's own averages, with no account counts or confidence ranges, and the automatic rollout means advertisers may notice changes in performance without having changed anything themselves.
Agencies are building agents on top of such systems. AdExchanger reported that Canvas Worldwide's agentic layer helps Zillow distinguish real renters from dreamers. Zillow's problem is a familiar one for property sites: many visitors browse with no near-term plan to move. "We are known for dreaming," said Ravi Kandikonda, Zillow Group's senior vice president of marketing. Those visitors build awareness, but Zillow also needs lower-funnel conversions from actual movers, and off-the-shelf renter audiences were not precise enough. Canvas, Zillow's media agency since 2023, launched a division called ART Dept. in September and built an agentic layer, Talos, that detects under- or overpacing and proposes optimisations, each approved or rejected by a person on the team before it goes live.
The audiences come from partner data, including a Model Context Protocol connection to Dstillery, which predicts which sites an audience will visit and when. Kandikonda's example is reaching multifamily renters in Seattle while they are on a particular platform: "I need to know when they are on a particular platform." Sally Lee, Canvas's senior vice president of programmatic and ART, described the loop: "Build an audience, activate it, see how it's performing against what that client cares about." AdExchanger reported no performance figures, so the claim for now is about workflow rather than results. Zillow states the outcome it wants, and Canvas turns that into a weighted scorecard; a data-management product called Audience+ builds custom audiences from partner data, and Talos activates and adjusts them in a continuous loop. The human approval step is the notable design choice, at a time when platforms are pushing automatic changes into accounts.
The industry is still arguing about what to call the platforms that sit above all this. Digiday asked WTF is a unified media platform? The term competes with unified advertising platforms, unified buying platforms, demand-side platforms and agency systems, all pitched as AI tools that simplify buying across fragmented channels. The useful distinction, Digiday argued, is between software that coordinates existing systems and software that actually controls where budget goes. Jay Friedman, co-founder of CartographAI, said a unified media platform executes buys across search, social, programmatic and direct deals: "Traditionally, this has been the domain of agency order management systems."
The limits are set by the walled gardens. Ravi Patel, chief executive of SWYM.ai, said such platforms can manage setup and pacing through APIs but not impression-level choices inside Google, Meta or Amazon: "It can manage the 'what' and the 'how much.' It can't manage the 'which impression.'" Inconsistent attribution windows and self-reported measurement are further obstacles, and standards such as AdCP and the IAB Tech Lab's work can cut integration costs but cannot force platforms to disclose data. Ana Milicevic, co-founder of Sparrow Advisers, called the labels directional rather than settled and offered three signs of real change: budgets moving under independent allocation, credible measurement outside the walled gardens and restructured marketing teams. "Value always accrues to whoever is closest to the decision or outright owns the decision," she said.
Google is consolidating its own travel products. Search Engine Roundtable reported that Google released several help documents on AI Max for Search campaigns for travel, seven in all, covering setup, reporting, best practices, troubleshooting and audiences. They accompany the migration of Travel campaigns into a new format called Search campaigns for Travel, in which Google automatically creates new campaigns from existing ones and copies over the closest equivalent settings.
