TITLE: Amazon's Italian delivery firms can face postal licence rules, EU court says
OUTLET: PPC Land

Three Amazon companies that store, route and hand over parcels in Italy can be treated as postal service providers under EU law, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled today, a finding that leaves Italy free to require them to hold a postal authorisation. The Fifth Chamber's judgment in Joined Cases C-535/25 to C-537/25 answers questions from Italy's Council of State, which is hearing Amazon's appeals against fines imposed by the Italian communications regulator AGCOM in July 2018.
The ruling does not close the case. According to the judgment, it is for the Italian court to check the facts and decide whether the three companies actually fit the tests the Court set out. But the reasoning is broad. The Court held that a company does not need to carry out every step of a delivery to be a postal operator, that handing the driving to separate firms does not take a company outside the rules if it sets the terms those firms work under, and that a parcel left in a locker is not delivered until the customer takes it out.
In Short
The EU's top court said today that Amazon companies in Italy which pack and route parcels, direct local delivery firms and run parcel lockers can count as postal service providers, so Italy may require them to hold a postal authorisation. The answer matters beyond Amazon, because it covers any group that runs an online marketplace and also handles delivery of goods sold by other merchants on it. Italy's Council of State must now apply the Court's tests to the facts and decide whether AGCOM's 2018 fines stand.
What the Court decided
The question put by the Council of State was identical in all three cases. According to the judgment, it asked: "Do Articles 2 and 9 of [Directive 97/67] preclude national legislation which makes activities of the kind carried out by [AIL, AIS and AIT] and described in [the resolution at issue] subject to the authorisation regime laid down in respect of the provision of postal services to the public?"
The Court's answer was no. The operative part of the judgment reads, word for word:
Articles 2 and 9 of Directive 97/67/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 15 December 1997 on common rules for the development of the internal market of Community postal services and the improvement of quality of service, as amended by Directive 2008/6/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 20 February 2008,
must be interpreted as not precluding, as regards companies which are involved in the delivery to the end customer of goods sold by third parties on an e-commerce platform operated by the group to which those companies belong, a piece of national legislation which makes the following subject to a general authorisation regime in respect of the provision of postal services:a company which, after taking the goods concerned from its warehouses, packaging those goods and affixing a label bearing an address thereto (thereby enabling those goods to appear in the final form in which they are to be transported), allocates the parcels so composed among the transport undertakings responsible for their delivery, which are selected depending on, inter alia, the delivery time and the areas covered;a company which, after transporting to its warehouses parcels which are ready for dispatch, entrusts transport undertakings which are legally distinct from that company with the delivery of those parcels to the end customer, where that company sets the conditions under which those undertakings are to provide that service; anda company which manages the lockers in which the transport undertakings called upon by that company deposit the postal items of addressees who have selected that delivery method, where those items continue to be monitored and overseen by that company pending the withdrawal of those items by the addressee and where, if the items are not withdrawn within the prescribed period, the company ensures that they are returned to the sender, without any involvement being required in that regard on the part of the addressee.
In plainer terms, Italy may treat each of those three kinds of company as a postal business and make it hold a general authorisation - a standard permit that covers a category of postal services, as opposed to an individual licence granted case by case. The Court's own press release, No 172/26, carried the headline: "Certain activities of the Amazon Italia group companies could be classified as 'postal services' and be subject to obtaining authorisation."
Three companies, three cases
The three appellants are Italian subsidiaries of the same group. According to the judgment, AGCOM noted that their parent company is Amazon.com, Inc. of the United States. Each company has its own case number, and each was matched to a different step of what EU law calls the postal delivery chain.
All three work, according to the judgment, as intermediaries in "the sale and delivery to end customers of items marketed by third-party sellers through Amazon's e-commerce platform, known as 'Amazon Marketplace'." The press release adds that the companies were serving third-party sellers who sold on the platform "but did not themselves ensure delivery of the goods to customers."
How the Amazon delivery chain works, according to the judgment
The judgment reproduces the description of the delivery process set out in AGCOM's 2018 resolution. It has eight steps:
- A third-party seller sends goods to one of the logistics centres run by AIL, using a third-party transporter. The goods remain the seller's property "at all times." AIL receives, sorts and stores them.
- Once a customer places an order, AIL takes the goods from its warehouses, packages them and attaches a label. According to the judgment, the label "includes, inter alia, the name and the address of the end customer."
- When the parcel is ready, AIL allocates the labelled parcels among the delivery companies. These can be third-party national express couriers or Amazon Logistics, which the judgment describes as an entity forming part of AIT. Couriers are selected "depending on, inter alia, delivery times and areas covered."
- Where Amazon Logistics gets the job, AIT collects the goods from the logistics centres and takes them to warehouses called "Delivery Stations," which AIT manages.
- At the Delivery Stations, parcels are divided among local transport companies "with which Amazon collaborates at local level."
- Those local companies carry out the "last mile" to the customer, "under AIT's direction."
- The parcel goes either to the address the customer gave or, if the customer asks, to a locker run by AIS.
- AIS does not just provide the lockers. According to the judgment, it "manages" them, "by monitoring them remotely and overseeing the depositing of postal items by transport undertakings, as well as the subsequent withdrawal of those items by addressees."
Readers familiar with Amazon's seller services will recognise steps 1 to 3 as the model behind Fulfillment by Amazon, in which independent sellers keep stock in Amazon warehouses and Amazon handles packing and delivery. The judgment itself does not use that product name.
What a postal service is, in law
The case turned on definitions in Directive 97/67, the EU's postal services law, as amended in 2008. Article 2 of the directive defines "postal services" as "services involving the clearance, sorting, transport and delivery of postal items." A "postal service provider" is an "undertaking that provides one or more postal services."
Two more definitions did most of the work. The directive defines "distribution" as "the process from sorting at the distribution centre to delivery of postal items to their addressees." A "postal item" is "an item addressed in the final form in which it is to be carried by a postal service provider," which, according to the directive, includes "postal parcels containing merchandise with or without commercial value."
"Sorting" and "transport" are not defined at all. According to the judgment, because the list in Article 2 follows the order of a delivery, those two terms "must be understood as referring to activities distinct from the clearance and distribution of a postal item and taking place between those two steps."
Relying on its 2018 ruling in Confetra and Others (C-259/16 and C-260/16), the Court restated a two-part test. A company is a postal service provider when it provides "at least one of the services exhaustively set out in Article 2(1) of that directive (with the exception of transport-only services)" and that service relates to a "postal item." Transport on its own does not count. Recital 17 of Directive 2008/6 says so directly: "Transport alone should not be considered as a postal service."
Sorting: the packing warehouse (Case C-535/25)
For AIL, the Court held that assigning packed and labelled parcels to delivery companies is "sorting." According to the judgment, the activity involves "determining, depending on considerations connected with the organisation of the delivery process, the arrangements and operators to which those parcels are to be entrusted."
The Court read the word broadly. Citing point 68 of the Advocate General's Opinion, it said sorting can cover "operations performed by an undertaking in order to rationalise and optimise the postal chain, in particular inasmuch as those operations concern the organisation of items in accordance with logistical or operational criteria or the selection of the undertakings responsible for transport depending on transport time and the efficient allocation of resources."
The second condition was also met. Once AIL attaches the label, the parcels "bear an address in the final form in which they are to be transported by the postal service provider," so they are postal items.
The missing first step
One argument the Court dealt with, without attributing it to a party, was that AIL's work cannot be sorting because nothing was "cleared" first. Clearance means collecting postal items. AIL does not collect addressed parcels from anyone; it makes them itself, from stock that sellers deposited earlier.
The Court rejected that point on two grounds. The first was a change in the wording of the directive. In its original 1997 version, Article 2(4) defined clearance as "the operation of collecting postal items deposited at access points." The version in force at the time of the facts reads "the operation of collecting postal items by a postal service provider." With the reference to access points gone, according to the judgment, clearance now covers a business model in which "the sender deposits at the premises of a postal service provider not an already composed 'postal item' but a product which is stored by that provider and which, after the order is received, is packaged and labelled by that provider in such a way that it constitutes a 'postal item'."
The second ground was simpler. Even without clearance, a company only needs to perform one listed service on a postal item to be a postal operator.
Distribution through other firms: the delivery network (Case C-537/25)
AIT does not drive most of the parcels to the door. Local transport firms do. According to the judgment, the Council of State found that AIT nonetheless sets the terms of that work in detail. AIT "sets, in detail, in contracts concluded with local transport undertakings, the conditions under which those undertakings are required to provide their services as regards, in particular, the management of staff employed by those undertakings, and monitors whether those conditions are satisfied, which may give rise to the imposition of financial penalties if, for example, objectives are not met." According to the referring court's description, AIT could set recruitment conditions for those firms' staff and require that certain employees "whose performance it considers unsatisfactory, be dismissed."
