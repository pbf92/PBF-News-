TITLE: Loblaw lets Meta's Muse plan meals and check out groceries in Canada
OUTLET: PPC Land

On November 18, 2026, Meta named Loblaw as the first Canadian retailer to connect its catalogue to Muse, the company's AI agent app. Starting that day, according to Meta, shoppers can ask Muse to plan meals, build a basket and check out with participating Loblaw banners without leaving the app. The announcement, published on Meta's Canada Newsroom under the title "Meta Welcomes Loblaw as First Canadian Retail Partner to Muse," does not say which banners take part, and it carries no statement from Loblaw.
The same post switches on two features that Canadian users had been missing. Voice conversations, which Meta began rolling out in the US on November 5 while leaving Canada out, now reach Canada on mobile, web and Mac, with the animated avatar included. Canadians can also build spaces, the custom tools Meta introduced a day after voice. On the advertising side, Google's November Marketing Platform update, also dated November 18, placed Loblaw Advance audiences inside the Commerce Media Suite in Display & Video 360.
In Short
Meta's AI agent Muse can now plan meals, fill a basket and check out with some of Loblaw's stores in Canada, making Loblaw the first Canadian retailer plugged directly into the app. It matters for Canadian shoppers and for brands that sell through Loblaw, because the grocery order can now start and end in a Meta app rather than on Loblaw's own site, on the same day Google opened Loblaw's shopper audiences to advertisers in Display & Video 360. Meta has not said which Loblaw banners are included, whether orders earn loyalty points or what happens to remembered details such as food allergies.
What the Loblaw connector does
Meta's wording is brief. "Starting today, Loblaw is bringing its catalogue to Muse as a partner connector, the first Canadian retailer to do so," according to the post. A connector is the link that lets Muse act inside another service on a person's behalf, and Meta treats such links as the core of the product: "Connectors are a big reason Muse can do so much."
For Canadian users, the general connectors Meta names are Gmail, Google Calendar and Instagram, so that Muse can "manage email, coordinate schedules and draw on saved content to get everyday tasks done." Loblaw is the first retailer on that list. The post describes the grocery use in one sentence: "With participating Loblaw banners, Canadian shoppers can now ask Muse to help with meal planning, build their basket and check-out, all in Muse."
Memory is part of the pitch. "Muse can remember brand preferences and information like food allergies, making next week's planning even easier," Meta writes. That makes the connector something more than a search box: a shopper's choice of brand, and a health-related detail such as an allergy, are stored by the agent and carried from one week's order to the next.
What "participating" covers is not explained. Loblaw said in February that it operates more than 2,800 locations, including more than 1,100 grocery stores, and employs more than 220,000 people. Its corporate site lists brands including Shoppers Drug Mart, Pharmaprix, President's Choice, Joe Fresh, PC Optimum and the PC Express online grocery service. None of these names appears in Meta's post.
How the arrangement works, according to Meta
The post sets out the mechanics in general terms, without any detail specific to Loblaw. Read in order, they describe five stages.
- A person connects Muse to services they already use. Meta names Gmail, Google Calendar and Instagram for Canada, and now Loblaw's catalogue through participating banners.
- The agent runs on Muse Secure VM, which Meta describes as "a dedicated computer in the cloud that securely stores the data and credentials for the services you connect, while keeping passwords and payment methods out of the agent's view."
- The person sets the scope. In Meta's words: "You choose what your Muse can access."
- Muse handles the request - a meal plan, a shopping list, a basket - drawing on remembered preferences such as brands and allergies.
- Before a purchase goes through, Muse asks. According to the post, "it checks with you before sensitive actions such as sending an email or making a purchase."
The post links to a longer description of Meta's safety approach on its research site and to a security page at security.muse.ai. It does not say whether an order placed through Muse is fulfilled for pickup or delivery, or through which Loblaw service.
Voice and spaces catch up in Canada
Muse has been available in Canada since September 18, when it reached the country on iPhone and the web, ten days after the US launch. Canadian users have nonetheless trailed on features. When Meta added spoken conversations on November 5, it limited voice and avatars to the US at first.
That gap closes with the November 18 post. "At Connect 2026, we previewed real-time conversations with your Muse. Two weeks ago, we introduced voice and now we're rolling it out in Canada in the Muse app on mobile, web and on Mac," according to the post. A tap on the phone icon in the top-right corner starts a conversation. The Canadian version includes the animated avatar: "As your Muse speaks, the avatar uses subtle expressions and gestures that match the tone of the conversation." Three display modes are offered - voice only, the avatar alongside the conversation, or full screen.
Spaces are the second addition. Meta describes a space as "a custom experience you create inside Muse by describing your idea, like a holiday gift tracker, a daily word game, or a planning board for your next DIY project." Connected apps keep them current in the background, "so new concert dates, price changes, or savings progress show up," and a person can pin spaces to the sidebar, move them into the main tabs, hide them or schedule when they appear.
The wording on spaces sits awkwardly next to Meta's earlier announcement. When the feature arrived on November 6, Meta said spaces were available wherever Muse was available, which at the time included Canada. The November 18 post lists spaces among the new experiences for Canadians - "Canadians can now also build spaces" - without explaining whether Canadian access had been held back.
Australia's launch the day before put Canada in an odd position: a market that has had Muse for two months received voice a day after a market where Muse launched with it. Meta's November 17 post for Australia named nine local partners, from Canva to Ticketek, with their features rolling out progressively. Canada now has one named retail partner, and its connector, according to Meta, is live from November 18.
ChatGPT, Google and now Muse
Muse is not Loblaw's first move into AI shopping. On February 12, Loblaw launched a PC Express app inside ChatGPT, built with OpenAI, to help Canadians shop for groceries from Loblaw grocery stores. Retail Insider described those purchases as completed through PC Express for pickup or delivery. Eight days later, the same publication reported that Loblaw would let Canadians buy health, beauty and apparel products directly through AI Mode in Google Search and the Gemini app, using Google's Universal Commerce Protocol, the open standard Google launched in January for AI agents to shop across platforms.
Loblaw's chief executive, Per Bank, told analysts on the company's first-quarter call in May that customer adoption of the ChatGPT integration was ahead of plan and that a second version was coming, according to Retail Insider, whose report gives no figures for the app itself. Loblaw's online sales rose 20.3% year on year in that quarter, according to the same report, driven primarily by PC Express delivery and the integration of third-party delivery services - a figure that covers all online sales, not the ChatGPT app alone.
The three arrangements differ in scope. The Google integration, as reported, left grocery to the ChatGPT app. The Muse connector is framed around meal planning, which points to food. Whether the Muse version runs on the same PC Express systems as the ChatGPT app, neither Meta nor any Loblaw statement published on November 18 says.
The same day, on the advertising side
Loblaw's name also appeared the same day in Google's November Marketing Platform Drop, a post by Marta Martinez, Managing Director, Google Marketing Platform. According to Google, advertisers can now reach Loblaw Advance audiences and prove sales across what Google calls Canada's largest grocery retailer, with closed-loop SKU-level measurement, through Google's Commerce Media Suite in Display & Video 360, where Walmart, Costco and other major retailers are already available.
Loblaw Advance is Loblaw's retail media network, the business that sells advertising built on its shopper data. When Criteo became a preferred demand partner for it on August 5, the two companies described a network with more than 18 million active PC Optimum loyalty members, 2,500 store locations and approximately 25 million opted-in emails sent each week. That store count is lower than the more than 2,800 locations in Loblaw's February release; neither release explains the difference, and the two figures may be defined differently.
Google's suite has been adding retailers through the year. Its January 20 post named Best Buy and Shipt as new participants, and in March Kroger joined the Commerce Media Suite, offering consumer packaged goods brands reach on YouTube and third-party inventory with SKU-level sales reporting.
The timing puts two different routes to the Loblaw shopper side by side. In Display & Video 360, brands buy Loblaw's audience data to reach shoppers across Google's inventory and then measure sales back at Loblaw. In Muse, an agent builds the basket from Loblaw's catalogue on the shopper's behalf. Neither announcement says whether Loblaw Advance placements, such as sponsored products, appear when Muse assembles a basket, or whether a purchase made through Muse is counted in the closed-loop reporting Google describes.
Why this matters for marketers
Muse's relations with large retailers have been uneven. Amazon cut the agent off from Amazon.com in September, eBay restricted its access in November, and GeekWire reported on November 17 that Muse's shopping instructions now tell the agent to leave Amazon out of its searches unless a user asks for it. Loblaw is taking the opposite route, with a sanctioned integration rather than an agent browsing its pages uninvited. For agentic commerce, where software finds and pays for goods for a person, that distinction shapes how much control a retailer keeps over what the agent sees.
The Canadian audience is not broken out. Mark Zuckerberg told analysts on October 21 that more than 10 million people in the US and Canada had set up Muse personal agents within a month and a half, without separating the two countries. Sensor Tower's estimates, published a day earlier, put Muse at 10 million US downloads in 40 days, with the US accounting for the great majority of installs.
Money and data are the two open questions. On the same October call, Zuckerberg said Meta would build commerce tools so that it is paid a fee on the transactions it helps facilitate. The November 18 post does not say whether Loblaw pays such a fee. On data, Meta said at the September launch that conversations and the data inside a person's Muse Secure VM are not shared with its ad systems. In October, Zuckerberg added that people who opt in can connect Muse to the personalization of their content and ads across Meta. A connector that remembers grocery brands and food allergies sits squarely between those two statements, and the Loblaw post does not mention advertising.
Canadian businesses were already part of the Muse rollout: in September, Meta extended Muse for small businesses in the US and Canada, linking the agent to ad accounts and 15 business tools. The Loblaw connector is the first time Meta has named a Canadian consumer retailer inside the agent.
