TITLE: GTA 6 Exposes the Console Hardware Crisis
OUTLET: EE Times

GTA 6 is the biggest launch in entertainment history. But the consoles it runs on were designed around assumptions about silicon economics that no longer hold.
What’s under the hood?
On Nov. 19, after more than a decade of waiting and several delays, Rockstar released Grand Theft Auto VI, or GTA 6. According to Take-Two Interactive, Rockstar’s parent company, the game sold 32.5 million copies before release and generated $3.79 billion in revenue on its first day, which is probably the biggest entertainment launch in history.
While most of the coverage has been about the game, I find the hardware underneath it a more interesting story: GTA 6 runs only on the PlayStation 5 and the Xbox Series X|S, consoles whose silicon was defined around 2019 and which launched in 2020. A console is a long bet on silicon economics, placed years before the first unit ships and, for reasons we’ll explain below, expected to hold for about seven years. In this generation, it seems to me that each of the key assumptions behind that bet has failed, and GTA 6 is the moment when tens of millions of buyers feel it, both at the checkout and on the screen.
The three assumptions are that hardware gets cheaper as a generation matures, that memory is a commodity, and that a frozen platform can carry a generation for seven years.
Hardware was supposed to get cheaper
For decades, the console business ran on a simple curve. A machine launched at a price that often barely covered its cost, and over the following years, as dies shrank, cheaper components and higher volumes brought that cost down, which allowed the price to follow. The PS4 launched at $400 in 2013, and the PS4 Slim arrived three years later at $300. Shawn Layden, the former head of PlayStation, likes to point out that the PS2 became the best-selling console ever, at nearly 160 million units, and that much of that success came years after launch, once the price fell.
The PS5 went the other way. It launched in 2020 with the disc model at $499 and the Digital Edition at $399. After Sony’s second increase in less than a year, effective April 2026, the disc edition costs $649.99, the Digital Edition $599.99, and the PS5 Pro $899.99. Microsoft has moved in the same direction, and as The Economist notes, both consoles now cost more than when they launched six years ago. That is a sharp break with history: Ars Technica found that consoles released before 2016 typically sold for less than half their inflation-adjusted launch price three years after launch.
Tariffs and inflation are part of the story, but the deeper reason is a semiconductor one. As Ars Technica put it already in 2025, chips aren’t improving like they used to, and that is eroding the console price cut. IMHO, the key point is that moving to a newer node no longer makes a chip of the same complexity meaningfully cheaper, because advanced wafers have become so expensive. A die shrink used to be a cost-reduction tool, and for this generation it has mostly stopped being one. Then memory has made things worse.
Memory stopped being a commodity
Beyond scaling silicon down, memory was the component console makers could always count on to get cheaper. In 2026, it became the opposite, in what The Economist calls “ramageddon.” TrendForce revised its first-quarter forecast for conventional DRAM contract prices up to an increase of 90% to 95% quarter over quarter, followed by a further 58% to 63% in the second quarter, with meaningful ramp-ups of new capacity not expected until the second half of 2027, and to contribute meaningfully only in 2028. The increases have moderated to 10% to 15% in the fourth quarter, but they are still increases, on top of a record base.
The mechanism is well known to readers involved in the semiconductor industry. Memory makers are moving wafer capacity and investment to HBM and server DRAM, where AI customers pay more and sign long-term agreements to secure supply. Console memory is a different product from HBM, but it comes from the same three suppliers and competes for the same fab capacity, so it gets squeezed along with everything else. Sony said so directly to investors, noting that rising memory prices increase bill of materials costs for the PS5. Its March price increase was widely attributed to the same cause.
Here is the irony: Technologies are often built for one purpose and later find a second use, and usually the second use sits alongside the first or remains a side business. The GPU went much further. Gamers paid for a decade of GPU development, and the chip designed to draw pixels became the engine of the AI era. In Nvidia’s fiscal first quarter of 2023, gaming and data center were nearly equal, at 43.68% and 45.25% of revenue. By fiscal 2026, Nvidia’s entire graphics business, gaming included, was 11.45%. And starting this year, Nvidia no longer breaks out gaming revenue at all, having folded it into a broader “Edge Computing” line. The second use outgrew the first and has now turned against it: The AI market that grew out of gaming is large enough to outbid gamers for the memory they need. The Economist puts the paradox neatly: Even if AI reduces the cost of making software, it is pushing up the price of hardware, especially memory chips.
Here too, it’s a CPU vs GPU dilemma
The third assumption shows up on screen. Rockstar says GTA 6 targets 30 frames per second on every console, including the PS5 Pro, with no 60fps mode anywhere. The Pro adds sharper images and ray-traced shadows, but it cannot double the frame rate to 60fps. The reason is the CPU. As Rockstar’s technology production director Marc Chambers explained, the Pro’s processor is only slightly better than the base PS5’s, and “it is not powerful enough to run the game’s complex simulation at higher frame rates.”
In fact, much of what makes GTA 6 impressive is open-world simulation of thousands of pedestrians with their own routines, traffic that reacts, physics, police behavior, and a city streamed continuously from storage. Much of that work falls on the CPU, and a good part of it is hard to spread evenly across cores, because each step depends on the one before. At 60fps, the CPU would have about 16 milliseconds per frame to update the whole world, and at 30fps, it has about 33. Given that CPU, Rockstar had to choose between a denser world and a smoother picture with a higher frame rate, and it chose density.
The CPU in question is a custom AMD design with eight Zen 2 cores, a technology from 2019. Even the PS5 Pro, launched in 2024, kept the same CPU and only raised its clock by about 10%. Sony had done the same with the PS4 Pro in 2016, for the same reasons.
Why not design a new chip after all these years the way phone makers do every year? One reason is software compatibility: A console game is tuned to one exact machine. That is how consoles get so much out of modest silicon, and it also means that a new CPU would require revalidating, and often re-tuning, the games already built for it.
True, phones also have to run existing apps, but they handle compatibility differently. A phone app is written for a range of devices, through layers of software that hide the hardware, and it adapts to whatever it finds.
Another difference between phones and consoles is the economics: Phone makers earn their margin on the hardware, so a new model each year means new revenue. Console makers sell the box near cost and earn on software and services over the generation. Studios that spend five years or more on a game need the platform to stay put, and a custom console chip is expensive enough that it has to be amortized over the long run. The volumes are also very different. Apple alone shipped about 248 million iPhones in 2025, while the PS5 took six years to pass 100 million units.
I think there is a broader point here for the semiconductor industry. The PS5 Pro spent its upgrade budget on GPU compute, ray tracing, and an AI upscaler, which is the same direction silicon budgets are moving everywhere, particularly in data centers: Accelerators get the budget, and general-purpose CPUs get much less.
What comes next
What will happen in the next generation of consoles? Sony’s CEO, Hiroki Totoki, has said that the company has not yet decided when to launch its next console, or at what price, and that he expects memory prices to remain high through the 2027 financial year. Bloomberg has reported that Sony is considering pushing the PS6 as far out as 2029, although some have argued that a long delay would cost Sony more than paying extra for memory. Even Nintendo, which launched the Switch 2 at $449.99, has since raised its price by $50, citing “changes in market conditions,” a sign that a less memory-hungry design offers little protection.
Microsoft is in a harder spot: Xbox is cutting about 20% of its workforce, and its CEO, Asha Sharma, has described the industry as facing “the most severe hardware crisis in its history.” Enders Analysis, quoted by The Economist, argues that the future of Xbox itself is now an open question, although reports say its hardware team was largely spared, and work on its next console continues.
The Economist invites us to look at what such increasing hardware costs may do to future games: Raph Koster, a game-industry veteran, estimates that the cost of developing a top-quality game has risen tenfold every decade since the mid-1990s, and GTA 6 reportedly cost over $1 billion to develop. Given these high price tags, publishers are responding with fewer and bigger bets, and the market is increasingly concentrated in a handful of franchises that are decades old. If the hardware needed to run those games continues to get pricier, the audience able to play them may stop growing, and each billion-dollar bet becomes riskier. Mat Piscatella of Circana expects consumers facing $1,000 consoles to move to cheap PCs and phones, and to games such as Fortnite and Minecraft that were designed to run “on a toaster.” Nintendo and Roblox are already serving that audience.
For people in semiconductors, consoles are a useful test case in a broader sense, because they are unusual among consumer chips. They are designed against a fixed price point years in advance, sold at thin margins, and expected to hold for most of a decade. As we saw, a phone chip faces similar cost pressure, but it gets a new design and a new price every year.
The game console story tells us something about the industry as a whole: Silicon costs are not scaling down as fast as they used to, and this is compounded by AI demand pulling memory and other components away from everyone else.
GTA 6 will sell tens of millions of copies regardless. The more interesting question, I think, is what the machines that will run GTA 7 will look like, and how much they will cost.
