TITLE: Antiquities Thieves in Israel Stage Picnic to Hide Robbery
OUTLET: Haaretz

Many a family or casual group of friends enjoys the occasional picnic or bonfire in one of Israel's parks and nature reserves. Sheltered from the moonlight by trees, evening diners al fresco do not normally arouse suspicion of evildoing as they share their meal with ants and other entomological passers-by. But last weekend, a group of outdoor night-time snackers were arrested together with their alleged colleagues on grave suspicions of illegal excavation and attempted antiquities theft.
The picnickers' job was to distract from the alleged fact that a team was in the process of excavating and robbing an ancient cistern 9 meters below ground at Horvat Adash – also known as Khirbet Abu Adas – in Begin Park in the Judean hills outside Jerusalem, the Israel Antiquities Authority explained on Wednesday.
They had come prepared for their nefarious mission with more than sandwiches and potato salad or whatever it was they were eating, and fuel for their campfire. Altogether six, the team had brought excavation equipment including an electric jackhammer – not too subtle there; they also had silent augers and digging tools, a long ladder to descend into the black depths, and lighting equipment to see what was there to steal. They also brought a generator of course, to power the jackhammer and lights.
The arrest was made by inspectors from the IAA's Robbery Prevention Unit, led by the unit's director Amir Ganor.
The damage they did before being apprehended was terrible, Ganor said.
Horvat Adash contains the remains of a settlement that survived for almost 1,000 years, from roughly the 1st century B.C.E. – the Second Temple period – through to the Early Muslim period in the 8th century C.E. Proper excavation under the pine has uncovered ruins of industrial installations and water storage systems, a mikveh featuring a rare double entrance, and a building that archaeologists believe was an early mosque.
Those are the big finds. The thieves were after mobile ones, and in their search within the debris, they damaged the plastered walls and floor of the cistern itself.
Despite their attentions, the cistern still contained fragments of what seem to have been hundreds of pottery vessels, several stone vessels, and a piece of a zoomorphic vessel with a neck shaped like an animal that dates back to the Early Muslim period, the IAA says. In short, the cistern had apparently been repurposed at some point as a garbage dump.
Presumably the thieves were hoping to find treasure, not only broken pottery, in the cistern, the IAA says.
The six suspects may face charges of damaging an antiquities site, a crime for which the statutory penalty is up to five years in prison, the authority adds.
Note that in Israel, all excavations are illegal unless specifically permitted and licensed by the IAA.
Having been the stamping ground for hominins going back at least 2 million years, and in the era of Homo sapiens, becoming the Holy Land whence three great religions would arise, Israel is littered with antiquities sites, and antiquities robbers are legion. It's a constant battle between the cops and robbers, who not rarely come extravagantly prepared for the mission – tractors are not unknown, and the damage they cause can be both enormous, and irreversible.
"Ancient layers are destroyed; findings are displaced and disconnected from their archaeological context," Ganor says. "That causes valuable historical information to be lost, which can never be restored."
It is sadly not rare for the anti-theft authority to notice signs of illegal excavation, and to lurk in wait for the thieves to return. That is how, for example, just this March robbers were caught in the act at Khirbet Hermesh in northern Israel, and wound up sheltering with the cops during a missile attack. The missiles were intercepted.
It also bears saying that the thieves are perpetuating an ancient profession, based on ample evidence of grave robbery in antiquity. In 2022, a convert to Judaism named Yaakov HaGer from the Late Roman or early Byzantine period died and was interred in a cave at Beit She'arim, with a hex designed to deter grave robbers. We don't know if it worked.
"The ancient sites of the Land of Israel are not a free-for-all," pointed out Heritage Minister Amichai Eliyahu. "We will not allow these sites to be turned into a playground for treasure hunters at the expense of our shared public heritage."
