TITLE: New research: Timing of prenatal stress matters for both mothers and infants
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

MSU has a satellite uplink/LTN TV studio and Comrex line for radio interviews upon request.
Why this matters:
- As both prenatal and postpartum depression are key public health issues, researchers at Michigan State University examined not just the volume of stress during pregnancy, but also when it specifically occurs, to understand the effect on women and children following pregnancy.
- Researchers investigated how the timing of stress that pregnant women experience can affect male and female infants differently after birth. Now, through a $3.2 million grant from the National Institutes of Health, the team is expanding the scope to study the impact of prenatal stress on children into childhood.
- The findings will allow healthcare providers to identify periods during pregnancy when women and children are more vulnerable to stress, offering more targeted support and intervention.
EAST LANSING, Mich. – Mental health challenges during pregnancy remain a critical public health issue. As many as 70% of women experience symptoms of stress and anxiety during pregnancy, which can not only affect their own health but also contribute to preterm births, or births before 37 weeks, threatening the health and well-being of infants.
Existing research has examined prenatal stress during a single time point during pregnancy but less has been investigated on how stress during different points in pregnancy can contribute to postpartum maternal mental health and infant outcomes.
Researchers at Michigan State University are filling this critical research gap — conducting the first research of its kind to measure stress during each week of pregnancy starting as early as week 15.
Published in the Archives of Women’s Mental Health and in Psychoneuroendocrinology, MSU researchers found higher stress during pregnancy was associated with more depression for mothers six months after birth and that women who experienced past adverse childhood experiences were additionally susceptible to variability in their stress levels and the timing of stress. As for infants, the timing of when mothers experience stress and the sex of the child can impact how infants behave at six months.
Study findings and impact
In the study published earlier this year by MSU Associate Professor Amy K. Nuttall, the researchers found that higher stress throughout pregnancy is associated with greater risk of postpartum depression.
The study also indicated that the timing and consistency of stress are key to this finding. For women who had four or more adverse childhood experiences, or childhood trauma experiences, higher stress during week 20 and more weekly variability in stress was associated with worse depression.
This research highlights the importance of measuring stress throughout pregnancy and that clinicians should adopt a more targeted approach for women with significant past childhood trauma.
“Postpartum mental health is critically important for mothers and infants. These results could lead to stress screenings in pregnancy and targeted interventions to prevent serious outcomes for the most vulnerable women,” said Nuttall, one of the principal investigators and an associate professor in the Department of Human Development and Family Studies in the College of Social Science.”
To examine the impact of the mother’s stress on infants, Alytia A. Levendosky, professor in the Department of Psychology and interim director of the MSU Psychological Clinic, sought to understand the stress timing impact on infants at 6 months of age.
Her study, also published this year, “Pinpointing the timing of prenatal stress associated with infant biobehavioral reactivity,” found the timing of stress mattered — differing for boys and girls.
The researchers found that for girls, stress around mid-gestation was a sensitive period for cortisol responses when infants were six months old, which refers to hormones that regulate the body’s stress system. Meanwhile for boys, stress around late-gestation was a sensitive period for infant cortisol responses. Overall, higher average stress throughout pregnancy as well as stress in mid-to-late gestation was associated with difficult (fussy) temperament for girls. In contrast, for boys there was only a timing effect of mid-to-late gestation on difficult (fussy) temperament.
“This finding of differential timing effects of prenatal stress on boys and girls has implications for understanding what the most vulnerable periods of gestation are that may inform development of preventative interventions for the intergenerational transmission of stress,” Levendosky said.
Grant powering more research
Building on the existing study, measuring weekly stress in pregnancy in a cohort of almost 400 mother-child pairs; the researchers are investigating how prenatal stress and early childhood stress impact those same infants, now in middle childhood. Joseph S. Lonstein, professor in the Department of Psychology, is also an investigator on this research.
The funding, made possible by a $3.2 million grant from the National Institutes of Health, will be the first longitudinal, multimethod study of how differential timing of early life stress influences development in middle childhood, an age when about 20% of children have behavioral problems.
Specifically, the researchers aim to identify the specific period when stress is most impactful on child outcomes; for whom in terms of subgroup differences such as sex; and how specific biological and behavioral processes explain the effects. The study is also innovative in examining exposure to supportive contexts that buffer the deleterious impacts of stress in addition to examining stress exposure.
“Identifying when, for whom, and how stress has negative impacts on children is highly significant research critical for developing data-informed, targeted interventions for early-life stress to address the alarming mental health crisis among our youth,” said Nuttall.
