TITLE: How Infor and Anthropic Are Bringing Claude to Manufacturing
OUTLET: Manufacturing Digital

Anthropic announced Claude for Manufacturing on 12 November 2026, along with a range of connectors that allow users to integrate the AI tool with their existing systems. One of these connectors is for Infor, which builds cloud-based ERP systems tailored to specific industries.
On the same day, the two companies launched Infor Industry AI for Claude, a separate plugin that allows Claude to execute actions within Infor's ERP software.
Andrew Dalziel is Vice President, Industry and Solutions at Infor. He joined the business in 2013 and has worked with manufacturers for more than two decades. Today, his team's job is to make sure Infor is building and delivering industry-specific solutions that match how businesses operate.
Manufacturing Digital asked Andrew all about Infor's new partnership with Anthropic.
Andrew's key insights:
- Infor's Enterprise AI Adoption Impact Index found 73% of manufacturing respondents say off-the-shelf AI doesn't adequately address their industry's needs.
- Some 54% of business leaders Infor surveyed are comfortable with AI agents fully executing critical processes, yet 15% say no one has clear responsibility for AI governance.
- Only 10% of businesses in Infor's research have a Chief AI Officer. Elsewhere, responsibility for AI sits with the CEO at 23%, the CIO or CTO at 22% and AI committees at 15%.
- Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027. Andrew says AI with no named owner tends not to survive budget review.
- Andrew says how much AI should be allowed to do comes down to two things: how easy an action is to undo and what it costs if it's wrong.
What problem does Infor's partnership with Anthropic solve for manufacturers?
The partnership aims to reduce friction in manufacturing workflows by eliminating manual data movement across systems and enabling AI‑assisted decision-making. Our aim is to solve the gap between knowing something has gone wrong and doing something about it. Manufacturers have stopped treating volatility as the exception. Every day brings a rush order, a late delivery, a machine down or a shift short. The goal manufacturers talk to us about now is organisational agility, ahead of efficiency or digital maturity: how quickly the business can respond when the plan breaks; taking better decisions and faster, enhancing customer service; how can we scale with the same resources? What they want from their software is help deciding what to do next.
This partnership provides the solution to that problem. Users can work in either Infor or Claude, ask questions in everyday language, review data and insights from Infor systems, and execute recommended actions. After reviewing Claude’s recommendations, users can approve actions such as updating a purchase order or adjusting production lines. A planner can ask why a production order is at risk, see whether the cause is a material shortage, machine capacity or labour, and start the fix in Infor from the same conversation. A buyer can see which orders a late receipt will hit, find an alternative supplier and place a replacement purchase order. Customer service teams get the same help on customer orders. Claude provides the reasoning and the conversation; Infor provides the manufacturing logic, the operational data and the controls to reflect real operational constraints. It all must happen in the moment, because that's when you can still change the outcome.
Why isn't a general-purpose AI model enough for a manufacturer on its own?
A generic model can be capable in a general sense, but it doesn’t understand the specificities and context of your business. It hasn't seen your factory, it doesn't know your bills of materials, your routings, which customer orders sit on which line, or that an ingredient batch is on quality hold. Without that context, advice stays generic and that limits the operational potential you are able to realise.
In our Enterprise AI Adoption Impact Index, 73% of manufacturing respondents said off-the-shelf AI doesn't adequately address their industry's needs. UK firms also report the lowest efficiency gains from AI of the seven global markets we surveyed, at 23%. We read that as a problem with how AI is being adopted. Too much of the work of making generic tools fit has fallen on the business, impacting the outcomes achieved.
As our CEO Kevin Samuelson said when we announced the partnership, “when AI begins managing your workflows, the system underneath matters more, not less.”
Skills built for Claude carry Infor's industry-specific manufacturing knowledge into each conversation, and the connector uses the open Model Context Protocol standard, so teams can work across Infor and the other compatible software tools they already rely on. Cost predictability matters as well – without understanding future cost implications, businesses run the risk of running up significant token usage credits, where Infor offers a flat, predictable model.
Letting AI take actions, not just give answers, is a big step. Where should manufacturers draw the line?
It is a big step, though leaders are further along than you might expect. In our findings, 54% of business leaders surveyed said they're comfortable with agents fully executing critical processes, yet 15% say no one has clear responsibility for AI governance. That's a big opportunity.
Businesses will be ready to give AI more to do when they’re able to assign a qualified person to oversee its decisions. Where to draw the line comes down to two things: how easy the action is to undo, and what it costs if it is wrong.
For example, preparing a replacement purchase order, drafting a re-plan or flagging quality drift are good places to start. The AI can do the legwork, with human checks and approvals top ensure any mistakes are caught. But final decisions around changing recipes, releasing a batch or anything in food safety could stay with people.
So before switching anything on, write down what the agent is allowed to do, who signs it off and what gets recorded. And if you can't answer all three, then it isn't ready.
How do you keep a human in control when AI can act inside core systems?
Build the control into the system itself, so it doesn't depend on someone remembering to check.
With Infor Industry AI for Claude, every change needs a person's explicit approval before it happens. The AI can only see and do what that person is already permitted to perform within the Infor solution, so existing roles still apply. Every action is recorded in the Infor solution, so quality teams and auditors can see what was suggested and who approved it. We firmly believe in the importance of keeping a qualified ‘human-in-the-loop’ and ensuring authorisation, traceability and auditability as part of governance.
And we know UK businesses want this – in our research, 53% of UK firms said their AI output needs more human review, the highest of any market we surveyed. However, confidence has also dipped, with the share of UK firms confident they can introduce AI without disrupting operations falling from 75% to 66% between our spring and summer surveys.
Industry-specific tools help close the confidence gap. When the AI already understands how a plant plans, makes and ships, and works inside the controls the business has set, its recommendations make sense to the people reviewing them, so there's less to second-guess and more reason to trust it. That depth of industry expertise is what we've built Infor Industry AI for Claude around.
Who should own AI decisions inside a manufacturer? Why?
The person who owns the business outcome owns the decisions. Whoever sets the rules for an agent owns those rules. Whoever approves an action owns that decision. Think of AI as a member of your team. If the AI is re-planning a line, that's the planner and the operations lead; if it's switching suppliers, it's the buyer. IT owns the platform, the security and the guardrails, but shouldn't be signing off a production schedule.
Most businesses haven't settled this yet, which gives early movers an advantage. In our research, only 10% have a Chief AI Officer, and responsibility is spread across the CEO (23%), the CIO or CTO (22%) and AI committees (15%). The firms that understand all the decisions that they take and can name an owner for each decision first will be the ones that scale.
It matters because AI that nobody owns tends not to survive budget review. Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027. Our prediction for 2027 is that manufacturers will fund AI by the order, the line and the batch, not by the pilot. If a named person can't point to the order, it was able to find a way to deliver on-time or the hours it freed, it will lose its budget.
Where is the quickest AI win in manufacturing operations?
Start where the data already exists, and where the pain is felt every day. For most plants that's the 6am problem: a supplier is late, a line goes down or two people don't turn up, and someone spends the first hours of the shift working out which orders are affected. Getting that answer in minutes, alongside a recovery plan, is the quickest win I know of.
Before switching anything on, write down what the agent is allowed to do, who signs it off and what gets recorded. And if you can't answer all three, then it isn't ready.
