TITLE: IAB Australia survey: 8 of 12 search recruiters expect fewer junior roles
OUTLET: PPC Land

IAB Australia today published Future Search Skills, a 21-page paper from its Future of Search Working Group that names three skills search marketers will need over the next few years and argues that automation is removing the routine work through which junior practitioners used to learn the trade. The paper draws on the experience of working group members from brands, agencies, publishers, platforms and retail media networks, on discussion at the industry body's Discovery: AI & Search Summit in July, and on a survey of 12 specialist search and performance marketing recruiters conducted in October 2026.
The recruiter numbers are small, and the paper says so. On future demand for search roles the recruiters are split, but on skills and junior hiring they point the same way. According to IAB Australia, 58% of the recruiters said demand for search and performance roles had fallen over the past 12 to 24 months, 92% said demand for AI-related skills had risen, and two thirds expect demand for junior roles to keep falling over the next two to three years.
In Short
IAB Australia's search working group today published a paper naming three skills search marketers will need: judging what AI systems recommend, working across paid, organic, AI and social search, and pairing search depth with a second specialty. It matters because platforms now automate bidding, campaign setup and reporting, and a small survey of 12 recruiters in the paper found two thirds expect demand for junior roles to keep falling. What changes is how people learn the trade, with the paper asking employers, platforms and universities to rebuild entry-level training around diagnosis, testing and judgement instead of manual execution.
A paper written by the people it describes
The Future of Search Working Group is made up of practitioners, not academics, and three of its 18 listed members work for platforms whose products appear in the paper as examples: Google, Microsoft and Reddit. Agencies including WPP Media, OMD, Spark Foundry and iProspect sit alongside publishers such as News Corp Australia and The Guardian, advertisers such as Mars and Suncorp, and Cartology, the Woolworths Group retail media business.
According to the paper, its aim is "to help existing search professionals, in both SEM and SEO, expand their skills, and to help people preparing to enter the workforce understand how search-related roles are changing and what they will need to succeed in them."
The same group has published on search before. In June 2025 it released research on social search, which PPC Land covered as social media becoming a search engine for younger shoppers, and in June 2026 it produced a 14-page paper on the agentic AI shift for search marketers. The new paper turns from how consumers search to the people who run search campaigns.
Search is still the largest line in the budget
"Search is not shrinking," the paper states. Search advertising in Australia reached A$8.6 billion in the 2026 financial year and remains the largest digital advertising investment sector, according to IAB Australia. Total internet advertising reached A$19.8 billion, the strongest growth in four years. When those figures came out at the end of August, PPC Land reported that search held a 43% share of the market and grew 13.2% on the previous year.
The paper also cites Google handling more than 5 trillion searches a year, and notes that people who research on platforms like ChatGPT "still tend to return to search to find the brand and buy." What has changed, in the paper's framing, is where people search and the fact that AI agents increasingly do the searching for them. Discovery now runs across search engines, AI answers, social and video platforms, retail marketplaces and AI assistants.
One agency search lead at the July summit put it in a line the paper repeats: most teams are still built to run search as a channel, "when it has become a behaviour."
That shift moves practitioner work upstream, toward planning, stakeholder management, the evaluation of campaigns and creative, and closer contact with chief financial officers. The paper insists the work still needs someone who understands how automated systems operate, a role it describes as human in the loop. One recruiter quoted in the paper went further:
"Platform proficiency matters more than ever, even at senior level. People used to move off the tools as they progressed. That's no longer advisable."
Five eras, from manual bids to agents
The paper divides the history of search platforms into five "activation eras," ending in an agentic period in which agents connect advertising platforms and data through standards such as MCP, the Model Context Protocol. The paper's glossary defines it as "an open standard that lets AI tools and agents connect to other software and data, such as advertising platforms." The dates and descriptions below are the paper's.
The fifth era is the one the paper is written for: practitioners set goals and guardrails while agents act on both sides of the auction, for the consumer who searches and for the marketer who buys.
Skill one: judgement over AI-driven search
The first skill is AI-driven search, though the paper is careful about what that means. Using AI, it argues, is "becoming a baseline expectation rather than a differentiator on its own." The skill is judgement: questioning what automated systems recommend, checking it against the business objective, and knowing when a person needs to make the call.
The paper illustrates the point with a familiar case. An automated campaign finds more conversions, lowers cost per acquisition or uncovers new query demand. On paper, the optimisation is working. But the extra leads may be lower quality, the growth may come from a market the business cannot service efficiently, or the campaign may be optimising toward form submissions when the business cares about qualified opportunities and revenue. "These are not necessarily optimisation problems. They are business judgement problems," the paper says.
AI also opens analysis that rarely had time before. One agency search lead described matching search postcodes to socioeconomic data from the Australian Bureau of Statistics to check whether campaigns were reaching the right people, and to compare who searches with who clicks through.
The paper cites Microsoft's account of the marketing services group Stagwell, which reduced some paid search campaign audits "from hours to minutes" using a Copilot agent connected to Microsoft Advertising data. It adds a qualification: "Microsoft's other published MCP examples still retain human reviewers before changes go live." In June, Microsoft moved its Advertising MCP server into an open pilot with read-only access to live campaign data, naming Stagwell among the pilot partners.
Andrew Ng, founder of DeepLearning.AI, is cited for what he called a human "context advantage": people carry years of customer, organisational and situational context that may never reach the AI system. The paper turns this into a list of questions. Has the market changed? Has the competitive position changed? Is the proposition still compelling? Are the right conversion and customer signals being fed back into bidding? Is search being asked to solve the right acquisition problem? The question it wants raised most often is this one:
"Is this improving the business outcome and strategic direction of the campaign, or mainly improving the metric the platform has been asked to optimise towards?"
Clients are also changing. "With AI giving clients easy access to information, they question decisions more, so the ability to influence and back up the work matters more," one recruiter said.
The governance gap
Tools are getting easier to use; the operating model around them is not. The paper cites Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise research for Australia: 61% of Australian organisations report improved efficiency from AI, while only 30% say they are using AI to deeply transform how they work. Deloitte also reports that 69% are using autonomous AI agents, while only 22% have advanced agent governance models.
"More automation does not necessarily mean a more mature system," the paper says. Maturity, in its definition, is whether an organisation has deliberately decided what should be automated and to what extent, what still needs practitioner review, what business and customer data can be used, who owns the decision when the system recommends an action, how risk and brand controls are managed, and what success is measured against. Flagging search-term anomalies or summarising an account audit may be relatively low-risk; reallocating major budget, expanding into new demand, changing brand messaging or optimising against sensitive customer data may need far more human involvement.
The paper points to two IAB Australia resources: its AI-Ready Advertising Infrastructure, covering organisational maturity, data governance, vendor assessment, model risk, generative AI usage policies and incident response, and its 2026 LLM Prompting Framework. In November 2025, IAB Australia's AI Working Group published an LLM prompting guide built around context, role, objective and task.
How the learning framework works
According to IAB Australia, the framework for building practical AI capability in search has four stages, running from core capability to greater technical complexity. The paper calls Apply and Govern "the centre of gravity for most practitioners, with Govern as the key focus." The stages, with the paper's examples, are:
- Understand. Build enough AI literacy to use it safely and to know where it appears across search planning and management. The examples: follow platform documentation, AI usage guidelines, workshops and industry updates; treat outputs as drafts and check claims, numbers and sources before use; understand privacy, security and approved-use requirements before sharing business or customer data; and know whether AI is "assisting, recommending, optimising or acting."
- Apply. Use AI features intentionally, monitor results closely and learn through testing. In automated bidding, the platform manages auction-time bids "while the practitioner still defines the conversion goal, value signal and efficiency target." For Google's AI Max for Search, the paper's wording is "Test expanded matching, asset customisation and URL expansion with the right controls in place." AI-assisted analysis is validated against the business question, and experiments or A/B tests come before scaling.
- Govern (key focus). Measurement quality, business-value signals, attribution, quality assurance, privacy, risk and human review. The examples: optimise to qualified leads, not form fills, by feeding CRM outcomes back into bidding; measure commercial outcomes such as ROI alongside the effect of the AI feature itself; check AI-generated assets against brand, legal and category requirements; challenge recommendations against the market, customer and objective; and capture learnings for future decisions.
- Orchestrate. Connected workflows, agents and automation "where they solve a real business problem." The paper's example is Stagwell's Assembly, which used a Copilot agent connected through Microsoft Advertising MCP to cut some audits from hours to minutes, "with practitioners reviewing outputs and making the final call." Connected workflows use APIs, MCPs or agents "with clear permissions, approval points and ownership."
AI Max carries its own trade-offs. When Google released its implementation guide in 2025, PPC Land reported that enabling Final URL expansion means pinned headlines are not respected, the kind of control question the Apply stage is meant to surface.
