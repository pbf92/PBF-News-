TITLE: Umbrella Review Identifies Shared Inflammatory Biomarkers Linking Depression and Cardiovascular Disease
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive umbrella review synthesizing more than a hundred meta-analyses has mapped the shared inflammatory landscape connecting depressive disorders and cardiovascular disease, uncovering nine prioritized biomarkers that demonstrate convergent statistical signals across both medical domains. To address this separation, investigators systematically searched five major biomedical databases from inception through 1 April 2026, retrieving 11,964 records across PubMed, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. Following deduplication and screening, the investigators reviewed 431 full-text reports and ultimately incorporated 103 systematic reviews with meta-analyses into their overarching synthesis. The included publications comprised 45 systematic reviews evaluating inflammatory markers in depression and 58 reviews examining inflammatory markers in cardiovascular disease, capturing cumulative research trends that expanded steadily over time across adult populations.
Across the 103 included systematic reviews, the primary literature examined 157 distinct inflammatory biomarkers and contributed an initial pool of 553 biomarker-level estimates, consisting of 380 estimates from depression research and 173 from cardiovascular investigations. To establish cross-field relevance, the authors implemented a rigorous cross-field filtering criterion, retaining only biomarkers that had been quantitatively evaluated in systematic reviews within both clinical fields. This requirement narrowed the primary synthesis down to a focused subset of 21 shared inflammatory biomarkers covered across 84 systematic reviews, with 43 reviews addressing depression and 41 addressing cardiovascular disease. These 84 reviews contributed 314 shared-biomarker estimates, with 183 originating from depression meta-analyses and 131 drawn from cardiovascular meta-analyses. The remaining 19 systematic reviews focused exclusively on biomarkers studied within a single medical domain and were set aside from the shared synthesis, although they remained within the wider methodological evaluation to ensure a complete appraisal of review quality across the broader research landscape.
The retained 21 shared biomarkers spanned three distinct biological measurement categories: soluble molecular markers, cellular measures, and complete blood count-derived composite indices. Soluble molecular markers comprised circulating cytokines, chemokines, and acute-phase proteins, including C-reactive protein, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, interleukin-18, monocyte chemoattractant protein 1, also designated as CCL2, interleukin-1, interleukin-1 beta, interleukin-8, P-selectin, platelet factor 4, also known as CXCL4, transforming growth factor-beta 1, interleukin-17, fibrinogen, interleukin-10, and tumor necrosis factor. Cellular measures consisted of isolated counts of circulating immune cells, including absolute neutrophil counts, lymphocyte counts, monocyte counts, and total white blood cell count. Composite indices captured integrated hematological measures derived from routine complete blood counts, specifically the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and the systemic immune-inflammation index. As defined within the synthesis, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio reflect divisions of absolute neutrophil or platelet counts by lymphocyte counts, whereas the systemic immune-inflammation index integrates all three cell populations by multiplying platelet count by neutrophil count and dividing by lymphocyte count.
To evaluate the strength of evidence supporting each shared candidate, the researchers implemented a prespecified biomarker-level grading framework based on statistical significance and sample size thresholds within the largest eligible pooled meta-analytic estimate in both fields. Biomarkers were designated as class I, indicating highly suggestive shared evidence, if the largest eligible pooled estimate in both depression and cardiovascular disease achieved statistical significance at a threshold of P less than 0.05 and encompassed at least 1,000 participants. Nine biomarkers satisfied these strict class I criteria: C-reactive protein, including high-sensitivity assays, interleukin-6, interleukin-18, monocyte chemoattractant protein 1, neutrophil counts, monocyte counts, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and the systemic immune-inflammation index. Two additional markers, interleukin-1 beta and CXCL4, demonstrated statistically significant pooled associations in both fields but lacked the required 1,000-participant threshold in at least one field, resulting in their classification as class II biomarkers with potential shared evidence. The remaining ten biomarkers were categorized as class III inconclusive evidence because they failed significance or sample thresholds across both literatures.
Despite the identification of prioritized signals, the methodological confidence of the contributing evidence base proved remarkably fragile when appraised against the standardized AMSTAR 2 measurement tool. Among all 103 systematic reviews evaluated, 78 reviews were rated as having critically low methodological quality, 25 were rated as low quality, and none attained moderate or high ratings. This widespread deficit was driven predominantly by recurrent transparency failures and analytical omissions across critical appraisal domains. Specifically, 92.2 percent of systematic reviews failed to provide a comprehensive list of excluded studies with justifications, 63.1 percent failed to adequately consider the impact of primary study risk of bias when interpreting conclusions, and 60.2 percent lacked a preregistered study protocol. Conversely, technical execution was generally sound: 93.2 percent detailed literature search strategies adequately, 83.5 percent conducted risk-of-bias assessments, 83.5 percent evaluated publication bias, and 99.0 percent utilized appropriate statistical meta-analytic methods. A scenario analysis revealed that 33 reviews could have reached moderate confidence through proper protocol registration and excluded-study documentation.
To test whether class I findings depended on critically flawed publications, the authors conducted quality-restricted sensitivity analyses by excluding all systematic reviews rated as critically low on the AMSTAR 2 scale. Following this restriction, cross-field support was retained for six of the nine prioritized biomarkers: C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, neutrophils, monocytes, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and the systemic immune-inflammation index. For neutrophils, monocytes, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and the systemic immune-inflammation index, the largest eligible meta-analyses in both fields originated from reviews that were not rated as critically low, leaving their class I standing unaffected. For C-reactive protein and interleukin-6, the primary estimates derived from critically low reviews, but both remained fully supported when replaced by secondary meta-analyses from reviews rated as low. However, support for the remaining three biomarkers weakened under quality restriction. Platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio retained a significant cardiovascular association, but the largest remaining sample fell below the 1,000-participant threshold at 822 individuals, while interleukin-18 and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 lacked eligible cardiovascular-side meta-analyses rated as low quality.
The researchers also examined statistical heterogeneity and primary-study duplication across the shared-biomarker literature to clarify how independently these pooled estimates were generated. Among the 314 extracted shared estimates, high statistical heterogeneity exceeding an I-squared value of 75 percent was present in 54.1 percent of estimates, moderate heterogeneity between 25 and 75 percent occurred in 18.8 percent, and low heterogeneity under 25 percent occurred in only 9.6 percent. Single primary studies accounted for 4.8 percent of estimates, while 12.7 percent omitted heterogeneity data. Primary-study overlap, calculated via the corrected covered area across 1,646 confirmed primary study occurrences, differed markedly between fields. In cardiovascular disease, overlap was slight across all evaluable sets, ranging from 0.00 percent for interleukin-18 to 4.17 percent for neutrophils. Within depression meta-analyses, however, overlap ranged from slight for C-reactive protein and monocytes to moderate for CCL2 and neutrophils, high for interleukin-18 at 10.71 percent, and very high for neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio at 16.18 percent and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio at 16.67 percent.
Biologically, the class I findings align with a multilevel model of systemic inflammation that bridges soluble signaling cascades with dynamic leukocyte and platelet redistribution across both pathological states. At the soluble level, interleukin-6 functions as an upstream cytokine inducer produced by activated monocytes and macrophages, stimulating hepatic production of C-reactive protein while altering neuroendocrine pathways and neuroplasticity relevant to depression. C-reactive protein operates downstream as an integrated systemic marker of cytokine activity that may alter blood-brain barrier permeability. Interleukin-18 signals inflammasome activation linking innate immune responses to interferon-gamma release, while CCL2 governs chemokine-mediated monocyte recruitment to vascular tissues. At the cellular level, elevated neutrophil and monocyte counts corroborate innate immune mobilization implicated in atheroinflammatory injury and cytokine release.
Exploratory supplementary analyses further characterized distinct clinical niches within the evidence base, examining central nervous system markers and disease severity correlations. Within the shared dataset, 19 estimates evaluated biomarker measurements containing cerebrospinal fluid, representing 16 fluid-specific or cell-count measures and 3 combined peripheral and fluid samples. In cardiovascular literature, cerebrospinal fluid estimates were confined entirely to subarachnoid hemorrhage, where interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor were elevated. In depression, interleukin-6 was elevated across all cerebrospinal fluid meta-analyses, whereas interleukin-8 showed mixed findings and interleukin-1 beta, tumor necrosis factor, and white cell counts remained mixed or non-significant. Separate targeted assessments of excluded continuous-score studies identified positive correlations between C-reactive protein and subsequent depressive symptom scores across two systematic reviews, while interleukin-6 showed mixed associations. In cardiovascular records, positive correlations linked neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio to angiographic coronary artery disease severity, alongside positive correlations between interleukin-6 and coronary artery calcification severity, illustrating that peripheral inflammatory intensity tracks clinical severity dimensions.
In their concluding synthesis, the study authors emphasized that while these prioritized biomarkers identify consistent cross-field signals, they do not establish a singular causal mechanism or provide an immediately deployable clinical panel. Generalizability remains bounded by clinical context: cardiovascular inflammatory processes diverge substantially between acute cerebrovascular trauma such as ischemic stroke and chronic vascular conditions like stable coronary disease. Similarly, depressive presentations varied across populations, though post-stroke depression notably mirrored the broader elevations in C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio.
