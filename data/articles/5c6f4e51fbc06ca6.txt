TITLE: Common classroom accommodations for ADHD fail to improve student behavior or schoolwork in clinical trial
OUTLET: PsyPost

A clinical trial published in JAMA Pediatrics provides evidence that four widely used classroom accommodations for children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) do not improve student behavior or academic performance. In contrast, structured behavioral classroom supports and a moderate dose of stimulant medication both substantially reduced classroom rule-breaking, while medication also increased assignment completion and quiz scores.
The findings suggest that schools seeking to help students with ADHD succeed may achieve better results from active behavioral strategies than from standard classroom accommodations.
Why classroom accommodations for ADHD needed a test
ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by ongoing difficulties sustaining attention, curbing impulsive actions, and regulating physical restlessness. In elementary school classrooms, those traits frequently surface as trouble staying seated, following classroom rules, completing independent worksheets, and keeping up with group lessons.
In the United States, roughly half of students diagnosed with ADHD receive formal educational services through individualized education programs under federal special education law or Section 504 plans under disability civil rights law. These plans routinely center on academic accommodations, which are adjustments to classroom surroundings, work schedules, or testing conditions meant to help students complete the same assignments as their classmates. Common examples include extra time on assignments and quizzes, frequent short breaks, noise-canceling headphones with desk dividers, and permission to stand while working.

Keeping students with ADHD engaged in schoolwork involves more than simply blocking out hallway noise or visual clutter. For instance, an earlier classroom study of children with ADHD indicated that students with the condition were especially prone to internal mind-wandering, which lowered their classroom work output when they were anticipating a reward, while stimulant medication lengthened reaction times on a sustained attention task without improving productivity in that classroom setting.
Clinical guidelines for elementary school children with ADHD generally recommend stimulant medications, which act on brain chemical signaling involved in focus and self-control, alongside positive behavioral interventions, which use structured expectations, frequent specific praise, daily behavior goals, and earned privileges.
Ordinary teacher reminders alone rarely match the impact of systematic reinforcement; a 2026 single-student classroom study observed that a first-grader with ADHD stayed focused on assignments for only about a minute and a half when teachers relied on routine verbal prompts, whereas introducing structured positive reinforcement extended the student’s on-task time.
Even though accommodations are common in school plans, researchers had not systematically tested how they perform on their own or alongside behavioral therapy and stimulant medication. To test all three approaches separately and together, Gregory A. Fabiano, a professor of psychology at Florida International University and co-author of the book “Daily Behavior Report Cards,” led a clinical trial at the university’s Center for Children and Families, one of its preeminent programs, and its Center for Children and Families of Western New York.
“Children with ADHD experience considerable difficulties in school settings, and this is coupled with concerning long-term academic outcomes,” Fabiano told PsyPost. “Given this, it is critical that we are adequately supporting and treating these children, to give them the best opportunity to proceed. One of the most common approaches includes provision of academic accommodations such as extra time to complete tasks or a low distraction workspace, but these approaches have typically not been rigorously evaluated to see if they work.”
Inside the summer classroom crossover trial
The researchers conducted a crossover randomized clinical trial registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, with the study protocol and statistical analysis plan published alongside the paper. Rather than dividing participants into separate permanent groups, a crossover design rotates every child through every treatment condition in a randomized, counterbalanced order so that each student serves as their own comparison.
Across four summers from 2022 to 2025 at sites in the southeastern and northeastern United States, the team screened 306 children in kindergarten through sixth grade and enrolled 280 who met diagnostic criteria for ADHD based on parent and teacher symptom ratings and caregiver diagnostic interviews. Children with an IQ below 70, prior adverse reactions to methylphenidate, a school suspension or expulsion in the past year, psychosis, or higher-support-needs autism were excluded.
Of the 280 enrolled children, 25 did not start the summer program, including six whose families had concerns about medication or side effects. Among the 255 children (79 percent male and 21 percent female) who began the intervention, 235 completed the trial; of the 20 who left early, 11 departed to pursue alternative treatments, four left over medication or side-effect concerns, three had scheduling conflicts, and two no longer wished to participate.
The study took place inside a structured eight-week summer treatment program, which was adjusted to seven weeks with modified block lengths and added class periods for the fourth cohort due to local school calendars. Grouped by age into classes of 12 to 15 peers, students attended two one-hour classroom sessions each day before spending the remaining hours in recreational activities such as sports, board games, and arts and crafts. Each classroom was staffed by a teacher and an aide who completed a week of intensive training and received daily supervisor feedback, following prescribed procedures 97 percent of the time during seatwork and 94 percent of the time during lessons.
During the first daily class hour, students worked independently at desks on reading, math, and language arts assignments. During the second class hour, teachers led a whole-group science or social studies lesson followed by small-group cooperative tasks and a 10-question multiple-choice quiz scored from 0 to 100 percent.
Testing behavior therapy, medication, and four accommodations
Three treatment factors were systematically varied for every student across the summer. Classroom behavior management alternated in two-week blocks between a general classroom modeled on typical school practices and an enhanced behavioral intervention classroom. In the general classroom, staff reviewed rules at the start of class, quietly logged rule violations on a private clipboard, gave praise sparingly at a ratio of one praise statement for every three commands, awarded recess regardless of behavior, and sent students out to speak with a supervisor after three aggressive, defiant, or disrespectful acts.
In the enhanced behavioral classroom, staff recorded rule violations on a public point board and delivered three specific praise statements for every command or correction. Students also received individualized daily report cards tracking personalized classroom behavior targets, earning free time at the end of each period for meeting their goals alongside public recognition and points they could trade weekly for small toys and games.
Stimulant medication alternated in one-week blocks under double-blind conditions, meaning neither the children nor the classroom staff knew which capsule was given each week. Every morning between 7:30 and 8:00 a.m., participants swallowed an opaque capsule containing either an inactive placebo or extended-release methylphenidate at a moderate weight-based dose of 0.3 milligrams per kilogram, rounded to the nearest 10 milligrams and averaging about 19 milligrams.
Academic accommodations rotated randomly each day of the week, cycling every child through a standard classroom day with no special adjustments and four common school accommodations. On extended-time days, students received 50 percent more time than usual, expanding the independent worksheet period from 30 to 45 minutes and the daily quiz from 10 to 15 minutes. On frequent-break days, staff paused work for a one-minute quiet break to rest, draw, or read after every five minutes of seatwork and every 2.5 minutes of quiz time, working out to roughly six breaks per hour.
The other two accommodations altered the student’s physical workspace rather than the schedule. On adapted-seating days, children were permitted to stand at their desks instead of remaining in their chairs, while low-distraction days provided noise-canceling headphones to muffle classroom sounds and three-sided desk dividers to block visual clutter.
Daily outcomes included observed classroom rule violations in both class periods, quiz scores, and academic productivity. Observers recorded rule violations with moderate-to-good consistency across raters, reflected in an intraclass correlation of 0.71 on a 0-to-1 agreement scale.
The researchers initially planned to analyze the outcomes using analysis of variance, a standard method for comparing group averages. Because counts of rule violations and completed problems varied widely from student to student, they shifted to statistical mixed models built for count data. To account for the extra minutes given on extended-time days, they measured seatwork productivity as the rate of problems completed correctly per minute.
Accommodations failed to improve behavior or schoolwork
When the researchers first tested whether any treatments modified one another’s effectiveness, none of the two-way or three-way interactions were statistically significant. Academic accommodations did not become helpful when paired with stimulant medication or behavioral therapy, and medication and behavioral therapy worked independently rather than amplifying or blunting each other.
Across all four accommodations, none produced a statistically significant drop in rule-breaking during either class period, and none produced a statistically significant increase in quiz scores compared with the standard classroom without accommodations. On seatwork productivity, every accommodation leaned in the opposite direction, with none increasing the rate of problems completed correctly per minute.
Extended time and low-distraction workspaces lowered per-minute problem completion by 11 percent and 4 percent, respectively, with both reductions holding up after the researchers adjusted for multiple statistical tests; in the unmedicated general classroom, for example, students averaged 1.25 correct problems per minute with extended time compared with 1.41 without accommodations.
Frequent breaks showed a 3 percent dip that was not statistically significant at any threshold, while a 3 percent dip with standing desks was no longer statistically significant once adjusted for multiple tests. In the paper’s discussion, the authors write that accommodations produced no benefit and, if anything, a general worsening, and argue that school effort spent evaluating students for and providing them may be useless at best and counterproductive at worst.
“We were surprised that accommodations did not work alone, or in combination with any of the other treatments,” Fabiano explained. “The results we obtained suggested that these approaches really don’t move the needle with regard to improving classroom functioning in children with ADHD.”
