TITLE: Curcumin Increases HDL Cholesterol in Metabolic Syndrome but Certainty Is Very Low
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis examining dietary polyphenol supplementation in adults diagnosed with metabolic syndrome indicates that these compounds provide no consistent health benefits across syndrome components, despite a modest increase in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol associated with curcumin. While curcumin supplementation was found to elevate high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels among trial participants, the researchers determined that the certainty of this evidence remains very low.
Published in Frontiers in Nutrition by researchers from Dalian Medical University in Dalian, China, the study sought to resolve persistent inconsistencies across clinical trials regarding polyphenol efficacy. To clarify these mixed findings, the authors conducted an outcome-specific systematic review and meta-analysis, evaluating trials separately by compound and clinical outcome. The investigation analyzed six specific dietary polyphenol interventions: curcumin, resveratrol, anthocyanin-rich foods, catechin preparations, hesperidin, and genistein.
To establish the evidence base, investigators searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library through March 10, 2026, for randomized controlled trials conducted in adults diagnosed with metabolic syndrome. Linked reports were treated as single trials to prevent double counting. The systematic search identified twenty-three reports representing twenty independent trials evaluating the six polyphenol interventions. Fifteen of these trials contributed data to thirty-eight separate outcome-specific meta-analyses covering curcumin, resveratrol, anthocyanin-rich foods, and catechin preparations. Meanwhile, clinical trials investigating hesperidin and genistein could not be mathematically synthesized and remained unpooled.
The authors pooled trial outcomes using restricted maximum-likelihood random-effects models. When at least three trials contributed to an analysis and the estimated between-study variance was positive, the researchers reported the wider interval between the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman method and the Wald method; otherwise, standard Wald confidence intervals were used. The certainty of evidence for thirty pooled outcomes of curcumin, resveratrol, and anthocyanin-rich foods was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework. The eight pooled catechin outcomes were not assessed using this framework.
In the curcumin meta-analyses, supplementation was associated with a statistically significant increase in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, producing a mean difference of 5.01 milligrams per deciliter, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 1.78 to 8.23 milligrams per deciliter and an I-squared heterogeneity of 78.9 percent. This finding persisted during leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. Conversely, curcumin showed no statistically significant effect on low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, with a mean difference of minus 4.03 milligrams per deciliter, a wide 95 percent confidence interval from minus 20.07 to 12.01 milligrams per deciliter, and heterogeneity of 87.6 percent. Other primary pooled outcomes for curcumin were nonsignificant.
For catechin preparations, the primary statistical model pooling two trials indicated a statistically significant reduction in body mass index, yielding a mean difference of minus 0.82 kilograms per square meter with a 95 percent confidence interval from minus 1.39 to minus 0.26 kilograms per square meter and an I-squared value of 0.0 percent. However, this estimate proved sensitive to the assumed crossover correlation value and relied on two small trials with differing study designs. The remaining seven pooled catechin outcomes showed no statistically significant differences between intervention and control arms.
The meta-analyses evaluating resveratrol and anthocyanin-rich foods showed no statistically significant primary pooled effects across any analyzed metabolic syndrome components. When evaluating the certainty of evidence for the thirty-eight outcomes assessed across the review, certainty ratings ranged from moderate to very low.
The study was authored by Tiecheng Huang, Gulimire Abolimiti, Ningning Wang, Jiaxin Zhu, Yangyi Zhou, Yixue Deng, Xin Zheng, and Tao Yang of Dalian Medical University. The authors concluded that current empirical evidence does not establish consistent therapeutic benefits for dietary polyphenols across metabolic syndrome components. Because the few observed improvements were characterized by very low certainty, sensitivity to model assumptions, or small sample sizes, the researchers stated that these findings do not justify routine clinical recommendations for dietary polyphenol supplementation.
