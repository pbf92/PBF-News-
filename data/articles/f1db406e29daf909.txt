TITLE: Bombay HC backs Meta's right to disable Blink Digital's ad accounts over policy violations
OUTLET: Storyboard18

The Bombay High Court has rejected Blink Digital India Private Limited's plea seeking immediate restoration of its four Business Manager accounts and associated advertising assets on Meta's platforms, following their disablement in August 2026. However, the court has allowed the agency to approach Meta afresh for reinstatement, provided it assures compliance with the platform's policies. Meta has been directed to consider the request and communicate its decision within two weeks of receiving it.
In an order pronounced on November 20, Justice Sandeep V. Marne held that Blink Digital had not established a case warranting an interim mandatory injunction for restoration of its accounts. The court observed that there was prima facie material indicating policy-violating advertisements and business practices, which Meta was entitled to consider while taking enforcement action.
The court has also directed Meta to preserve all data, records, content, logs and communications associated with Blink Digital's Business Manager accounts, advertising accounts, Facebook page and Instagram account while its restoration request is pending. However, this protection will cease if the agency does not submit its request within four weeks or if Meta rejects it.
The order came in Interim Application (L) No. 31469 of 2026, filed by Blink Digital in its commercial suit against Facebook India Online Services Private Limited and Meta Platforms Inc. Blink Digital was represented by senior advocate Kunal Dwarkadas, along with counsel from Tuli & Co. Meta Platforms Inc. was represented by senior advocate Janak Dwarkadas and a team from Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas, while Facebook India was represented by advocate Varun Pathak, also from Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas.
What led to the court's decision?
The dispute centres on Meta's decision to disable four Business Manager accounts operated by Blink Digital on August 4, 2026. The accounts — Hustle Neo, Hustle Digital, Hustle AI and Hustle New — collectively administered approximately 9,000 advertising accounts, along with Facebook and Instagram pages and other business assets.
Blink Digital had sought immediate restoration of these accounts, associated permissions and functionalities, besides protection of its Managed Agency Partner status and the benefits attached to it. Alternatively, it had sought directions restraining Meta from deleting or destroying its business data and records, and for the same to be shared with the agency.
The agency argued that Meta had disabled its accounts without adequate prior notice, specific reasons or a reasonable opportunity to contest the action. It also contended that Meta had provided different sets of explanations at various stages of the dispute, including through subsequent legal correspondence and its affidavit before the court.
However, Meta maintained that the enforcement action was necessitated by repeated violations of its Advertising Standards, Community Standards and Account Integrity policies. Its submissions included allegations that Blink Digital had facilitated advertisements involving misleading financial offers, sexually suggestive content, prohibited drugs and online gambling-related claims.
Meta also questioned the agency's own marketing practices, particularly its promotion of advertising accounts as 'ban-free', 'no-ban' and without spending limits, along with claims around rental advertising accounts and access to Meta insiders who could allegedly overturn enforcement decisions.
The court, while examining the material placed by Meta, observed that there was prima facie basis to infer that Blink Digital had marketed its services by promising advertisers an unfair advantage over other businesses and suggesting the availability of workarounds to Meta's enforcement systems.
It also noted that the agency had previously sought guidance from Meta on policies and signals that could trigger account suspensions, while offering to remove non-compliant clients from its network. The court observed that these circumstances weakened Blink Digital's contention that it had the requisite expertise in Meta's advertising policies and compliance requirements.
Court holds agency responsible for compliance across managed accounts
A key aspect of the ruling was the court's examination of the responsibilities of advertising agencies operating through Meta's Business Manager infrastructure.
The court observed that Blink Digital's role extended beyond campaign execution. It managed advertising accounts, reviewed advertisements for apparent compliance, monitored campaigns and acted as an interface between advertisers and Meta. As a Managed Agency Partner, the agency was expected to understand the platform's policies and ensure that advertisements appearing through accounts under its management complied with them.
The court also rejected Blink Digital's contention that Meta's reliance on older instances of alleged violations weakened the justification for the August 2026 action. It held that Meta was entitled to consider the cumulative conduct of the agency, including earlier instances of account suspensions and alleged policy violations, while deciding on enforcement.
On the question of procedural safeguards, Blink Digital had relied on Rule 4(8) of the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021. The provision requires significant social media intermediaries to notify users of the grounds for disabling access, provide a reasonable opportunity to dispute the action and ensure appropriate grievance redressal oversight.
The court held that Meta had substantially complied with these requirements, considering its August 4 communication and the subsequent correspondence between the parties. It further observed that even if there had been a procedural lapse in strict compliance with Rule 4(8), that alone would not establish a sufficiently strong case for an interim mandatory injunction, particularly in view of the material relating to the agency's conduct.
The court also noted that Meta, as a private entity, could not be compelled to provide its advertising infrastructure to an agency as a matter of right. It observed that Blink Digital was not prevented from conducting advertising business on other platforms, including Google, TikTok, Snapchat and Yahoo.
The background: Rs 515 crore revenue and 80-90% dependence on Meta
Blink Digital's case before the court highlighted the commercial implications of losing access to Meta's advertising ecosystem. According to the agency's submissions, its revenue from operations for assessment years 2023-24 and 2024-25 stood at Rs 515 crore, with approximately 80-90% of its revenue generated through advertising on Meta's platforms.
The agency contended that the disabling of its Business Manager accounts had affected its client relationships and employee base. It also argued that the action was disproportionate, given the scale of the business assets and advertiser accounts linked to its infrastructure.
In its main commercial suit, Blink Digital has sought damages of Rs 1,000 crore from Meta, apart from declaratory and injunctive reliefs challenging the account disablement.
Meta, on the other hand, argued that the agency's conduct exposed the platform to potential policy and statutory risks, and that disabling the accounts was necessary to protect its users and advertising ecosystem.
The court's November 20 order disposes of the interim application and does not finally determine the Rs 1,000 crore damages claim or the other substantive issues raised in the commercial suit.
