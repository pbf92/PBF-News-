TITLE: AI Models Achieve Moderate-to-Good Discrimination in Cardiac Arrhythmia Meta-Analysis
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence reveals that artificial intelligence and machine learning models achieve moderate-to-good overall discriminative performance when predicting cardiac arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, yielding a pooled area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.79 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.75 to 0.84. Conducted by an international team led by Faizan Ahmed and Gabriel Benjamen, the study evaluated ten clinical investigations encompassing 80,133 participants across their full cohorts, with eight studies involving 79,396 individuals providing complete discrimination data for quantitative synthesis. The protocol was registered prospectively in PROSPERO under CRD420261437132 and adhered to PRISMA 2020 guidelines. By synthesizing evidence across supraventricular and ventricular electrophysiological targets, the authors assessed whether computational algorithms can reliably anticipate lethal events and post-ablation recurrences across cardiovascular populations.
The clinical drive toward automated prediction stems from persistent diagnostic limitations in electrophysiology, where sudden cardiac death and recurring arrhythmias remain major causes of mortality. Conventional clinical pathways rely heavily on coarse risk scores and resting left ventricular ejection fraction to guide catheter ablation or primary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillators. However, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction incompletely captures complex arrhythmogenic substrates and temporal risk shifts, frequently leaving high-risk patients undertreated while others undergo device implantation without deriving clear therapeutic benefit. Earlier electrophysiological techniques demonstrated that subtle electrical abnormalities could supplement basic clinical variables, including signal-averaged electrocardiograms detecting low-amplitude late potentials from delayed ventricular conduction and microvolt T-wave alternans reflecting repolarization instability. Yet the predictive value of these historical tests varied considerably across ischemic populations due to confounding bundle branch blocks and beta-blocker therapy, prompting modern investigators to determine whether nonlinear machine learning models can better integrate multidimensional patient records.
To evaluate the global evidence base, investigators conducted a systematic search across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and the Cochrane Library covering publications through March 2026 without language or publication restrictions. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings and text terms for atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, and sudden cardiac death with algorithmic keywords including machine learning, deep learning, artificial intelligence, and neural networks, identifying 3,161 total records. Following the removal of 689 duplicate entries using Rayyan software, two independent reviewers screened 2,472 titles and abstracts, excluding 2,340 reports. Full-text evaluation of the remaining 132 reports led to the exclusion of 122 articles due to missing clinical outcomes, index model mismatches, non-target populations, or insufficient abstract data. Ultimately, ten studies met the defined Population, Index Model, Comparator, and Outcomes criteria, spanning single-center observational cohorts, multicenter registries, and intensive care datasets ranging from 136 to 46,266 patients.
Methodological quality and risk of bias across the ten included studies were independently evaluated by two reviewers using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool across four standardized domains. All ten investigations demonstrated low risk of bias in the participants domain, while eight showed low risk in predictor definitions and nine exhibited low risk in outcome assessment. Consequently, the overall risk of bias was rated high in 80 percent of the reviewed literature, with only two studies, authored by Rosman and Kolk, achieving an overall low risk of bias. Applicability concerns remained low across most participant and predictor domains, though two studies demonstrated high applicability concerns regarding outcome definitions within specific anticoagulated or device-monitored cohorts.
Quantitative meta-analysis was performed using R version 4.5.2, pooling discriminative metrics through an inverse-variance random-effects model using the DerSimonian and Laird method. While the pooled area under the curve of 0.79 demonstrated respectable overall discrimination, the analysis identified substantial between-study heterogeneity, with an I-squared of 81.5 percent, a tau-squared of 0.0030, and a statistically significant Cochran’s Q test. Furthermore, the calculated 95 percent prediction interval ranged from 0.65 to 0.93, indicating that model performance in future clinical settings could vary considerably. To confirm that no single study disproportionately drove the summary estimate, investigators conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses by sequentially omitting each individual cohort. The pooled area under the curve remained stable between 0.78 and 0.81, with I-squared ranging from 73.2 percent to 83.8 percent, while Baujat plot diagnostics confirmed that an ablation study by Saglietto contributed the largest individual share of heterogeneity.
A prespecified subgroup analysis compared models developed for post-ablation atrial fibrillation recurrence against those predicting sudden cardiac death or malignant ventricular events. Four investigations focusing on atrial fibrillation yielded a pooled area under the curve of 0.79 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.72 to 0.86, a prediction interval of 0.55 to 1.02, and substantial heterogeneity of 87.7 percent. Similarly, four studies targeting ventricular arrhythmias and sudden death produced a pooled estimate of 0.80 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.74 to 0.86, a prediction interval of 0.60 to 0.99, and heterogeneity of 77.1 percent. Although a formal test for subgroup differences revealed no statistically significant divergence between the two electrophysiological categories (chi-square 0.05, p-value 0.82), the authors emphasized that statistical non-significance does not establish equivalent predictive performance, given notable disparities in patient populations, prediction horizons, and model architectures.
Across individual studies predicting supraventricular outcomes, model discrimination ranged from 0.72 to 0.85 depending on input modalities and cohort design. Alomari and colleagues achieved an area under the curve of 0.850 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.811 to 0.889 using a CatBoost algorithm trained on 47 physiological, laboratory, and intensive care variables to predict new-onset atrial fibrillation within 48 hours among 46,266 critically ill patients. In a post-ablation setting, Jiang and coworkers utilized a convolutional neural network analyzing preprocedural twelve-lead electrocardiograms from 1,618 pulmonary vein isolation patients, achieving an area under the curve of 0.840 and showing that algorithmic recurrence predictions carried a hazard ratio of 5.97. Conversely, Saglietto and colleagues trained random forests on clinical and echocardiographic variables across 3,128 patients, yielding an area under the curve of 0.721, while Liu and collaborators used biomarker profiles in a nomogram among 136 ablation patients, reporting an area under the curve of 0.725.
Investigations predicting sudden cardiac death and malignant ventricular tachyarrhythmias yielded individual discrimination values ranging from 0.74 to 0.88. Au-Yeung and associates achieved the highest single discrimination metric in the meta-analysis, reporting an area under the curve of 0.880 for ten-second warning windows and approximately 0.81 for five-minute windows using random forests and support vector machines trained on intracardiac heart rate variability signals from 788 defibrillator patients. Rosman and colleagues applied an RF-SLAM algorithm incorporating time-varying implantable defibrillator data across 13,440 patients, securing an external validation area under the curve of 0.81 for a composite outcome of death and heart failure hospitalization. In contrast, Kolk and coauthors developed a dynamic machine learning framework incorporating longitudinal six-lead electrocardiograms and variational autoencoders across 2,942 device recipients, achieving an area under the curve of 0.738, while Bernardini and coworkers applied gradient boosting to 11,078 anticoagulated registry patients, obtaining an area under the curve of 0.745 for cardiovascular death.
Two identified publications satisfied all systematic eligibility criteria but were evaluated narratively because their discrimination metrics could not be pooled quantitatively. Corianò and colleagues formulated a deep learning survival architecture termed DARP-D integrating late gadolinium enhancement cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and clinical covariates among 154 patients with non-ischemic dilated cardiomyopathy, reporting a graphical two-year area under the curve of 0.84 across eight years of follow-up without variance estimates. Furui and coworkers evaluated the CAAP-AF scoring tool through Classification and Regression Trees among 583 catheter ablation recipients, stratifying individuals into low, intermediate, and high risk categories across 1.8 years of follow-up. Additionally, the meta-analysis authors refrained from interpreting an exploratory Egger’s linear regression test for publication bias, explaining that because only eight studies contributed to quantitative pooling, the resulting p-value of 0.66 lacked statistical reliability under established methodological criteria.
Despite the favorable pooled discriminatory capacity of 0.79, the authors emphasized that mathematical discrimination alone cannot establish clinical utility or justify immediate healthcare deployment. Model calibration metrics—such as calibration slopes, calibration-in-the-large, and decision curve analysis—were reported inconsistently across the included studies, though isolated investigations including Saglietto’s recalibrated model, Liu’s decision curves showing net benefit between 18 and 80 percent, and Rosman’s external calibration slope of 1.00 showed encouraging performance. Furthermore, the predominance of retrospective observational data, high analysis bias, limited algorithmic interpretability, and lack of external validation across diverse healthcare systems represent substantial implementation barriers. Moving forward, the research team recommended that investigators prioritize prospective multicenter validation trials with standardized endpoints, transparent calibration reporting, and federated learning architectures. Only through well-designed randomized controlled trials directly testing algorithm-guided interventions against standard clinical care can electrophysiologists determine whether computational risk prediction truly reduces sudden death and improves long-term patient outcomes.
