TITLE: Vermont bans feeds built on kids' data from January 1, 2027
OUTLET: PPC Land

Vermont's attorney general has set the final terms for how apps, games and websites must treat children in the state. Two rules, one on data and design and one on age checks, take effect on January 1, 2027, the same day as the Age-Appropriate Design Code Act they carry out. The final versions are dated October 19, 2026, the day Attorney General Charity R. Clark certified them.
In Short
Vermont has finished two rules under its children's online design law: one limits how apps use kids' data and design features to keep them scrolling, the other sets privacy rules for age checks. From January 1, 2027, businesses whose services minors are likely to use cannot rank a child's feed from clicks, viewing history or guessed interests unless the child asks, and any age check must delete the data it collects right away, except an age range. Compared with the July draft, autoplay, endless feeds, alerts and rewards are no longer banned outright but judged by whether they drive compulsive use. The state's rulemaking page still labels the documents "final proposed rules," while law firm Mayer Brown says the attorney general has adopted them.
Two rules, one start date
The Vermont Legislature passed Act 63, "An Act Relating to an Age-Appropriate Design Code," in May 2025. The law sits in Title 9 of the Vermont Statutes, at sections 2449a onward, and it left two jobs to the attorney general's office. According to the office's rulemaking page, "The law and any adopted rules take effect on January 1, 2027."
The first job, under section 2449f(b), was a rule that would, in the statute's words quoted on that page, "[P]rohibit[] data processing or design practices of a covered business that, in the opinion of the Attorney General, lead to compulsive use or subvert or impair user autonomy, decision making, or choice during the use of an online service, product, or feature of the covered business." The second, under section 2449g(b), was to identify "commercially reasonable and technically feasible methods for covered businesses and processors to determine if a user is a covered minor," set out "appropriate review processes for users appealing their age designations," and add privacy protections for age-check data.
The legislature also tied the office's hands on one point. In writing the age-check rule, it had to "Prioritize user privacy and accessibility over the accuracy of age assurance methods." That instruction runs through the final text.
The two rules carry state numbers 26P027, "Prohibited Data and Design Practices," and 26P028, "Age Assurance Privacy." Age assurance is the law's term for working out whether a user is a child or teen. The hearing slides put it more simply: "In plain language: it is an age check."
What the law already required
The rules sit on top of duties the statute already imposes. According to the attorney general's office, the act requires covered businesses to set the default privacy settings of their products "to the highest level of privacy when used by a minor." It "prohibits the collection or sharing of a minor's personal data unless it's necessary to provide a certain service to the minor." It also limits a business's ability to let someone "monitor the activity or location of a minor on its digital product without providing a conspicuous signal to the minor."
According to Mayer Brown, the statute separately requires push notifications to minors to be switched off by default and bars sending them between midnight and 6:00 a.m. The firm says a violation counts as an unfair and deceptive act in commerce, enforced by the attorney general, and that the law gives no private right to sue. The statute's test for whether a service is reasonably likely to be used by minors includes a 2% audience threshold, which, according to Mayer Brown, the final rules leave untouched.
Final, adopted or final proposed?
The status of the rules depends on which document is read. The attorney general's page lists "Redlined and clean versions of the final proposed rules," four Word files dated October 19, 2026. The Secretary of State's rules listing for 26P027 still gives its status as proposed. A November 3 memo from the legislature's rules committee, the Legislative Committee on Administrative Rules, acknowledged receipt of the final proposed rules on October 19 and tentatively set the committee's review for its November 19, 2026 meeting.
Mayer Brown, in a note published on November 25 by partners Larissa Lee and Amber C. Thomson and associate Alex Akre, treats the matter as settled. "The Vermont Attorney General has published two final rules under the Vermont Age-Appropriate Design Code Act," the note says. "These two Final Rules are a design and data rule, and an age-assurance rule. Both take effect January 1, 2027, the same day as the statute." The firm says the "final proposed" label reflects the procedural stage and that the attorney general has adopted the rules.
The design rule: feeds, autoplay and alerts
The design rule's strictest line concerns what a child sees next. According to Mayer Brown, a covered business may not use personal data, behavioral data or inferred preferences to select, recommend, rank, order, prioritize or sequence media for a covered minor. Behavioral data, in the firm's reading, covers clicks, scrolls, time spent looking at something, viewing history and navigation patterns. Three exceptions remain: a minor's express and unambiguous request, privacy or accessibility settings the user picks, and search queries.
For platforms built on recommendation engines, that is the provision with real reach. On Mayer Brown's reading, any service that orders videos, posts or products by past behavior would need another way to order them for users identified as covered minors. The firm describes personalized feeds as the strictest provision left in the rule, while the rest became more flexible than the July draft.
Everything else in the rule turns on two tests. A practice breaches the rule on compulsive use if it causes or materially contributes to repetitive use that materially disrupts one or more major life activities. It breaches the rule on autonomy if it materially steers, pressures, manipulates, obstructs or adds lopsided friction that meaningfully impairs a minor's choices. Factors include session length, habitual use, engagement-boosting techniques and A/B testing, and according to the firm no single factor settles the question.
One detail will matter to any team that runs experiments. Mayer Brown says internal testing, analytics and metrics may be used as evidence that a harm was foreseeable, but that the rule creates no duty to test, assess risk or keep records. In other words, the engagement data a company gathers to improve retention could be used as evidence against it.
The attorney general's filing to lawmakers says the office removed the proposed independent assessment, testing and documentation requirements from the final design rule. It also says the office lacks the information to estimate statewide dollar costs for businesses.
The age-check rule: delete everything but the age range
The age-check rule starts from a narrower premise than the draft. According to the attorney general's October 19 letter to the Secretary of State and the rules committee, signed by Assistant Attorney General Sarah L. Jameson, the rule does not require a particular identity-based method or a universal age check. A business may treat a user as a covered minor rather than collect more data, where that allows it to comply with the statute.
How the final age-check rule works
According to the attorney general's letter and Mayer Brown's summary, the final rule describes the process in seven steps:
- A covered business decides whether an age check is reasonably necessary to meet its obligations. If not, none is required, and a business may treat all users as covered minors and collect no age-check data at all.
- It chooses a method that is commercially reasonable and technically feasible. No method is required or preferred, and the methods listed in the rule are not a ranking. The business weighs how sensitive the data is, accessibility, less intrusive alternatives and how confident it needs to be, giving priority to privacy and accessibility over marginal gains in accuracy. If its choice materially raises privacy risk, it must document why.
- It collects only the data strictly necessary for the check and deletes it immediately afterwards, keeping only the age range the statute permits. The rule adds no exception for auditing, fraud prevention, security or compliance.
- It does not repeat checks more often than reasonably necessary and keeps linking of age data across services to a minimum.
- It offers an accessible alternative method where one is available. An identity document is not required as a universal fallback.
- It gives users a way to contest an age decision that materially affects access. Automated or alternative methods may handle the appeal, and meaningful human review applies where they cannot reasonably resolve the dispute.
- It stays responsible for vendors that run checks on its behalf, takes reasonable steps to stop them keeping, reusing, sharing or combining the data, and keeps documentation proportionate to the method's risks. A missing document does not by itself establish a violation.
The rule's section headings, as listed in the attorney general's letter, are: Applicability; Definitions; Age Assurance Selection; Graduated Escalation; Accuracy and Error Evaluation; Frequency and Reassessment; Data Minimization in Age Assurance; Linkability and System Design; Prohibited Uses, Retention, and Disclosure of Age Assurance Data; Fallback and Accessibility; Appeals Process; Compliance and Oversight; Illustrative Examples; and Severability.
According to the letter, the office reworked the accuracy provisions to address both false negatives and false positives while keeping privacy and accessibility ahead of marginal accuracy gains. According to Mayer Brown, the rule sets no numerical accuracy threshold. On deletion, the letter says collected personal data must go immediately after the determination, except for the age range, and that the office removed or narrowed language that could have allowed longer retention for audits, fraud prevention, security or compliance.
From the July draft to the final text
The slides used at the three hearings, posted in a file dated September 24, 2026, describe the July proposals in plain terms. Placed next to the final filing, they show where the office moved.
The slides were careful to frame the July drafts as a work in progress. "The rules are not final," one slide said. "Public comments can help us improve them before adoption." Another read: "The proposed rule text controls. This presentation is only a summary."
Why not simply ask everyone for ID? The deck posed that question itself. "Asking all users for identity documents can collect sensitive information and may discourage lawful anonymous use," it said. On design, it drew a line between enjoyment and pressure: "The concern is not that a young person enjoys an app. The concern is design that keeps pushing continued use when the young person did not clearly ask for it." It listed six examples people may recognize: endless scrolling, autoplay, streaks or rewards, nighttime alerts, hard-to-find exits and social pressure through likes and reactions. And it added a caution of its own: "A design choice does not violate the rule just because a young person uses a service more."
Mayer Brown had written on September 11 that the draft would force a redesign of how online services present content to minors, reaching well beyond social media companies. By November 25, the same firm said both final rules give businesses more room than the proposals did.
