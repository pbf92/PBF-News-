TITLE: GTA VI Does What Most Video Games With Choice And Morality Systems Don't
OUTLET: Aftermath

I woke up this morning, brushed my teeth, and balked at the dark circles under my eyes after another Grand Theft Auto VI gaming session that ended within striking distance of 1 a.m. What kept pulling me back to the game everyone and their mother hyped to high heaven as either the industry killer 3000 or the Final Video Game wasn't its high-fidelity Floridian graphics, nor was it playing dress-up with Jason and Lucia. GTA VI’s morality system kept pulling me back in for one more hour to see what spur-of-the-moment choices I'd make.
Unlike past GTA games, where you're essentially a one-person army mowing down anyone between you and a bigger payday, GTA VI introduces morality into its otherwise polished open-world formula. Through the criminal profile system, the game tracks whether you have Lucia and Jason solve problems with restraint or wanton bloodshed. It does so with five color coded devil-face emojis, categorizing your level of violence across five tiers: Professional, Hardened, Violent, Psycho, and Irredeemable.
These five tiers are shaped by how often you choose lethal force while gallivanting about Leonida as its Florida-coded Bonnie and Clyde. Engage in firefights by disarming enemies with stealth takedowns, the concussive force of punches and kicks, or carefully disabling them with nonlethal shots (with the occasional headshot because pobody's nerfect), and you'll land somewhere in the Professional or Hardened tiers. Go into every encounter John Wick sicko mode-style, popping headshots like it's Sniper Elite on everyone in sight and bludgeoning NPC's indiscriminately for minor inconveniences, and your profile will inch towards Violent, Psycho, or Irredeemable. On its face, GTA VI's criminal profiles are a surprisingly austere system from the folks who brought us six-star wanted levels and all the chaos that entails. It's also the gameplay system that made the way I play feel just as important as the spectacle of the early story missions themselves.
There's no real penalty for playing one way or the other, aside from reaching Irredeemable, which prevents you from surrendering to police. Otherwise, the game "rewards" whichever approach you prefer as Jason and Lucia with perks. Mechanically, many of those perks are tailored to your playstyle, like the expanded time slowing when you turn on “focus” mode, which benefits players who prefer a less trigger-happy approach and need more time to aim for kneecaps rather than heads.
I've always had a tricky relationship with the way morality systems in video games manifest. Put simply, they've rarely hit for me. Like its close cousin, romance dialogue trees, moral dilemmas in video games are often presented less like chewing on a genuine dilemma and more like the game massaging your jaws toward a "correct” answer. They never land for me because they're presented like a fish flopping on the floor for me to pick up or ignore.
A lot of the time video game morality systems feel less like a real judgement on the character’s morality, and more like an instruction manual on how to play the game the “best” way. Whether it's "bae over bay" binaries like kissing Chloe Price from Life Is Strange for the umpteenth time, or punching tabloid reporters as an otherwise mild-mannered Commander Shepard in Mass Effect, the choice might be fun in the moment, but it's rarely framed as a binary decision with an obvious reward attached, which is what these choices all boil down to. I can practically see the approval points floating behind one option as a pulsating heart emoji or a bright-red Renegade meter filling up behind the other like demerits to a report card I'll have to reconcile with later. The key difference with GTA VI’s criminal profiles is that decisions stop being about impediments towards a particular playstyle and start being about my behavior as a player.
So many other morality systems end up feeling less like active decision-making and more like Sunday school. You learn which answers make the congregation happy when you raise your hand and are called upon, which answers draw disapproving looks, and you move accordingly. Rather than expressing a consistent personality, I'm studying a checklist of what every companion likes and reshaping myself to fit into their good graces. Choices become exercises in identifying right and wrong rather than opportunities to roleplay someone nuanced: someone who can be compassionate, petty, principled, and conniving, on a case-by-case basis.
What’s made GTA VI’s criminal profiles click in a way that so few morality systems ever have is the way it encourages me to roleplay. There are still scripted story beats and major decisions, sure, but what fascinates me is everything in between. The downtime. The minutiae. Instead of beelining it to the next heist, I'm consciously building a routine for my characters. I'm thinking about what they eat for breakfast (for Jason: a steady diet of apples and coffee; Lucia, protein bars and mineral water), whether they worked out that morning (Jason's a gym rat and Lucia's an as I go about my day kinda girl), how they spend their evenings (Jason doomscrolling as passenger princess on the way to a hit but Lucia prefers to browse social media while Jason's already fast asleep in bed), and how they wind down after a long day (they fucking). Somewhere along the way, I stopped merely playing GTA VI and started living in it.
In my head, Jason is a guy deeply scarred by whatever happened during his military service. He doesn't want to kill people unless he absolutely has to. Whenever possible, he shoots to wound, goes for nonlethal takedowns, and tries to keep situations under control before they spiral. Because of that, one of the game's early shootouts hit me harder than I expected. During a drug deal gone bad, I accidentally killed a man as he rounded the corner toward his getaway boat on some movie shit. Whether the moment was scripted or the result of my own panicked trigger pull didn't matter. It felt like a failure of Jason's personal code. When he later brushed it off as "handled," the way his Buck Strickland-ass boss probably would've wanted, it landed less like a victory and more like an aged Batman Beyond-era Bruce Wayne staring at a gun in horror and realizing he'd crossed a line worth hanging up the cowl for good.
Lucia, meanwhile, has developed into a very different protagonist in my playthrough. She doesn't want to hurt people, but she's far more willing to if circumstances or her thin patience demand it. While Jason sticks to his healthy routines and tries to keep his conscience clean, Lucia is constantly stressed, broke, and scrambling for opportunities to resurface and catch a fucking breath from all the bullshit life's served her. She doesn't always have the luxury of being an altruistic criminal. Sometimes the quickest way out of a bad situation is a bullet to the head. Not because she's cruel, but because she's exhausted, and survival takes precedence over idealism.
Funnily enough, some of my favorite role-playing moments have come from situations the game never explicitly told me how to feel about. After finally saving enough money to buy Lucia a car that didn't fishtail every time I touched the gas pedal, some road-raging idiot rear-ended me and shoved me off the road. After a long day of failed deals and mounting frustration, I snapped. I chased the guy down, dragged him out of his car, and curb-stomped him. Not to kill him. Just to let him know he fucked with the wrong one today. Lucia slipped into the Hardened criminal tier while Jason remained a squeaky-clean Professional. And honestly, I'm not mad at it. At least, not to the point where I feel inclined to save scum like other games of this ilk when I feel like I fucked up on a "perfect" run, moral or otherwise. This feels in character for how I play these Jason and Lucia, and not because I selected different morality options from a dialogue wheel, but because those decisions felt authentic to each character in the heat of the moment.
That's the magic of GTA VI's morality system. It feels tailored to me rather than forcing me to tailor myself to it. The game watches how I naturally play, then reflects that behavior back at me through the way Lucia and Jason carry themselves, react to situations, and relate to one another. Whenever I'm in a bind as either character, they won't go off the handle and slit a person's throat in cold blood as recompense like other players going ham in their respective playthroughs. They'll just give them a well-placed V-Trigger or Superman punch to give myself space to collect myself, escalate matters, or thank them for handling it without giving us more problems to deal with down the line.
GTA VI's criminal profile transformed how I engage with "morality." It’s less like a progress report running on mood ring logic; It's a system I indulge in because I'm curious what kind of people my Jason and Lucia will become under pressure. If anything, its existence feels like the dream come true of what morality and decision making can look like in video games. I’m not playing to try to unlock the correct series of actions to give me a “good ending.” I'm making decisions because they feel right for the people I've imagined Jason and Lucia to be.
