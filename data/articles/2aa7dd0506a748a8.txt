TITLE: Why Is Rockstar So Into Guys Building Houses?
OUTLET: The Punished Backlog

I am playing Grand Theft Auto VI, my first ever GTA game. There are many things that have not been surprising to me in its first 10 hours, but I was shocked by how familiar one quest was to developer Rockstar Games’ last title, Red Dead Redemption 2 from 2018.
Slight spoilers for the epilogue of Red Dead Redemption 2 and the first hours of Grand Theft Auto VI.
At the end of Red Dead Redemption 2 (a game I loved, especially for Sadie Adler), players take on John Marston for the first time. While Marston is the hero of Red Dead Redemption, he’s kind of a messy jerk in the prequel who can’t do anything right. He’s constantly prioritizing get-rich-quick schemes over his wife and son. However, in the game’s epilogue, he’s decided to go straight for his family and he builds a log cabin from a kit.
I loved that sequence. Music begins. Players can participate in a rhythm mini-game of nailing planks into the roof.1 It’s a surreal, kind of meditative experience as John works with others to start a new life. Building the house felt very notably different than shooting people on crime sprees. The house felt orderly, focused, intentional.
Similarly, I didn’t expect my opening hours of Grand Theft Auto VI as Jason Duval, a low-level drug smuggler, to be spent painstakingly fixing up his house. Once again, I was putting a roof together, though this time with a nail gun and roof tiles. The effort to paint a wall was so detailed in its rendering that I got tired thinking of my own house painting. Seeing this familiar scene made me wonder: Why is Rockstar so into this?
Do they think they’re being super avant-garde by showing that these troubled criminal men also just want to build a house? Are they trying to surprise us in gameplay, pulling the rug out from under players expecting to have Bonnie & Cycle evenings but instead just doing home improvement projects? Or is it something deeper — do the developers simply admire men who build houses?
Don’t get me wrong — I liked what the house building does for pacing in both Red Dead Redemption 2 and in the beginning of Grand Theft Auto VI. I like even more what it does for story. By making these men (and, thus, the player) responsible for building their own homes, we are rooted in their sense of place. (I loved what Corvo Bianco did for Geralt in The Witcher 3: The Wild Hunt – Blood & Wine.)
But it was also just funny to me that every time my husband came by the room (he is not interested in playing GTA VI), I wasn’t fighting a crocodile or chatting with a hot girl or holding someone up at gunpoint for their drug money. Instead, I was putting up drywall or getting new lumber for my porch railing.
As of the time of writing, I just got to the part in Chapter 1 in which Jason and his friend Cal very endearingly try to rearrange the furniture in the house to have more “feng shui.” Even though they spend a lot of time minutely moving the couch, they put the TV in front of the ocean view (bad) and don’t hang up framed prints on the empty walls (also bad). It’s very well-represented boy logic. I’m honestly amazed that Jason has a bed frame and not just a mattress on the floor.
This quest works for a few reasons. One, it is a kind of sweet fantasy — setting up your house with your friend and then sitting down to play video games together with a beer and pizza. Secondly, fixing up the house in GTA makes Jason’s girlfriend Lucia happier (I too would be mad if my boyfriend told me to come hang out and the roof was leaking). But I still can’t get over how familiar those beats were — watching a male protagonist hammer nails into his roof.
Do all Rockstar games have guys building houses? Or is this a recent phenomenon? Does this tie into the millennial pain that most of us feel we will never get to own property or homes? Is this the same animation as Red Dead Redemption 2, or did they code completely new stuff? Did they watch it side-by-side? Did they actually forget that they had already done this? Or did they definitely not forget and they felt like it was an intentional, cool updated comparison and a commentary about how men — across time and space and cultural differences — are just trying to find a home? Is this about the societal pressure that men feel to provide a house for women in order to be taken seriously? Did anyone else notice this similarity or am I thinking way too much about this?
I’m mostly being facetious (and I definitely am thinking too much about this), but if you do have the answer to any of these questions, please tell me.
