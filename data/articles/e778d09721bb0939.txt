Prehospital Antibiotics fail to Reduce Mortality in Suspected Sepsis: JAMA
A clinical trial found that administering antibiotics to patients with suspected sepsis during ambulance transport did not reduce in-hospital mortality compared with placebo. Prehospital antibiotic administration offered no significant survival benefit, suggesting that giving antibiotics before hospital arrival may not improve mortality outcomes in patients with suspected sepsis. The study was published in JAMA by Damon C. and colleagues.
To investigate the clinical impact of early antibiotic administration in the ambulance versus standard emergency department care for sepsis, a multi-center, 2 x 2 factorial, randomized, placebo-controlled trial was carried out involving four paramedic services and their respective receiving hospitals in Ontario, Canada. The recruitment period took place between March 23, 2020, and August 20, 2025, with the last follow up performed in December 2025.
Adult participants suspected of having sepsis based on clinical judgment and characterized as suspected infection with fever > 38.0 °C and hypotension (<100 mm Hg) were enrolled into this study and randomized to receive either 1 g of intramuscular ceftriaxone or an identical looking placebo (in addition to factorial randomization to liberal vs. usual prehospital intravenous fluids) while in the ambulance. All patients in this trial could be eligible for receiving antibiotics in the emergency department in an open label fashion.
The primary outcome for this study included the hospital mortality, defined as mortality before hospital discharge through day 90, while there were 17 secondary endpoints, including 90-day all-cause mortality, hospitalization, intensive care unit admission, mechanical ventilation, dialysis, positive cultures and inpatient antibiotic administration in less than 24 hours.
Key findings:
- Statistical evaluation among 2,091 randomized patients, of whom 2,048 were eligible for primary analysis (1,026 randomized to ceftriaxone and 1,022 randomized to placebo. Median age 75 years [IQR, 62–85]; 1,162 males [56.7%]), confirmed equivalent mortality and clinical trajectories across treatment arms.
- Mean time from paramedic arrival at the patient to study drug administration was 37 ± 36 minutes in the ceftriaxone group and 39 ± 55 minutes in the placebo group.
- Subsequent open-label in-hospital antibiotics were administered to 735 of 795 patients (92.5%) in the ceftriaxone arm and 730 of 792 patients (92.2%) in the placebo arm at a mean of 247.3 ± 207.7 minutes and 252.8 ± 218.0 minutes after paramedic arrival, respectively.
- The primary endpoint of hospital mortality prior to discharge through day 90 occurred in 182 of 1,009 evaluable patients (18.0%) in the ceftriaxone group compared with 186 of 1,011 patients (18.4%) in the placebo group, showing no statistically significant difference (risk ratio, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.81–1.17; P = 0.77).
- Across all 17 prespecified secondary clinical, microbiological, and resource-utilization endpoints, including 90-day all-cause mortality, intensive care unit admission, mechanical ventilation, dialysis requirement, and positive culture rates, outcomes remained comparable between the prehospital ceftriaxone and placebo groups.
In summation, multicenter randomized placebo-controlled trial data collected for over 2,000 subjects in the province of Ontario demonstrates that treatment with intramuscular ceftriaxone in the ambulance prior to arrival does not reduce hospital mortality rate in comparison with regular antibiotics therapy conducted in the ED. It is important to state that according to the conclusions drawn from the data analyzed, prehospital antibiotic administration has no survival benefit over ED sepsis management.
Reference:
Scales DC, Rogowsky A, Seymour CW, et al. Prehospital Antibiotics for Patients With Suspected Sepsis: The PITSTOP-Antibiotics Factorial Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. Published online November 24, 2026. doi:10.1001/jama.2026.25627
