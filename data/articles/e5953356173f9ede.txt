TITLE: Hit Worship Songwriters Want More Living Room, Less Arena
OUTLET: Christianity Today

Brett Younker, a longtime staff member at Passion City Church and its worship collective, Passion Music, in Atlanta, has a keen sense of what helps a song move a congregation.
Passion helped popularize the music of Chris Tomlin, Matt Redman, David Crowder, and Charlie Hall, and it is one of the four chief producers of the most-used worship music in North American churches alongside Elevation, Bethel, and Hillsong. Passion’s upcoming January 2027 gatherings will mark the 30th year of a conference that draws tens of thousands of young adults every year.
Younker will be one of the worship leaders on stage at the Passion 2027 conferences. But when he set out to make a solo album in 2023, he wanted to produce something that didn’t sound like it was recorded in, or for, a stadium. He wanted to make something a little quieter.
“On a lot of the live worship albums these days, everything is at a ten,” Younker said. “We slam the songs so full of sound that there’s no room for the singing.”
His album, Keep the Faith, showcases what Younker calls the “songwriter’s versions” of popular worship songs like “No Body” and “Fall Like Rain.” It’s a studio recording intended to highlight melodies and lyrics, and to demonstrate that the songs don’t require a crowd of thousands, a lighting rig, and a ten-piece band.
The first Passion conference, held in Fort Worth, Texas, in 1997, drew around 2,000 students. By 2000, the event had grown to over 40,000. In January 2026, over 50,000 students packed Globe Life Field in Arlington, Texas.
The Passion conferences, alongside megachurches like Elevation, have changed many Christians’ perceptions of what it means for a worship song to “work.” They have also changed the job of “worship leader,” logistically and aesthetically.
Even artists who lead worship at events like Passion recognize that a song can get a vocal and emotional response from a crowd in an arena but fall flat in a church of 200.
Paul Baloche, who wrote “Open the Eyes of My Heart,” has been an influential figure in the development of worship music since the late 1990s, with other hits like “Above All” in subsequent decades.
“It’s okay to be inspired by the big events,” said Baloche, pointing out that songs like Tomlin’s 2023 hit “Holy Forever” are clearly resonating with both small congregations and megachurches.
But Baloche worries that the link between high-production performances and congregational worship has become too strong, and that it’s changing the way church communities sing, or don’t sing, together.
“When we lead worship, we have to create moments that break down the wall between ‘us’ on the stage and ‘them’ in the congregation,” Baloche said.
The veteran worship leader jokingly refers to worship sets with layers of synths and prerecorded backing tracks as “church karaoke.” He knows many Christians who are ready to move away from the model created by and for arena worship.
“There are plenty of people who want to walk into a church and see just a guitar, a piano, maybe a few vocalists,” he said. “And we need leaders who are reminding people that this is not American Idol, this is not The Voice,this is a big living room.”
Baloche and Younker want to help worship leaders identify songs that can be stripped down and used for congregational worship with minimal instrumentation and production. Younker’s album offers worship leaders ideas for turning down the volume and production level on a song they have always heard performed “at a ten,” he said.
Neither artist is advocating for a shift to modern hymns or a return to an older model of worship. Both regularly lead worship in megachurches and at conferences. Music made with those settings in mind canserve the local church, they believe.
Younker understands the impulse to bring back hymns, but he sees a lot of value in the relatability of contemporary music. He wants worship leaders to find balance.
“I don’t want every song to sound like a seminary paper,” he said. “I need some that feel prayerful and devotional.”
He points to his song “The Lord Will Provide” as an example of an anthem that is personal and singable.
“That song worked in the stadium with 50,000 people, and it works with just a guitar,” Younker said.
Many hit worship songs are popularized by leaders with powerful tenor voices, which can make them difficult for laypeople to sing. It’s also difficult for a vocalist to tone down the intensity of a song for a more intimate setting when it is keyed in a range that requires all-out vocal belting.
For Keep the Faith, Younker recorded some of the tracks in a lower key than the original version. But adjustments to key and instrumentation will only do so much.
“A culture of singing comes down to leadership,” he said.
Britton Wesson has seen these music industry trends play out as he grew up leading worship.
At 15, he started leading worship in his Southern Baptist church in Arkansas. When he became a full-time worship pastor in 2007, Passion and Hillsong were shaping the sound of worship in his church. Looking back, he appreciates that era of music but thinks that many leaders like him learned some of the wrong lessons about shepherding a singing congregation.
As more leaders began aspiring to the conference model, they started planning Sunday morning music as cohesive sets, Wesson noted. They stopped teaching, praying, and reading Scripture between songs to avoid interrupting the flow.
“My generation learned not to do much talking on stage. We just let the music do the talking,” said Wesson.
This practice helped create seamless, concertlike musical experiences, but sometimes at the expense of a pastoral voice. Baloche suspects that many leaders avoid teaching or speaking between songs because it feels risky if they don’t have practice doing it. It’s easier for some musicians to stand on a stage and play an uninterrupted set.
“It takes time and effort to peel away your insecurity and learn to be your authentic self in front of a small group of people,” Baloche said.
In his view, that’s a problem for ministry. Lack of communication between the leader and the congregation reinforces the division between the two and adds artifice where there should be a relationship.
“You have to care about the people first,” Baloche said. “We need to help leaders cultivate a pastoral posture.”
Baloche says local leaders can do a few simple things to shape worship services that push against the arena worship model—including experimenting with minimally amplified services that bring down the volume and intensity. For churches used to turning up the speakers and turning down the lights each week, it might feel uncomfortable at first. But he believes it’s worth it.
“Have the courage to go unplugged one Sunday a month,” Baloche suggested. “It’s beautiful to hear people sing. We’re just singing out our prayers together. That’s what it should feel like.”
