TITLE: El Roi / The God Who Sees
OUTLET: Christianity Today

Nothing has shaped my apprenticeship to Jesus quite like waiting. Decades of unwanted singleness have perhaps shaped me more than anything else. I have found that waiting, along with suffering and silence, is among God’s most effective means in forming Christlikeness. All three persistently surface some of life’s biggest questions: Does God see me? Is he out to do me good? Do my desires matter to him?
Many days, I remember wrestling with those questions in quiet despair. The silence echoed. Yet somehow, in those barren moments, a hushed reminder came: Hagar knew these questions intimately. Though our stories were worlds apart, God’s name and gaze also found me.
Abandoned and betrayed, Abraham’s concubine caught sight of the living God, El Roi: “‘I have now seen the One who sees me’” (Gen. 16:13). The only person in the Scriptures to name God, she didn’t just proclaim “he sees” but “he sees me.” The mutuality of that gaze is astonishingly intimate.
Most eye-to-eye encounters recorded in Scripture detail the creature on their face before the Lord. The Creator shielded Moses with his hand as the prophet set eyes on God’s back (Exod. 33:22). The disciples famously fell on their faces in utter overwhelm (Matt. 17:6). Paul was blind for three days following his encounter with Jesus (Acts 9:9). And the apostle John collapsed as dead in Revelation (1:17). On each occasion, eyes were averted or covered.
But not Hagar’s. “‘No one may see me and live,’” God himself says in Exodus 33:20. And yet he seems to have made an exception for the refugee woman. She seems to behold him beholding her. “If we live in beholding, we continually live in a new creation,” says author Maggie Ross.
The God who sees Hagar, and me, invites resurrection life, particularly in seasons of waiting. Each Advent, we join the church universal in remembering the liminal, or threshold, space between the first and second comings of Christ.
We ache and we groan. And God welcomes us to practice again the gift and grit of waiting. Each year, we’re mercifully invited to ask the big questions and occupy the generous space that is necessary to wrestle them to the ground again.
In Genesis 32, Jacob took God up on the struggle to be reborn and renamed. He, like Hagar, beheld God, was brought to silence, and emerged a limping, albeit new, creation. Jacob renamed the land Peniel “‘because I saw God face to face’” (v. 30).
When waiting feels stagnant, we can be assured that meeting God’s gaze always creates something new. Like the work of seeds and yeast, slowly but surely, in the dark and hidden places where we feel unseen and unknown, Holy Saturday always births Resurrection Sunday.
There it is again, the gift and grit of waiting. Advent silence invites us to die and become new, even expanded to hold more hope. Find yourself in the waiting story today.
Behold him as he beholds you.
