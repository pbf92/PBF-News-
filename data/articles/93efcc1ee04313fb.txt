TITLE: Systematic Review Identifies Severe Secondary Immune Deficits Following Targeted Therapies
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A newly published systematic review indicates that secondary immunodeficiency represents an underrecognized clinical challenge characterized by complex therapy-driven risks and widespread diagnostic inconsistencies. Evaluating evidence across multiple medical disciplines, the investigation reveals that modern therapeutic regimens can induce significant immune vulnerability, leaving affected patients susceptible to serious complications that routine laboratory measurements frequently fail to capture.
Secondary immunodeficiency is defined as an acquired immune deficiency resulting from an array of external and systemic factors, including cancer treatment, organ failure, chronic disease, nutritional deficiency, and physiological aging. The underlying pathology impairs antibody production, attenuates T-cell immune function, and disrupts protective mucosal immunity. As a consequence of these overlapping functional deficits, affected individuals face an increased risk of developing several severe infections.
To clarify these clinical patterns, the review compiled evidence on medical approaches documented between 2011 and 2026, encompassing therapeutic interventions, clinical reviews, and observed disease trajectories. The analysis examined the heterogeneous definitions currently applied to secondary immunodeficiency as well as the practical therapeutic challenges surrounding immunoglobulin replacement therapy. It specifically examined clinicians’ widespread reliance on serum immunoglobulin G testing as their primary tool for evaluating whether a patient maintains functional antibody production.
The investigation was conducted through comprehensive literature searches across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, strictly adhering to PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Following a systematic screening process, the researcher identified and synthesized data from 100 eligible studies spanning five distinct clinical areas to evaluate how secondary immunodeficiency manifests across diverse patient populations and treatment environments.
Across the reviewed literature, the three primary outcomes identified were hypogammaglobulinemia, reduced vaccine response, and an increased frequency of infections. Treatment-specific data demonstrated substantial risks, with 20 to 50 percent of patients developing clinically significant hypogammaglobulinemia following anti-CD20 therapy. In addition, patients undergoing chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy exhibited extended immune system deficits that persisted beyond immediate treatment windows.
Importantly, the findings demonstrated that individuals possessing normal serum immunoglobulin G levels frequently exhibited functional antibody deficiency. This disconnect complicates clinical identification, as normal quantitative test results can obscure significant functional immune deficits. The review also highlighted ongoing challenges linked to limited regional access to immunoglobulin therapy as well as essential preventive healthcare services.
The review concludes that secondary immunodeficiency warrants formal recognition as a clinically significant and undertreated syndrome rather than a secondary consideration. Addressing these shortcomings will require the urgent development of worldwide clinical standards that prioritize functional immune assessment frameworks alongside coordinated efforts to secure equitable access to necessary therapeutic interventions.
