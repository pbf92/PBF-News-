TITLE: The time is now: kidney replacement therapy liberation from a pediatric critical care perspective
OUTLET: Springer Nature Link

While the optimal timing of kidney replacement therapy (KRT) initiation has been the focus of numerous multicenter randomized controlled trials [1,2,3], its converse, the optimal timing of KRT liberation, remains understudied. Yet liberation timing is arguably as consequential as initiation timing. Both impact the risk of dialytrauma, intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital length of stay, cost, and resource utilization. Although the benefit of early initiation remains less clear in adults [1], the pediatric literature has shown associations between earlier initiation and improved outcomes, including shorter KRT duration [4] and reduced mortality [5]. Though the evidence is limited, timely liberation trials have also been associated with improved outcomes [6]. More than a decade after the 2012 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guideline called for identifying clinical parameters that predict successful discontinuation of acute KRT [7], this work remains in its early stages.
In this issue of Pediatric Nephrology, Ceschia and Vidal [8] synthesize the existing literature on continuous KRT (CKRT) liberation and propose a practical framework for future study. They note that the definition of liberation varies widely, despite the 16th Acute Disease Quality Initiative (ADQI) consensus statement defining “kidney recovery” in acute KRT as independence for at least 14 days [9]. They weigh the consequences of premature liberation attempts against the often overlooked risks of delay, like dialytrauma, resource utilization, prolonged intubation, and reduced ability to mobilize/rehabilitate [6, 10]. The recent LIBERATE-D trial, which found that adults receiving intermittent KRT (IKRT) who were randomized to an indication-based strategy rather than conventional scheduled dialysis had decreased dialysis exposure and improved kidney recovery [11], corroborates the authors’ central argument: unnecessary continuation of KRT is not a neutral default.
The authors’ proposed framework, which separates clinical readiness from renal recovery, mirrors the 17th ADQI recommendation: “RRT should be discontinued if kidney function has recovered sufficiently to reduce the demand–capacity imbalance (current and expected)” [12]. Importantly, clinical readiness and renal recovery may occur on discordant timelines. A child recovering from hematopoietic stem cell transplant who requires multiple daily transfusions may have robust urine output and excellent solute clearance, but the necessary volume burden may exceed what their recovering kidneys can manage without recurrent fluid overload and resultant organ injuries. Conversely, a previously healthy child recovering from sepsis-induced multiorgan dysfunction syndrome may have resolution of shock before they have sufficient renal function to maintain euvolemia and solute clearance without KRT.
The evidence to inform this framework is limited not only in pediatrics, but also in adults. Urine output, during CKRT or around the liberation attempt, is the most consistent predictor of successful liberation, but there are no established cutoffs or standardized interpretation of urine output across studies [13,14,15,16,17]. In pediatrics, clinical factors like age, pre-existing kidney disease, greater fluid accumulation, and history of organ transplant or hematologic/oncologic disease may also influence odds of success [15, 18, 19]. As the authors note, over twenty biomarkers have been studied for predicting KRT liberation [13, 20]. Urine neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (uNGAL) has strong predictive performance in adults [20], and a single-center pediatric study found that levels below 900 ng/mL measured immediately surrounding liberation predicted success with excellent discrimination [21]. Plasma proenkephalin A (PENK) and urine TIMP-2×IGFBP7 also have promising, though limited, results and merit study in children [20].
One of the review’s most useful contributions is its distinction between three related but discrete clinical questions: (1) whether a liberation trial will succeed once begun, (2) whether liberation could be attempted earlier, and (3) whether a patient already off KRT will remain liberated. Nearly all existing studies address only the first question, but these questions have practical consequences for how a center may design a liberation protocol. A tool designed to predict success once a liberation trial begins does not shorten unnecessary KRT exposure in children who could have attempted liberation earlier. Identifying the earliest safe opportunity for liberation would require different methodology with serial assessments of predictors of liberation success throughout the KRT course. Determining whether a child will remain successfully liberated requires post-liberation surveillance of urine output, fluid balance, and laboratory trends to identify children who may benefit from re-initiation. Research on KRT liberation must address all three questions.
We propose KRT liberation be treated like ventilator liberation is in the pediatric ICU. In 2023, the Pediatric Acute Lung Injury and Sepsis Investigators (PALISI) Network published a consensus statement on pediatric ventilator liberation with operational definitions and clinical practice guidelines [22, 23]. The guidelines center on a protocolized extubation readiness testing bundle, which accounts for multiple factors that determine readiness and includes a daily test of readiness using the spontaneous breathing trial (SBT; a short, low-risk test of intrinsic lung function with minimal mechanical support) when appropriate [23]. Pediatric KRT liberation practice is currently driven by institutional practice patterns and individual clinician judgment, possibly due to the lack of standardized urine output or biomarker thresholds and validated liberation protocols [24, 25]. A KRT liberation bundle could be built using the framework illustrated by Ceschia and Vidal [8]. It could be implemented as a daily checklist during multidisciplinary rounds [26] (Fig. 1) to prompt review of urine output, fluid accumulation, and biomarker trends alongside regular clinical readiness assessments. It could suggest furosemide stress test (FST) administration or even possible de-escalation to IKRT (the way that extubation to non-invasive positive pressure ventilation serves as an intermediate step-down of respiratory support). The FST could serve the same purpose as the SBT: a simple, low-risk, inexpensive challenge of intrinsic kidney function. Two prospective adult studies and a multinational pediatric study found urine output after FST was highly predictive of or associated with successful liberation [15, 27, 28], although protocolized FST use to predict successful liberation in children merits further study. More robust clinical decision support tools, which have been used to identify nephrotoxin-associated acute kidney injury (AKI) [29] and guide CKRT initiation [4], could be leveraged for KRT liberation. Hybrid study designs, which combine effectiveness and implementation research, may be beneficial to promote evidence-based practice adoption [30].
Ceschia and Vidal [8] have examined an important evidence gap in pediatric AKI care. Existing pediatric predictors, including urine output and uNGAL, have not been prospectively validated, and multiple clinical questions remain unanswered. Until pediatric KRT liberation evidence matures, daily structured assessment of liberation readiness should mirror evaluations of extubation readiness. KRT liberation deserves the same investment that ventilator liberation has received: consistent definitions, prospectively validated predictors, and a shared framework for weighing clinical readiness against renal recovery.
