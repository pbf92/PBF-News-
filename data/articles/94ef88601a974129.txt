TITLE: Grand Theft Auto 6 Review: Insincere City
OUTLET: The Daily Downforce

Review Rated M for Mature
Let’s make a deal. You can enjoy Grand Theft Auto 6. You can spend the next ten years playing this game and have the time of your life. I’m not here to convince you that you’re wrong, but I’m going to say some very unkind things about this game, so please don’t take them personally.
Yes, the graphics are beautiful. It’s a remarkable technical achievement that runs fantastically on my Xbox Series X. Florida looks like Florida. If you need several thousand words about how impressive the lighting is or how realistically the water behaves, there are plenty of reviews out there that will satisfy you. You don’t need another one from me.
I’ve spent thousands upon thousands of hours playing Grand Theft Auto V, GTA Online, Red Dead Redemption 2, and Red Dead Online. I speedrun these games. My playtime in just the Expanded and Enhanced version of GTA Online is in the thousands of hours. I love these worlds, I love the art, and I’ve developed a deep appreciation for the artists who created them.
When I rolled credits on Grand Theft Auto 6 and closed the game, my Xbox playtime was 100 hours and 44 minutes. I completed the entire story, every side mission, and numerous stranger encounters and other activities. My overall completion was 69%, and I earned 420 Gamerscore.
That was probably the funniest thing Grand Theft Auto 6 did in 100 hours.
This isn’t a review in the sense that I’m going to tell you whether or not you should buy Grand Theft Auto 6. That ship has sailed. This is an artistic critique of a video game and an account of my experience with it as someone who has spent an embarrassing portion of his adult life enjoying the work of Rockstar Games.
Grand Theft Auto 6 is the first new Grand Theft Auto in the post-Dan Houser and Lazlow era of Rockstar Games. I don’t think I fully appreciated how much of what I loved about these games came from the artistic vision of those two men until I played one without them.
What Is This Game Even About?
When you ask someone what a movie is about, they’ll generally tell you the plot of the movie. Jurassic Park is about a billionaire who builds a dinosaur theme park, the dinosaurs get loose, and people get eaten.
That’s what happens in Jurassic Park. What Jurassic Park is about is something else entirely. It’s about scientific hubris, man’s relationship with nature, the commercialization of science, and the folly of believing we can control things we don’t fully understand.
I think Grand Theft Auto V is a fascinating work of art when viewed through that lens. Michael De Santa is a middle-aged man who has achieved some approximation of the American dream and is absolutely miserable. He wants to recapture some of the excitement and ambition of his youth. He sees himself in Franklin and attempts to mold Franklin into a younger version of himself, without entirely understanding what Franklin wants out of life. And then there’s Trevor, who’s basically the embodiment of the ultimate GTA player. He’s a psychotic lunatic who wreaks havoc on the world just for his own amusement.
There are things to think about in Grand Theft Auto V. There’s something being communicated about family, wealth, ambition, materialism, and growing older.
Red Dead Redemption 2 took this to another level entirely. I think it’s an incredibly pensive game. So much of that game is about reflecting on your life, the decisions you’ve made, the kind of person you’ve been, and the kind of person you still have an opportunity to become.
I’ve always found it interesting to think about those two games in the context of the artists themselves growing older. Grand Theft Auto V feels like a game written by people experiencing middle age. Red Dead Redemption 2 feels like a game written by people increasingly contemplating their own mortality and what they’ve done with the time they’ve had on this earth.
You can play almost any mission in Red Dead Redemption 2, get on your horse afterward, ride off into the wilderness, and spend some time thinking about yourself and your own life. I don’t know that I’ve ever played another video game quite like it in that respect.
So what the fuck is Grand Theft Auto 6 about?
I don’t mean what happens in the story. I know what happens in the story, I played the whole fucking thing. What are its themes? What is it communicating about the world? What does it have to say about the human experience? I spent 100 hours looking for the answer to that question.

I absolutely despise the writing in Grand Theft Auto 6.
It’s that particular style of writing that has become ubiquitous in blockbuster Hollywood movies and Netflix shows, that very Joss Whedon, very Guardians of the Galaxy style of sarcastic distancing and ironic detachment. Everyone has a quip. Everyone has something sarcastic to say. Even in moments of extreme violence or considerable danger, everyone is cracking jokes.
Every single character in this game seems to have been written as a lovable scamp. Cal is a lovable scamp. Nate is a lovable scamp. Dre’Quan is a lovable scamp. The crackhead you befriend in Chapter 2 is a lovable scamp. They all have basically the same sense of humor, and they all seem to speak with the same voice.
I liked Cal to some extent because I like Bobby Moynihan, the former SNL cast member who plays him. But there’s only so much an actor can do with a character who has essentially the same personality as everyone else in the game.
I generally preferred playing as Lucia because she occasionally had something to say. Jason is Arthur Morgan on Ambien, just stoic and one-note. It’s not like I found her particularly interesting, either. She just seemed to have slightly more sentences in her dialogue.
The problem with this kind of writing is that it’s almost impossible to become emotionally invested in anything that’s happening. The writers seem terrified of sincerity. They can’t allow a moment of tension or vulnerability to exist without immediately undercutting it with something sarcastic.
Look, this is a message to all writers out there, present and future: If you don’t care, then I don’t care. If you’re not scared, then I’m not scared. If you’re not worried, then I’m not worried. If you don’t have tension, then I don’t have tension. If your characters are constantly commenting on how stupid and lame and unimportant the story you’ve written is, well, guess what I think about the story you’ve just written.
Arthur Morgan is an incredibly funny character. So is Niko Bellic. So are Michael, Franklin, and Trevor. But the artists who created those characters knew when to stop being funny. They understood that the audience needed to see these characters frightened, angry, vulnerable, and occasionally just sad.
Think about Arthur’s conversation with Sister Calderón at the train station. Think about the sincerity of that performance and how much trust the artists placed in the audience to sit there and experience that moment with Arthur. Imagine if the writers had punctuated that scene with some sarcastic little remark about dying of tuberculosis. That seems to be the prevailing philosophy behind the writing in Grand Theft Auto 6.
So I guess the obvious answer is that GTA 6 is about love. Jason and Lucia are a couple, and much of the game is built around them being a couple. But what does the game have to say about love? What does it have to say about being in a relationship with another human being?
Their relationship is never challenged in a way that I found dramatically compelling. They don’t have particularly interesting disagreements. Their individual aspirations don’t create interesting conflicts between them. I never really felt as though their relationship was in any danger, nor did I feel as though the events of the game meaningfully transformed their understanding of one another.
They’re basically Jim and Pam from The Office. It’s cutesy, sarcastic, Marvel romantic comedy writing superimposed on a story about violent career criminals. For a game so heavily invested in depicting a romantic relationship, I came away from it with absolutely nothing to think about regarding love or relationships.
Who Do You Think You Are?
I don’t know how many aura-farming montages there are in Grand Theft Auto 6, but it felt like about 19 of them.
The game absolutely loves lining its characters up, walking them toward the camera in slow motion, putting some music behind them, and expecting you to sit there in awe of how fucking cool these people are. I spent most of these sequences cringing my spine out of my skin and rolling my eyes into my brain stem.
Who the fuck do you think you are? Why am I supposed to be experiencing this particular emotion right now? What has happened in this story that has earned this moment? What have you done with these characters that would make me feel this way about them?
Think about Arthur’s ride after returning from Guarma in Red Dead Redemption 2, accompanied by D’Angelo’s Unshaken. Think about his final ride, accompanied by Daniel Lanois’ That’s the Way It Is.
Those are two of the most extraordinary musical sequences I’ve ever experienced in a video game. They’re the culmination of dozens of hours of storytelling and of my own experiences with Arthur Morgan. There is an enormous amount going on inside your head during those sequences, and the music and presentation give you the space to experience it.
Grand Theft Auto 6 attempts to reproduce that sort of musical sequence, and I distinctly remember sitting there and saying, out loud, “Oh, go fuck yourself, game.”
I don’t give a fuck about these people. I don’t think they’re cool. I’m not laughing. I’m not smiling. You have spent practically the entire game using the cheapest possible form of humor to discourage me from taking these people seriously, and now I’m suddenly supposed to sit here and marvel at them? Holy shit, Grand Theft Auto 6 absolutely loves the smell of its own farts.
It knows what one of those iconic Rockstar Games moments is supposed to look like. It knows how to compose the shot, animate the characters, and put some music behind it. It seems to have completely misunderstood what made those moments iconic in the first place.
Your Girlfriend Is a Horse
I’ve always thought of video games as conversations between the player and the game designer. When I say “game designer,” I’m using that as something of a metaphor for the dungeon master. It could be one person or it could be a thousand people.
As a player, I communicate with the designer through my verbs. I can run, jump, shoot, drive, and interact with things in the world. The designer communicates with me through the way the world responds to those actions. That’s where a lot of the art happens.
The honor system in Red Dead Redemption 2 is a relatively obvious example. In a game so thoroughly concerned with the decisions we make in life and the kind of people we choose to be, there is a gameplay mechanic that encourages you to think about the decisions you make and the kind of person Arthur Morgan is.
It’s an imperfect system, sure, but I understand its artistic purpose. I understand what the designer is saying to me when I perform a particular action and my honor goes up or down. In Grand Theft Auto 6, I spent a stupid amount of time just shooting people in the fucking knees.
The criminal profile system is essentially the spiritual successor to the honor system. If you want to maintain a Professional profile and put yourself in a position to get a good ending, the game encourages you to incapacitate people rather than kill them.
So there I am in a gunfight, tediously aiming at knees and elbows, trying to accumulate my good-guy points for an ending to a story in which I have virtually no emotional investment.
