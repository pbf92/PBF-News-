TITLE: Elk River to pay $72,527 of $4.4m FTC judgment over Made in USA claims
OUTLET: PPC Land

Elk River Inc., an Alabama manufacturer of fall protection harnesses for construction and tower workers, has agreed to a $4.4 million judgment to settle Federal Trade Commission allegations that it falsely labeled and advertised its harnesses as "Made in USA." The FTC announced the case on November 17, 2026, the same day its complaint was stamped as filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama. Most of it will not be paid unless a court finds Elk River misrepresented its finances. According to the FTC, the judgment will be suspended after Elk River pays $72,527, "due to the company's inability to pay the full amount."
In Short
The FTC says a maker of worker safety harnesses called its products "Made in USA" on labels, its website, trade show flyers and social media posts, even though 68 of the 143 parts and materials in the harnesses came from abroad. The case shows that the FTC treats hashtags, catalog footers and flyer footers as origin claims, and that an unqualified claim has to meet an "all or virtually all" standard rather than a final-assembly test. Elk River settled without admitting or denying the allegations, will pay $72,527 of a $4.4 million judgment, and must email customers who bought its harnesses between August 2021 and June 2026 once a judge signs the order.
A domestic brand built on the claim
Elk River is based in Cullman, Alabama, at 1202 26th Street SW, according to the complaint. It makes harnesses, anchors and lanyards for what the FTC describes as "professional line workers (e.g., construction and tower climbing workers)." It sells 130 harness models, each assigned its own stock keeping unit, and moves "thousands of harnesses each year," the complaint states. It does not sell to end users. Every harness goes through "a network of authorized dealers and distributors."
The origin claim sat at the center of the brand, as the complaint describes it. According to the FTC, Elk River presented itself as "not just a brand, product, device, gadget or gizmo," but as "your fall protection partner," and drew an explicit line against foreign competitors: "not the low cost import you purchase to save a dollar. We are what you reach for when the protection, the comfort and the safety of your team is the number one priority."
That positioning is the backdrop for every count in the case. The FTC's complaint is a list of places where Elk River allegedly made the same unqualified promise, over several years, across print, web, social and the product itself.

Neither GME Supply nor X1 Safety, LLC, which trades as Novarlo, is a defendant. The claims appear in the complaint as evidence of how the message travelled, not as separate charges.
What is inside the harness
The FTC does not dispute where the harnesses come together. "While final assembly of the harnesses occurs in Defendant's manufacturing facility in Alabama, most components and materials - including components essential to the function and safe operation of the harness - originate outside the United States," the complaint states.
The count is specific. According to the complaint, "there are 143 components and materials in Defendant's harnesses. Of those, 68 (or 52%) originate abroad - including 34 from Taiwan, 17 from China, 8 from Vietnam, 4 from Japan, 3 from South Korea, and 2 from Mexico."
The numbers in the complaint do not reconcile. By PPC Land's arithmetic, 68 of 143 is 47.6 percent, not 52 percent, and the six country figures add up to exactly 68. The 52 percent figure matches the other 75 items instead. The complaint does not explain the difference, and both the complaint and the FTC's announcement describe "most" components and materials as foreign. The legal theory does not turn on a majority, however. The standard the FTC applies is "all or virtually all," and its case is that 68 imported items out of 143 is more than a negligible amount under either percentage.
Which parts were imported also matters to the FTC's account. The complaint names "D-rings, carabiners, snap hooks, and self-retracting lifelines" and spends a full paragraph on what they do. A D-ring, it says, "is made of steel or aluminum and acts as a secure attachment point connecting lifelines to the harnesses' webbing." Carabiners and snap hooks "operate as load-bearing connection points and are designed to prevent accidental disengagement." And "a self-retracting lifeline, in turn, works like a car seat belt: the lifeline can be slowly unwound from around a drum but automatically locks at the onset of a fall to arrest the worker's descent."
The complaint adds that "a significant portion of the harnesses' manufacturing costs are attributable to their foreign components and materials," without giving a figure. Its conclusion: the harnesses "contain more than a negligible or de minimis level of foreign content," so the claims were "false, misleading, and/or unsubstantiated."
Paragraph 35 adds a detail about timing. According to the FTC, Elk River "only ceased its unlawful activities after the start of the Commission's investigation into its unlawful conduct." The complaint does not say when that investigation began.
The rule wording
Three legal authorities sit behind the case, and the complaint quotes each one. The Made in USA Labeling Rule, 16 C.F.R. Part 323, took effect on August 13, 2021, according to the complaint. Its core provision, 16 C.F.R. 323.2, as quoted in the complaint, reads: "it is an unfair or deceptive act or practice . . . to label any product as Made in the United States unless the final assembly or processing of the product occurs in the United States, all significant processing that goes into the product occurs in the United States, and all or virtually all ingredients or components of the product are made and sourced in the United States."
The rule's definition, 16 C.F.R. 323.1(a), is broad. "Made in the United States" means "any unqualified representation, express or implied, that a product or service, or a specified component thereof, is of U.S. origin, including, but not limited to, a representation that such product or service is 'made,' 'manufactured,' 'built,' 'produced,' 'created,' or 'crafted' in the United States or in America, or any other unqualified U.S.-origin claim."
The second authority is Section 45a, 15 U.S.C. 45a, which ties labels to the FTC's own case law: "[t]o the extent any person introduces, delivers for introduction, sells, advertises, or offers for sale in commerce a product with a 'Made in the U.S.A.' or 'Made in America' label, or the equivalent thereof, in order to represent that such product was in whole or substantial part of domestic origin, such label shall be consistent with decisions and orders of the Federal Trade Commission issued pursuant to section 45 of [Title 15]."
Those decisions and orders, according to the complaint, allow a U.S.-origin claim through one of three routes:
- The first is the full standard: final assembly or processing in the United States, all significant processing in the United States, and "all or virtually all ingredients or components of the product are made and sourced in the United States."
- The second is a qualified claim, where "a Clear and Conspicuous qualification appears immediately adjacent to the representation that accurately conveys the extent to which the product contains foreign parts, ingredients or components, and/or processing."
- The third covers assembly claims: "For a claim that a product is assembled in the United States, the product is last substantially transformed in the United States, the product's principal assembly takes place in the United States, and United States assembly operations are substantial."
The complaint cites 13 FTC decisions and orders that carry this definition, from In the Matter of Nectar Brand LLC (Docket C-4656, August 28, 2018) to In the Matter of ExotoUSA LLC (Docket C-4800, January 10, 2024), along with a 2022 federal court order in FTC v. Cardiff. The third authority is Section 5(a) of the FTC Act, the general ban on "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce."
The split is worth noting. Only the labels fall under the rule-based count. The website, flyers, catalog and social posts are covered by the broader Section 5 count, which runs two months longer, to July 2026, the same month the trade show flyer left the website. The FTC seeks relief under Sections 13(b) and 19 of the FTC Act, 15 U.S.C. 53(b) and 57b.
The settlement terms
The stipulated order works like a consent decree: both sides agreed to its terms, and it binds Elk River once a judge signs it. According to the FTC's announcement, the order bars Elk River from "making misrepresentations regarding U.S.-origin claims" and from "making misleading or unsubstantiated country-of-origin claims," and requires the company "to notify its consumers of the settlement with the FTC." The proposed order, which the FTC published with the complaint, sets out the detail. Under it, Elk River neither admits nor denies the complaint's allegations, except where the order specifically says otherwise.
The dates sit close together. Elk River's external labels carried the claim until approximately May 29, 2026, according to the complaint. Its sworn financial statement is dated June 5, 2026. The homepage claim lasted until June 2026, and the customer notice window closes on June 10, 2026.
How the case proceeds from here
According to the FTC's announcement and the proposed order, the process has seven steps:
- The Commission voted 2-0 to authorize staff to file the complaint and the stipulated order.
- The complaint was filed on November 17, 2026, stamped at 11:46 a.m., in the Northern District of Alabama, Northeastern Division, as Case 5:26-cv-02278-HNJ. The stipulated order was filed the same day.
- A judge must approve and sign the order. The FTC notes that "stipulated orders have the force of law when approved and signed by the District Court judge."
- Within 7 days of entry, Elk River pays $72,527.50, and the rest of the judgment is suspended.
- Within 30 days of entry, Elk River emails the notice to customers who bought harnesses between August 1, 2021 and June 10, 2026.
- Within 60 days of entry, Elk River reports to the FTC on the notices, and reports again when the program ends.
- One year after entry, Elk River files a sworn compliance report. Compliance notice duties run for 20 years.
The order bears the names of Christopher Mufarrige, Director of the FTC's Bureau of Consumer Protection, and Annie Chiang, Deputy Director. The complaint is signed by FTC attorney James W. Clayton.
The fourth case this year
The FTC frames Elk River as part of a campaign. According to the announcement, the case "marks the fourth 'Made in the USA' enforcement action taken by the Commission this year," and the agency "issued warning letters in July to companies that appear to have misrepresented certain products as 'Made in the USA.'"
