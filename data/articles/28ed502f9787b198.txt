TITLE: Agent Orange Handlers Diagnosed With Parkinson's at Nearly Twice Rate of Unexposed Veterans
OUTLET: Tech Times

Veterans with direct or occupational Agent Orange exposure, including those who sprayed or handled the herbicide, were diagnosed with Parkinson's disease at nearly twice the rate of unexposed Vietnam-era veterans, according to an analysis of Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) health records published November 16 in JAMA Neurology by researchers at Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU) and the VA Portland Health Care System. The hazard ratio for direct or occupational exposure was 1.99.
Veterans with any exposure were also diagnosed more often with dementia with Lewy bodies (also called Lewy body dementia) and with REM sleep behavior disorder, a condition in which people physically act out dreams and that neurologists treat as an early warning of both diseases. The hazard ratios for these outcomes were 1.66 and 1.78.
The analysis covered 4,468,509 veterans followed for an average of 12.5 years. It is observational, so it shows an association and cannot prove that Agent Orange caused any diagnosis, a limit the authors reflect in concluding that exposure "may be an independent risk factor" for Parkinson's and related conditions.
For Vietnam-era veterans and their families, the findings add a dose-response signal and an early-warning marker to a link that VA already presumes for Parkinson's disease and parkinsonism when qualifying service is documented.
Hazard Ratios Climb From 1.44 to 1.99 as Exposure Gets More Direct
Researchers screened 4,917,272 veterans who served during the Vietnam War era, August 1964 to May 1975, and analyzed 4,468,509 of them using VA records from January 1, 1999, through December 30, 2022. The mean age was 60.6 and 98% were men. Veterans who already had one of the target conditions, or who developed one within six months of the start of follow-up, were excluded. Of those analyzed, 1,035,210 (23.2%) were classed as exposed, and 7,600 veterans had direct or occupational exposure through Operation Ranch Hand, the Army Chemical Corps, or direct handling or spraying.
Source for all five estimates: the JAMA Neurology abstract.
A hazard ratio compares how quickly new diagnoses accumulate in exposed and unexposed groups, and OHSU said the analysis adjusted for traumatic brain injury and frailty. It is a relative measure, so a ratio of 1.44 does not mean 44% of exposed veterans will develop Parkinson's. OHSU's release restates the ratios as percentages: 44% for Parkinson's, 66% for Lewy body dementia, and 99% for Parkinson's among veterans who directly handled or sprayed the herbicide. It also cites a combined 57% figure for the two conditions that is not in the paper's abstract.
The climb from 1.44 to 1.99 matters because risk that rises with heavier exposure, called a biological gradient, is one of the criteria epidemiologist Austin Bradford Hill proposed in 1965 for judging whether an association is causal, although he said no single criterion settles the question. Gregory Scott said the study is the first in veterans to tie higher Parkinson's risk to heavier Agent Orange exposure, a claim the OHSU release presents as his. Earlier work also pointed toward risk: a 2021 JAMA Neurology study of 316,351 Vietnam-era veterans found a hazard ratio of 1.68 for dementia, with exposed veterans diagnosed at a mean age of 67.5 versus 68.8 for unexposed veterans.
Exposed Veterans Show 78% Higher Hazard of REM Sleep Behavior Disorder, Early Parkinson's Warning
REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) occurs when the muscle paralysis that normally accompanies REM sleep fails, so sleepers act out dreams, sometimes violently enough to injure themselves or a bed partner. The paper counted RBD alongside Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies as a synucleinopathy, a disease family defined by clumps of the protein alpha-synuclein. Any exposure was associated with an RBD hazard ratio of 1.78, and direct exposure with 2.25, the highest estimate in the study. OHSU's summary of the findings lists only Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia as outcomes.
The RBD signal matters because most people with unexplained, or idiopathic, RBD go on to develop a related disease. A 2019 meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews reported that 82.4% of patients with idiopathic RBD had developed a neurodegenerative disease by 10.5 years. The paper does not estimate how many veterans with RBD in this cohort will go on to Parkinson's or Lewy body dementia, so the diagnosed cases may represent only part of the eventual total.
A small conference abstract published in Movement Disorders in 2025 found that 15 of 54 Agent Orange-exposed veterans without Parkinson's met a probable-prodromal threshold, compared with 30 controls. A definite RBD diagnosis requires a sleep study that shows dream-enacting behavior alongside REM sleep without muscle paralysis, so veterans or bed partners who notice dream enactment can raise it with a clinician. Exposed veterans may also be eligible for a free environmental health registry evaluation.
National Academies Called Evidence Limited in 2018; OHSU Now Says Definitive
OHSU's release says the study "definitively draws the link" between Agent Orange and neurodegenerative conditions including Parkinson's. The paper's own conclusion is narrower: the authors say Agent Orange may be an independent risk factor for Parkinson's disease and related conditions. A 2018 National Academies review of Vietnam-veteran health evidence kept its conclusion of "limited or suggestive evidence" of an association with Parkinson's disease. The committee noted that some earlier studies relied on self-reports or proxy measures of exposure, and it set aside others for lacking objective measures such as serum concentrations.
Self-Reported Exposure and 7,600 Direct Handlers Limit What Study Can Prove
The journal abstract says Agent Orange exposure was defined by self-report, while OHSU describes the exposure as possible or presumed. Self-reports can be distorted by differences in how accurately people recall past events, a recall bias that can exaggerate an apparent association. The direct-exposure group is small, at 7,600 veterans or 0.2% of the cohort, so its estimates are less precise than the overall figures, and the cohort is 98% men, so the findings may not carry over to women.
The results held after additional adjustment, and sensitivity analyses suggested that a substantial unmeasured confounder would be needed to explain the associations away. Statistical adjustment can account only for factors that were measured, which is why unknown confounders remain the central limit of observational studies. The same OHSU and VA Portland group published a November 24, 2025 JAMA Neurology study of 11,310,411 veterans that linked untreated sleep apnea to Parkinson's, with a hazard ratio of 1.92.
Korean and U.S. Clinic Studies Disagree on Whether Exposure Changes Parkinson's Symptoms
The JAMA Neurology authors call for work on whether exposed veterans show a distinct clinical picture of Parkinson's. Existing evidence conflicts. A Korean study compared 143 Parkinson's patients exposed to Agent Orange with 500 unexposed patients and found lower facial-expression scores, worse scores on tremor, rigidity and finger-tap items, and lower uptake of the imaging tracer 18F-FP-CIT in the caudate and putamen. A prospective study of U.S. veterans by University of Vermont researchers and colleagues, published in March 2026 in Parkinsonism & Related Disorders, found no statistically significant differences between veterans with and without exposure, although the authors noted trends that might need a larger sample to confirm.
Dioxin Contaminant Is Under Study, but Mechanism Remains Unproven
Agent Orange was a mix of the herbicides 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T, and the 2,4,5-T carried a contaminant called TCDD, a dioxin. Laboratory work offers hints rather than answers. A 2017 mouse study in the Journal of Biochemical and Molecular Toxicology found that TCDD raised the protein UbcH7 and promoted degradation of synphilin-1 in the ventral midbrain, a brain region central to Parkinson's. That result does not show how, or whether, those pathways cause Parkinson's in exposed veterans, and the JAMA Neurology authors list mechanism and gene-toxin interaction research among next steps.
Study Funding Came From VA, Defense Department and NIH Awards
According to OHSU, the work was supported by VA awards, private foundation and university awards, Department of Defense funding and a National Institute on Aging grant from the National Institutes of Health. OHSU notes that the content does not necessarily represent the views of the funders.
Does Agent Orange Cause Parkinson's Disease? What VA Already Presumes
The study cannot settle causation, and VA benefits do not wait for it: VA lists Parkinson's disease and parkinsonism among presumptive Agent Orange conditions, so qualifying veterans do not have to prove the connection. Parkinson's was added in a 2010 final rule, following a 2009 Institute of Medicine report. VA later added parkinsonism, a change the Michael J. Fox Foundation said extends to Parkinson's disease dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies and multiple system atrophy.
Qualifying service includes Vietnam from January 9, 1962, to May 7, 1975, along with inland waterways and certain vessels near Vietnam and Cambodia. The Korean demilitarized zone qualifies from September 1, 1967, to August 31, 1971. Other covered locations include Thailand bases, Laos, Cambodia, Guam and American Samoa, and Johnston Atoll, each with its own dates. Veterans previously denied for parkinsonism do not need to refile because VA reviews those denials automatically, while other previously denied claims can go through a Supplemental Claim.
More than 110,000 veterans with Parkinson's receive VA care, and veterans make up about 6% of the U.S. population but about 11% of people with Parkinson's, according to the Michael J. Fox Foundation. Jack Roberts, 82, who served at Tan Son Nhut Air Base starting in 1965 and was diagnosed in 2019, told WUFT that "the common denominator was Agent Orange."
For veterans and families, the study adds three practical points. Risk rose with how directly someone handled Agent Orange, an early-warning sleep disorder was elevated across all exposed veterans, and the numbers are relative hazards from records rather than personal odds. Parkinson's disease and parkinsonism already qualify for VA presumption with documented service, dream enactment is worth raising with a clinician, and the open questions are causation, mechanism and whether Parkinson's in exposed veterans looks different.
Frequently Asked Questions
Is Parkinson's disease a VA presumptive condition for Agent Orange exposure?
Yes, for veterans with qualifying service. VA lists Parkinson's disease and parkinsonism as presumptive conditions, and veterans can file online, by mail, in person or with an accredited representative, using medical and service records such as a DD214 or other separation documents that show time and location of service.
What is REM sleep behavior disorder, and why does it matter for exposed veterans?
It is a sleep disorder in which people act out dreams because normal muscle paralysis during REM sleep fails. The study found hazard ratios of 1.78 for any exposure and 2.25 for direct exposure, according to the JAMA Neurology abstract. A meta-analysis found that 82.4% of people with idiopathic RBD developed a neurodegenerative disease by 10.5 years, usually Parkinson's disease or dementia with Lewy bodies, which is why RBD is treated as an early warning.
Does this study prove Agent Orange causes Parkinson's disease?
No. It is an observational analysis of health records, and the authors conclude that exposure may be an independent risk factor. A 2018 National Academies review found limited or suggestive evidence of an association, and the new paper's exposure measure was defined by self-report.
