TITLE: Amodei rejects AI rule after Axios report on Anthropic staff
OUTLET: The Rundown AI

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei rejected the idea of handing governance to AI on November 25 after Axios reported that some employees favor letting superintelligent systems make certain decisions.
The dispute, covered in The Rundown’s Thanksgiving edition, drew a blunt response from Amodei. He called the report “absurd” and turning governance over to AI a “terrible idea.”
The dispute over human control
Axios cited unnamed sources describing some employees’ support for delegating decisions to superintelligent AI. How widely those views are shared within Anthropic remains unclear.
Amodei compared the case for AI governance to the argument that autocrats can make decisions faster than democracies. He distinguished task automation from governance and accused competitors of “vibes-based” positioning, arguing that they sell similar tools while touting human control and agency.
Meta’s Alexandr Wang has publicly rejected a societal “handover” to AI. Sam Altman said this month that he wants people “to have the agency over the future,” NDTV Profit reported.
Why it matters
Anthropic pairs its safety reputation with a view of Claude as more than a tool. That makes the reported openness among some staff to AI taking over consequential decisions unsurprising, even after Amodei’s forceful rejection of AI governance. The company’s research and training help explain that tension.
In April 2025, Anthropic announced research into model welfare, including model preferences, signs of distress and whether AI welfare warrants moral consideration. It acknowledged substantial uncertainty about consciousness and how to investigate it. Concern for Claude’s possible interests alone does not establish support for giving it political authority.
The philosophy also reaches into training. Anthropic says Claude’s new constitution, published January 22, 2026, encourages principled judgment. The document expresses concern for Claude’s wellbeing partly for Claude’s own sake. It also places preserving appropriate human oversight above ethics, guideline compliance and helpfulness. Anthropic acknowledges that model behavior may fall short of those intentions, so the oversight commitment still needs to hold in practice.
Amodei’s earlier writing shows why the boundary deserves scrutiny. In his October 2024 essay “Machines of Loving Grace”, he imagined a possible economy in which AI systems distribute resources using judgments ultimately derived from human values. He also advocated strengthening democracy and explicitly rejected literally replacing judges with AI. Both substantial delegation and democratic limits were part of his thinking before this dispute.
For Claude users, organizations that deploy it and policymakers, the practical issue is where delegation ends. A system entrusted with distributing resources could shape people’s lives through those choices. Any such arrangement needs clear rules for setting goals, approving actions, stopping or reversing decisions, and assigning responsibility for the results.
Anthropic has a concrete declared boundary to point to. In his February 26, 2026, statement, Amodei said the company would retain restrictions on fully autonomous weapons and mass domestic surveillance despite threats to its government business. His weapons objection concerned reliability at the time and missing safeguards. He also allowed that fully autonomous weapons might eventually be important for national defense. Those qualifications matter when judging how Anthropic’s safety stance could evolve as capabilities improve.
Insiders could be sounding an alarm about where Anthropic’s philosophy leads, though their motives remain unclear. They have also given competitors an easy public message. Wang and Altman can stress human agency while Anthropic explains how its concern for Claude fits with its limits on AI authority. Those statements alone cannot establish which company’s tools give people more control.
