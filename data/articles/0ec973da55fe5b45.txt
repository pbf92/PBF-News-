TITLE: Ad code is no shield: Google's back button spam rule hits the page
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google today added a frequently asked questions section to the back button hijacking policy in its spam policies for web search, answering five questions about a rule that has been enforced since June 15, 2026, and that has already shown up in the quarterly results of ad tech companies and publishers. The answers do not alter the definition of the practice. They settle who carries the risk when third-party advertising code is involved, confirm that Google Discover falls under the policy, extend it to visitors who arrive from social media and other non-Search channels, and place app hand-offs outside its scope.
In Short
Google today published five questions and answers explaining how its back button hijacking spam rule works. The answers matter most to publishers that run third-party ad code, depend on Discover or social media for traffic, or push readers into apps. Ad platform code is still allowed, but any page that traps the back button can face action no matter who wrote the script, and the rule covers Discover and visits from outside Search while leaving "open in app" behaviour alone.
What changed today
The change is short on the page and specific in effect. According to the Search Central documentation changelog entry dated November 23, Google "Added a back button hijacking FAQ section to the malicious practices section of our spam policies." The stated reason was "To answer common questions we've received about the back button hijacking policy." The spam policies page now carries the stamp "Last updated 2026-11-23 UTC."
The FAQ sits directly beneath the policy's definition, which reads: "Back button hijacking is when a site interferes with user browser navigation by manipulating the browser history or other functionalities, preventing them from using their back button to immediately get back to the page they came from." The changelog describes no edit to that sentence. What is new is the block of five questions and Google's answers, placed under the heading "Back button hijacking FAQ."
The format has a precedent on the same page. The site reputation policy section already carries its own FAQ, covering how manual actions taken outside the European Economic Area relate to rankings inside it. The back button policy is now the second policy on the page to receive one.
The five questions, in Google's words
The spam submission report in the second answer links to https://search.google.com/search-console/report-spam. The word Discover in the third answer links to Google's documentation on Discover and websites.
What the policy said before the FAQ
Google announced the policy on April 13, 2026, filing it under the malicious practices category of its spam policies and giving site owners two months before enforcement began on June 15. Pages engaging in the practice became subject to manual spam actions or automated demotions. In that announcement Google already acknowledged that some instances could originate from a site's included libraries or its advertising platform, and it said site owners were expected to review their technical implementation and remove or disable the code responsible.
Two weeks later, on April 27, Google started sending warnings through Search Console to sites it had detected using the technique. The notices listed sample URLs, stated that no manual action had been taken at that point, repeated the June 15 enforcement date, and said changes made on or after April 17, 2026 were not reflected. Google also told recipients it would re-verify compliance before taking any manual action.
None of the material reported at the time addressed Discover, visitors arriving from social media, or apps. That is the gap the FAQ fills.
Ad platform code: allowed, the behaviour is not
The first question opens with a statement of fact before it becomes a question. "Some back button hijacking was caused by third-party advertising platforms," Google writes, in the past tense. That acknowledgement frames the answer that follows. Google is not banning a category of vendor. It is judging what a page does.
"We only take action on pages that exhibit back button hijacking behavior," the answer reads. The relationship with an advertising platform is not the test. A publisher that imports a script which rewrites browser history is, under this wording, in the same position as a publisher that wrote the script itself. The answer also speaks of "pages", not domains or vendors, though it does not say whether an action can spread beyond the pages concerned.
The commercial record since June shows what was riding on that question. Taboola said in August that the end of Explore More, a product that showed users additional sponsored content after they pressed back on a publisher's site, would remove more than $20 million of second-half ex-TAC gross profit once Google's policy took hold. On November 4, Taboola's quarterly filing attributed lower revenue from existing publisher partners largely to the deprecation of Explore More following a Google policy change in the second quarter, and the company described a slower than expected rollout of its replacement, Next Engage. A day later, USA TODAY Co. cited lower revenue from third-party vendors as a result of Google's spam policy among the reasons its third-quarter digital advertising fell, with chief executive Mike Reed saying the policy had its full effect in the third quarter and that the remaining exposure was relatively small.
Google's own advertising business was not untouched either. AdSense had added a browser back button trigger to its vignette ads among six additional triggers announced on February 9, 2026, which switched on automatically from March 9 for publishers who allowed additional triggers. The overlap with the spam policy was flagged on April 28, and on May 7 Google said it would remove the back button trigger from vignette ads effective June 15, the same day enforcement began. The new FAQ does not mention AdSense or any named vendor.
Search consultant Marie Haynes summarised the first answer on LinkedIn today in plainer terms: "You can import code hosted by advertising platforms but if that code hijacks your back button, that's your responsibility."
Discover counts as Search
The third answer removes any doubt about Google's recommendation feed. "Discover is a part of Google Search and our spam policies apply to Discover as well," according to Google. The answer is short, but the stakes for news publishers are not. Discover accounted for two-thirds of Google referrals to news websites in Chartbeat data covering July 2023 to April 2025, drawn from 2,000 news and media sites and published by Press Gazette in August 2025.
The spam policies page already names Discover elsewhere. Under the heading on policy circumvention, Google states that if a site keeps trying to bypass its spam or content policies, it "may take appropriate action which may include restricting or removing eligibility for some of our search features (for example, Top Stories, Discover)." Today's answer connects that general statement to this specific policy.
Traffic from outside Search
The fourth answer is the broadest. The question describes a user who arrives from "a non-Search channel (such as social media)" and is trapped when trying to return. Google's answer does not mention the referral source at all: "If back button hijacking happens when a user is browsing within a browser, then the policy applies."
The test, in other words, is where the browsing happens rather than how the visitor got there. A page that behaves normally for visitors from a Google results page but rewrites history for visitors from a social network, a newsletter or a paid campaign is, on this wording, still within the policy. The consequences, however, still play out in Google Search. The page opens by stating that to be eligible to appear in Google web search results, content "shouldn't violate Google Search's overall policies or the spam policies listed on this page."
Behaviour that varies by entry point is not a new idea in Google's documentation. In the hacked content section of the same page, Google notes that malicious redirects can vary and that the kind of redirect "sometimes depends on the referrer, user agent, or device." The back button FAQ does not address referrer-dependent hijacking directly. Its answer simply makes the referrer irrelevant.
Haynes read the answer the same way: "It doesn't just apply to Search. If it happens in a browser, then the policy applies."
Apps sit outside the rule
The fifth answer draws the boundary on the other side. When a website opens an external app, and that app then keeps the user from returning to the original destination, Google says "app navigation and "open in app" behaviors are outside the scope of the back button hijacking policy."
That covers the familiar pattern of mobile sites that prompt visitors to continue in a native app. Once the user has left the browser for the app, what happens to the back button is no longer a matter for this policy. The answer does not say whether in-app browsers, the embedded browsers that many social apps use to open links, count as "a browser" for the purposes of the fourth answer.
Reporting through the spam form
The second answer confirms that users can report suspected back button hijacking through the standard spam submission report. That form has changed this year. Google altered it in early April so that submission text could be passed to a site owner when a report led to a manual action, and after criticism it said on April 24 that it would not process submissions containing personally identifying information.
The introduction to the spam policies page describes a narrower role for reports than many site owners assume. "We're focused on developing scalable and automated solutions to problems, and we'll use these reports to further improve our spam detection systems," according to Google. The FAQ answer does not say whether a back button report triggers review of the reported page.
How enforcement works, according to the spam policies page
The spam policies page and the April announcement together describe the enforcement path in five stages.
- Detection. Google says it detects violations "both through automated systems and, as needed, human review that can result in a manual action." A manual action is a penalty applied by a human reviewer.
- Reports. Users can flag suspected violations through the spam submission report at search.google.com/search-console/report-spam. Google says it uses these reports to improve its detection systems.
- Notice. Manual actions appear in the Manual actions report in Search Console. Ahead of enforcement in April, Google also sent advance warnings through Search Console with sample URLs.
- Consequence. "Sites that violate our policies may rank lower in results or not appear in results at all," according to the page. For back button hijacking specifically, the April announcement named manual spam actions and automated demotions. The FAQ adds that action is taken "only" on pages that exhibit the behaviour.
- Escalation. Under the policy circumvention section, continued attempts to bypass the spam policies can lead to the loss of eligibility for search features such as Top Stories and Discover and to broader action, such as removing more sections of a site from results.
A site with a manual action can submit a reconsideration request through Search Console after removing the offending code. The FAQ does not revisit that process.
A busy year for the spam policies page
Today's addition is the latest in a series of edits to the same document. On May 15, Google clarified that its spam policies apply to generative AI responses in Search; the page's introduction now defines spam as including "attempting to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search."
