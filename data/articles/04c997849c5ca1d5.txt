TITLE: To Save the World, We Need Good Dads
OUTLET: Christianity Today

“Fathers are the most important men in any community, anywhere, ever.” This bold and perhaps controversial claim captures the essence of Anthony B. Bradley’s belief that the fate of children, the family, American society, and perhaps even the free world depends upon good fathers. After reading his new book, The Fatherhood Effect: Consequences of a Dad-Deprived Culture and Practical Strategies for Positive Change, it’s hard to argue with him.
Bradley—a distinguished researcher, academic, and theologian—draws on his expertise at the intersection of theology and the social sciences and on his decades of experience teaching and mentoring students to make a compelling argument for the irreplaceable role of fathers. To make that case, he surveys the empirical research showing that father absence leads to measurable harm that can negatively shape the trajectories of children and entire communities, sometimes for generations.
The scope of Bradley’s research is exhaustive. He begins by tracing the erosion of fatherhood in American life over the past 200 years, highlighting factors such as the Industrial Revolution, mass incarceration, and the prevalence of children born out of wedlock.
He then devotes a chapter to each of several areas in which father absence does measurable harm. He documents the mental and physical effects, including significantly higher rates of depression, anxiety, suicidality, and obesity. He explores how father absence leads to higher rates of trauma and abuse. He speaks to challenges children face when their fathers are incarcerated. He addresses its impact on infant mortality and childhood development. And he highlights how children in fatherless households tend to exhibit earlier sexual activity, pregnancy, and exploitation, as well as higher rates of poverty and addiction.
The book is filled with alarming and depressing statistics. Here is just one statistical snapshot captures the scope of the problem: According to a 1998 US Department of Justice study, “children who come from fatherless homes account for
63 percent of youth suicides
90 percent of homeless and runaway youths
85 percent of children with behavioral disorders
71 percent of high school dropouts
70 percent of juvenile incarceration
75 percent of adolescent substance-abuse patients.
Each chapter follows the same pattern. Bradley begins by personalizing the issue at hand—how father absence impairs early childhood development, for example—with a real-life anecdote. He then surveys the empirical research on a particular domain (mental health, early childhood development, poverty), highlighting the consequences for children, families, and communities. In addition to surveying the relevant studies, he also devotes considerable attention to analyzing cultural artifacts drawn from literature, music, and film. Thankfully, he doesn’t just articulate the problem but ends each chapter with creative solutions for organizations like nonprofits and churches to make a positive difference in the lives of fathers and their families and communities.
Altogether, Bradley makes an overwhelming, evidence-based case that—all things being equal—the average child’s life is significantly worse without an engaged father or father figure and significantly better with one.
This is an eye-opening and compelling book that brings together a wide range of research, cultural analysis, and constructive solutions. I want to commend three aspects in particular.
First, I appreciated Bradley’s emphasis on the positive impact men can have on the lives of children, families, and communities without having biological children of their own. Throughout the book, Bradley commends surrogate fathers, noting that “in the absence of biological fathers, grandfathers and mature father figures are equally effective” at contributing to positive outcomes. (Here he is talking about faith transmission, but the positive impact is much broader than just this domain.) This is especially helpful as more men are marrying and having children later. One doesn’t have to have biological children to make a world of difference as a coach, mentor, or teacher.
Second, the resources that Bradley curates and recommends are worth the price of the book. As I read, I was surprised at the scope and scale of the problem, but I wasn’t ultimately surprised at the fact that father absence is very bad for kids, families, and society. If all the book did was spell out the depressing effects of father absence, it would be incomplete. This book needed to provide creative and actionable solutions, and Bradley does that in droves.
Bradley concludes each chapter with solutions focused on solving the particular challenge at hand, whether juvenile delinquency, childhood trauma, or teen sexuality. With each approach, he spells out what organizations can do, unpacks how they coach and train fathers, and suggests how trained fathers can intervene. He also provides a 20-plus-page “Directory of Fatherhood Resources” appendix where readers can connect with local, national, international, and faith-based organizations that can help them in equipping and encouraging fathers.
The third highlight is his articulation of what really matters for fathers to make a positive impact. Bradley draws a distinction between father absence and dad deprivation. Father absence is what it sounds like: a father who is both absent and uninvolved. This is obviously a problem. Dad deprivation, on the other hand, involves a father who may be physically present but neglects the act of fathering. It’s very possible to grow up with a father at home and still be dad-deprived.
Both dad deprivation and father absence contribute to negative outcomes for children, families, and communities. I found this distinction helpful because it highlights that it is not enough to be physically present; fathers must be engaged with their children if they want to set them on a trajectory to flourish. Bradley builds on this and points out that “it is the quality, not the mere quantity, of a father’s involvement that matters most.”
Research shows that the number of activities a father and child do together matters far less than the quality of relationship between father and child. He writes: “This distinction is profound. It suggests that a father’s power lies not in being a constant playmate but in being a steady, reliable source of emotional security.” This helpfully reframes how we imagine what is most important in fathering.
Not every aspect of The Fatherhood Effect was a success, however. I found two aspects of the book underwhelming.
The first is Bradley’s emphasis on the importance of cultural artifacts. Bradley notes that these “recover the human particularity that statistics necessarily abstract away.” (True enough.) But he goes further and argues that “the arts don’t merely illustrate our argument; they provide thick description of the human realities that make our statistical findings intelligible and urgent.”
He’s convinced these examples are essential, but I don’t know how much they actually strengthen his case. I’m simply not convinced that knowing that Tupac rapped about father wounds or that James Dean’s character in Rebel Without a Cause acted out because of an absent father moves the needle.
Second, I found his treatment of the role of fathers in faith formation rather disappointing. In the introduction, Bradley speaks to this essential role: “Here’s what the data shows: the most important predicator of a child’s faith persistence is their relationship with their father.” But then Bradley makes a hard pivot.
After appropriately criticizing stoic fathers who focus primarily on provision, discipline, and theological teaching, Bradley suggests that what is “even worse is a father who thinks of his children as his ‘disciples.’” He continues: “Children are not disciples, and the Bible never describes a parent’s role in those terms. Children are not pupils or students.” I found this surprising and strange.
It is true that the Bible does not explicitly say that children are the disciples of their fathers. But it seems hard to deny that the substance of this notion is in Scripture. There is ample evidence in the Scriptures that fathers are to teach, instruct, and have a very pastoral role in the spiritual and moral formation of their children (Eph. 6:4; Gen. 18:19; Deut. 6:4–7).
The Book of Proverbs, for example, is framed as a series of teachings from a father to his son, integrating moral and spiritual guidance with practical skills for life:
Listen, my son, to your father’s instruction
and do not forsake your mother’s teaching.
They are a garland to grace your head
and a chain to adorn your neck. (1:8–9)
I am left puzzled at Bradley’s insistence that children are not the disciples or students of their parents. I grant that a father is not principally the teacher or pastor of his children. But teaching and shepherding certainly fall under the job description of being an engaged father.
This aspect of parenting is certainly made explicit in my faith tradition. I am an Anglican priest, who performs the rite of baptism from the 2019 Book of Common Prayer. When baptizing children who are unable to speak for themselves, the priest informs their Christian parents on their responsibility. The priest says:
Today, on behalf of this child, you shall make vows to renounce the devil and all his works, to trust God wholeheartedly, and to serve him faithfully. It is your task to see that this child is taught, as soon as he is able to learn, the meaning of all these vows, and of the Faith that you will profess as revealed in the Holy Scriptures. He must come to put his faith in Jesus Christ, and learn the Creeds, the Lord’s Prayer, the Ten Commandments, and all other things that a Christian ought to know, believe, and do for the welfare of his soul.
This sounds a lot like the stuff of discipleship to me. Unless I am misunderstanding Bradley, I think he is misguided in his strong insistence that fathers ought not consider their children to be their disciples or students.
To be fair, the focus of The Fatherhood Effect is on much more than the father’s role in faith formation. However, as a pastor, I was particularly interested in this topic and left disappointed, especially since he speaks to it in his introduction. (If readers are looking for more on this, they will need to look elsewhere; I recommend The Intentional Father by Jon Tyson, Emotionally Healthy Spirituality by Peter Scazzero, and last year’s The Redeemed Man as helpful resources.)
Still, Bradley’s systematic review of the research makes a compelling case that father absence is a root cause of many problems afflicting our children and our culture. His book explains not just the scope of the problem but the devastating consequences of disengaged or absent fathers.
Despite its focus on the tragic effects of father failure, this is ultimately a hopeful book. And though he is not perfect or exhaustive in his coverage, Bradley does three things especially well. He helps readers appreciate the irreplaceable role of dads. He inspires men to become better fathers or father figures. And he persuasively argues that organizations should redirect their energy and resources to focus on the root cause—rather than the symptoms—of father absence.
Bradley is clearly on to something: If you want to save the world, start by helping men become good dads.
Kevin Antlitz is a writer and an Anglican priest in Pittsburgh. He previously pastored in Washington, DC, and did campus ministry at Princeton University.
