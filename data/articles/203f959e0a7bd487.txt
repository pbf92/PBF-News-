TITLE: Landmark review recommends major shake-up to Australia's surrogacy laws
OUTLET: ABC News (Australia)

In short:
Surrogates should receive greater compensation and policies should be nationally consistent, under a proposed overhaul of the system.
Under the changes, intended parents would be legally recognised from birth, if a domestic arrangement was approved before conception.
What's next?
The federal government says it will consider the recommendations alongside the states and territories.
Surrogates would be entitled to greater financial compensation for the impacts of pregnancy and childbirth under sweeping changes proposed by the country's law reform body.
The wide-ranging final report from the Australian Law Reform Commission (ALRC) found Australia's patchwork of state and territory laws made surrogacy unnecessarily difficult, were driving parents overseas, could leave surrogates financially worse off and did not adequately safeguard the rights of children.
As part of its 47 recommendations, it said there should be one nationally consistent surrogacy framework overseen by a regulator and proposed that intended parents be recognised as legal parents from birth.
"Good surrogacy laws should do two things: enable people to form families through surrogacy where it can be undertaken responsibly; and provide effective safeguards against exploitation and harm. Our current laws do not achieve either aim," ALRC Justice Mordy Bromberg said.
"Instead, they drive Australians to pursue overseas surrogacy arrangements, which can expose children, surrogates, and intended parents to great risk of harm and exploitation."
The Attorney-General asked the ALRC to examine the nation's surrogacy laws and practices in 2024, with a view to protecting the rights of children born of surrogacy and providing Australians with more clarity and certainty.
It was also asked to consider how surrogacy arrangements made overseas are recognised under Australian law and what information a child born from surrogacy should have a right to know.
The report recommended Australia maintain its ban on commercial surrogacy and only allow altruistic surrogacy, which is when the surrogate is not paid a fee or allowed to profit.
Currently, surrogates can be reimbursed for certain expenses, although what costs can be covered can vary between the states and territories.
However the ALRC discussion paper said the current rules had left surrogates unable to fully recover their financial and non-financial costs and losses.
The report recommended a significant expansion of what can be paid to an altruistic surrogate, proposing intended parents would have to pay a broader range of costs, so a surrogate was not left worse off.
It also proposed a monthly payment for pregnancy capped at an amount set by the proposed national regulator, to address "non-economic" losses from pregnancy and childbirth, such as physical and psychological impacts.
However, the ALRC noted these changes would not count as a profit or reward, so the arrangement would be legally distinct from commercial surrogacy.
'Problematic laws' driving people overseas
Because commercial agreements are illegal, finding a surrogate can be a long and complicated process for many couples, leaving many reliant on family and friends.
For this reason, a growing number of Australians have entered commercial arrangements with a surrogate based overseas.
More than 300 Australian children are born through overseas surrogacy arrangements each year, accounting for an estimated four in five Australian surrogacy births according to the ALRC.
The commission said the "problematic nature" of Australian surrogacy laws was a major driver of intended parents going overseas "where the risk of harm and exploitation is at its highest".
The ALRC recommended a uniform legal and regulatory framework, either under Commonwealth laws or "substantially consistent" across state and territory laws.
The commission said that legislation should "establish and empower" a national regulator.
It also recommended replacing certain criminal offences relating to prohibited surrogacy arrangements with civil penalties, arguing they are easier to enforce and could be just as if not more effective than criminal sanctions in protecting against harm and exploitation.
Currently, the surrogate mother, and her partner depending on state law, are considered the legal parents of the child at birth and are listed as such on the child's birth certificate.
Intended parents must then seek a court order to transfer legal parentage which can be an expensive and lengthy process.
The review proposed that for a domestic surrogacy arrangement approved before conception, intended parents should be recognised as legal parents automatically from birth, rather than having to go to court to transfer parentage.
"The change in approach would help remove a potential barrier for people who may consider becoming surrogates but are concerned they could be left legally responsible for the child, and create a strong incentive for intended parents to pursue surrogacy domestically rather than overseas," it said.
The Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia' supported this proposal and in its submission earlier this year explained this would better reflect the parties' intentions.
"It will avoid the difficulties that intended parents currently experience after the birth of a child, where they do not have the authority to make decisions about the child's welfare…" the submission stated.
Review supports child's right to know
The review recommended making it much easier for children born through surrogacy to access information about who gave birth to them.
In most states and territories there is no system for a child born through surrogacy to access information about who gave birth to them, so the commission recommended a National Surrogacy Register for recording and accessing a surrogate's birth details.
Once 16, the child would have the right to access that information held on the register.
The inquiry recorded more than 460 submissions from individuals with lived experience of surrogacy arrangements, community groups, and government departments.
Several churches and religious groups opposed any move to make surrogacy easier and submitted that when a child was separated from its gestational mother, its rights to biological and emotional bonds were violated.
In contrast, Equality Australia submitted that the current surrogacy laws are "overly restrictive" and the consequences disproportionately affect LGBTQ+ families and single parents.
A federal government spokesperson said because the states and territories had primary responsibility for the regulation of surrogacy, the report would be considered in consultation with them.
"At this time, the Commonwealth has no plans to make legislative changes to surrogacy arrangements," they said.
