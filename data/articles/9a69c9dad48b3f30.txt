TITLE: Generative AI health advice use associated with higher uptake of selected childhood vaccines in Japan
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

A new Japanese study suggests that parents who regularly turn to generative artificial intelligence (AI) for general health advice may be more likely to have their children vaccinated against mumps, influenza and COVID-19. Drawing on a national internet survey of thousands of parents and children, researchers found higher vaccination uptake among children of regular AI health-advice users than among children of parents who did not use such tools regularly. The pattern largely held after accounting for earlier vaccination history and vaccine attitudes, but no clear association emerged for routine measles-rubella (MR) vaccination. The findings are exploratory and cannot show that AI advice itself influenced vaccination decisions.
Childhood vaccination remains a cornerstone of child health and infectious disease control. Parents increasingly seek child health information online, including through websites, mobile apps, social media and generative artificial intelligence (AI), which offers fast, conversational and tailored responses. Earlier evidence has come from vaccine-specific chatbots tested in controlled trials or short-term experiments, leaving little known about routine parental use of AI for everyday health advice and actual childhood vaccination across vaccines with different decision contexts. Few studies have examined whether such associations differ by parent age or persist after prior vaccination history and vaccine attitudes. Because of these gaps, in-depth research is needed on how routine parental generative AI health-advice use relates to real-world childhood vaccination uptake.
Researchers affiliated with the David Geffen School of Medicine at the University of California, Los Angeles, Harvard Medical School, Tohoku University Graduate School of Medicine and other institutions analyzed 2025 data from the Japan COVID-19 and Society Internet Survey. The nationwide online survey included 5,170 parents and their reports on vaccination for 8,282 children aged 1 to 17. The study was published (DOI: 10.1007/s12519-026-01107-5) in World Journal of Pediatrics on 15 October 2026. In a subset covering 2,521 children, the team linked responses to the 2023 survey to test whether the main findings were sensitive to additional adjustment for earlier parental vaccine attitudes and vaccination of the eldest child in the household, which served as a proxy for family vaccination history.
The study classified parents as regular users if they currently used generative AI and consulted it for health or medical information or advice at least a few times a month. The comparison group included people who had never used generative AI, former users and current users who consulted it less often or never for health advice. About 15% of the children had a parent classified as a regular user. After accounting for differences such as parent and child age and household income, estimated vaccination rates among children of regular users and those in the comparison group were 76.6% and 72.7% for mumps, 56.7% and 49.0% for influenza, and 40.6% and 30.9% for COVID-19. The main analysis found no clear difference in age-appropriate measles–rubella vaccination.
The authors said these findings should be read as exploratory observational associations rather than evidence that AI advice shaped parental decisions. They said regular AI health-advice use may instead mark broader digital health engagement, stronger health-information seeking or favourable preventive attitudes. They also cautioned that the survey did not measure vaccine-specific or child-specific AI use, or when it occurred relative to vaccination, and that healthy-user bias cannot be excluded. The larger COVID-19 estimate among parents under 30, they said, rests on a small subgroup and needs replication in prospective studies.
The survey measured how often parents sought general health advice from AI and relied on parents’ reports of their children’s vaccinations. It did not record whether parents asked about vaccines or their children’s health, or when they consulted AI relative to vaccination. Differences in how actively families seek health information or engage with preventive care could help explain the patterns. Future studies could record the timing and content of vaccine-related AI consultations and follow children’s vaccination histories using documented records to investigate whether the advice plays a role in parents’ decisions.
