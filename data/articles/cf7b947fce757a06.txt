TITLE: What’s really on the minds of CMOs versus their CEOs and CFOs?
OUTLET: https://www.thecurrent.com/

November 24
Pity today’s Chief Marketing Officer if they are at a major advertiser other than Google, Apple, Meta, Netflix, Amazon or one of the high-flying AI companies. For most other advertisers, there’s a good chance that revenue growth has been slow during the past 15 years..
For the typical advertiser, sales revenue has been a fraction of nominal GDP growth, barely keeping pace with either GDP productivity increases or inflation. Approximately two-thirds of major advertisers—including the likes of Conagra Brands, Altria, Kimberly-Clark, P&G, Unilever, Campbell’s, Colgate-Palmolive, General Mills, Clorox, Kenvue, Diageo, Pernod Ricard, Mondelēz, Reckitt, Heineken and many more— have suffered low growth for some time. Case volumes have been falling, and revenue growth, such as it is, has relied importantly on product price increases.
I discovered this low-growth phenomenon while researching for my third book about the advertising industry, Madison Avenue Revisited (2026), which looks at the evolution of media. I was curious about how media transformations had affected the sales performance of major brands. The low-growth of major advertisers surprised me, because sales growth is not frequently written about—either in major publications or in the trade press.
Somehow, given all the hype about targeted advertising, I expected to find a major uplift in brand sales growth once targeted advertising took off.
Instead, I found that brand growth performance began to stall in 2009, when the technological shift to targeted rather than mass marketing began to develop a head of steam. The slow revenue growth performance of major advertisers stood in sharp contrast to my expectations.
The low level of product growth rates is not a subject that CMOs brag about. Instead, they tend to highlight their expert management of marketing’s transformation to digitalization; their mastery of digital, social and programmatic advertising; and the evolution of their marketing approaches from mass media channels to highly targeted channels, using data and technology.
That said, the short tenure of CMOs in their current jobs is well-publicized. Consumer companies have the shortest average CMO tenure at 3.5 years, followed by healthcare at 3.9 years, according to published findings.
The low sales growth phenomenon helped me to understand the dilemma facing CMOs in their relationships with CEOs and CFOs. CEOs and CFOs hold CMOs responsible for the achievement of brand growth. When brand growth disappears, marketing spend starts to look like a pure cost rather than an investment for the future.
In low-growth companies, costs are routinely reduced, driven by the need to please Wall Street.
CMOs, conforming to the near-term need of CEOs and CFOs to reduce marketing costs, pursue low-cost advertising with their media and creative agencies. In this respect, CMOs serve their CEO and CFO masters.
The irony, though, is that CEOs and CFOs continue to expect marketing to deliver brand growth, and in the absence of actual growth, they routinely sack their CMOs.
CMOs, who now manage marketing spend cuts, accept brand low growth as a given, and as part of this, they lead “custodial marketing” efforts rather than “growth-focused marketing” efforts.
They do not expect their agencies to innovate by proposing brand growth plans. They do not genuinely analyze or modify their media or creative scopes of work to achieve higher brand growth rates.
They seem to have pulled in their horns, accepting the career-limiting outcome of low-growth performance. And at the end of 3-4 years with their companies, after they are let go, they seek new CMO positions where they can talk about their mastery of digitalization, programmatic and targeted advertising.
But this could all change if CEOs, CFOs, CMOs and procurement sat down to debate whether higher brand growth rates could be achieved if marketing programs were constructed along other lines. They’d have to dig into the reasons for the slowdown in product growth rates and develop confidence that their brands still have unrealized growth potential. If all of this deliberation and analysis came out positive, a CEO could spur the leadership of new growth programs, and the CFO, CMO, procurement and agencies could align their efforts in new ways.
That does not appear to be happening, though, and as a result, we find CMOs only partly aligned with their CEOs and CFOs — by delivering low-cost marketing and foregoing the opportunity to rekindle brand growth.
The real pity is for advertiser and agency employees, who can no longer depend on employment security in their low-cost companies. Rank and file employees have suffered much more than CMOs, who have been successful in moving from company to company every 3-4 years.
