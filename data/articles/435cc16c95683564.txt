TITLE: Home Office loses bid to hide that it served Apple a secret notice
OUTLET: PPC Land

The Investigatory Powers Tribunal today rejected the Home Office's argument that national security would be damaged if it confirmed whether it had given Apple a technical capability notice under the Investigatory Powers Act 2016, clearing the way for most of Apple's challenge to that notice to be heard in public.
In Short
A UK court that hears complaints about surveillance ruled today that the government can no longer keep secret the fact that it served Apple with a technical capability notice, a legal order under the Investigatory Powers Act 2016. The judges found that officials had already confirmed the notice to The Times and the Financial Times, and that the government had let Apple brief senior US officials, so official silence was protecting nothing. Most of Apple's legal challenge can now be argued in open court, although what the notice actually requires Apple to do stays secret for now.
Two claims, three judgments
The OPEN judgment, neutral citation [2026] UKIPTrib 9, runs to 24 pages and is dated 19 November 2026. It was given jointly by Lady Carmichael, the tribunal's President, Mr Justice Chamberlain and Mr Justice Johnson, after a hearing on 17 September 2026. It deals with two cases together. The first is Apple Inc's claim against the Secretary of State for the Home Department, case IPT/26/191/CH. The second, case IPT/25/83-86/CH, was brought against the same respondent by Privacy International, Liberty, Ian Hosein and Ben Wizner, whom the judgment calls "the NGOs" throughout.
Two further judgments, one PRIVATE and one CLOSED, were handed down at the same time. Those will not be published. According to the tribunal, "The substance of our decision and reasoning is set out in this judgment. The PRIVATE and CLOSED judgments contain additional details that cannot be published without damaging the public interest."
The dispute has a history. Apple first brought a claim against the Home Secretary in early 2025. At that stage, according to the judgment, the Home Office argued that the existence of the claim itself had to be kept secret and that any hearing should not appear in the daily case list. The tribunal disagreed. It published a listing that withheld the parties' names, heard argument, and then, in OPEN, PRIVATE and CLOSED judgments dated 7 April 2025, rejected the government's request to keep both the identities of the parties and the subject matter secret. That is how it became public that Apple had sued the Home Secretary over the power to give a "relevant operator" a technical capability notice.
The NGOs filed their claim shortly afterwards, and it was listed for a full hearing in December 2026. Apple's first claim, meanwhile, was dismissed "because of a change of circumstances" - a phrase the judgment does not explain further. In July 2026, Apple started a second claim.
A case management hearing was then listed, partly to decide whether a December 2026 trial was still realistic. It was not. According to the judgment, the parties agree that a substantive hearing in December 2026 is impractical and indicate that they are likely to agree directions "with a view to a substantive hearing in the first half of 2027."
The question put to the three judges was narrower than the case as a whole, but it shapes everything that follows. Would it damage the public interest to reveal whether the Home Secretary has given Apple a technical capability notice, and, if so, what it contains? The answer decides how much of the eventual trial takes place in public, and how much evidence can be exchanged in OPEN rather than PRIVATE or CLOSED sessions.
What each side argued
The government relied on its long-standing policy known as neither confirm nor deny, or NCND. According to the judgment, "It is in furtherance of this policy that the Government has publicly neither confirmed nor denied whether Apple has been given a technical capability notice."
Neil Sheldon KC, for the Home Secretary, argued that NCND only works if it is applied consistently, and that "Official confirmation or denial of the existence of a technical capability notice in this case would create a damaging precedent for future national security cases." He drew a distinction between media speculation and formal confirmation by government, and asked the tribunal to give substantial weight to the Home Office's own assessment of the risk.
Ben Jaffey KC, for the NGOs, argued that NCND was "no longer tenable" in the light of the reporting and official statements. The sequence of events, in his account, told its own story: Apple's first claim was dismissed and a second one brought, which mirrored public reports that a notice had been issued, withdrawn under US pressure and then reissued. The judgment records his submission that "it follows as a matter of irresistible inference that it is now in the public domain that the Secretary of State has given Apple a technical capability notice."
Daniel Beard KC, for Apple, was, according to the tribunal, "constrained in what he could say in OPEN pending the Tribunal's resolution of this issue." He asked for permission for Apple to refer openly to "certain key facts", including whether a notice had in fact been given. Without that permission, he argued, Apple "would otherwise be unfairly prevented from publicly advancing facts it knows to be true."
Jason Beer KC, appointed as counsel to the tribunal, made no substantive submissions in OPEN. In the PRIVATE session, according to the judgment, he backed the NGOs' position and argued that the government's PRIVATE and CLOSED evidence did not show that disclosure would damage national security. He also pointed out that the respondent's documents explaining the true position were marked "OFFICIAL SENSITIVE" rather than "SECRET" or "TOP SECRET", which he said was inconsistent with the claim that disclosure would be damaging.
The Home Office's evidence
The government's case rested on two witness statements. The first, from Lucy Montgomery-Pott, Head of the Investigatory Powers Unit in the Home Office's Homeland Security Group, was served for the April 2025 hearing and only in PRIVATE. She said there had been an investigation into The Times's reference to Home Office sources confirming the order, and that "no Government source has been identified".
The tribunal was unimpressed by what that statement left out. According to the judgment, she "provided no detail of the investigation undertaken and no copy of any investigation report. She did not identify who had been investigated, or what investigative steps had been taken (for example whether electronic devices had been examined)." Nor did she say whether ministers, senior officials or special advisers had been asked if anyone authorised a briefing.
The second witness was Andrew Scurry, Director of Technology, Data and Innovation in the Homeland Security Group, who gave OPEN and PRIVATE statements dated 11 September 2026. He adopted Ms Montgomery-Pott's views and argued that "the NCND principle is only effective when it is maintained consistently." He also said that "Ministers and Home Office officials have consistently neither confirmed nor denied the existence, contents, scope or operational effect of any individual TCN."
The judges' response to that sentence was blunt: "If that sentence were true, then the statements in The Times and The Financial Times to the contrary effect would be false."
On the later Financial Times articles, Mr Scurry said that "Both of these Financial Times articles were subject to a leak investigation by Government Security Group." Again, according to the tribunal, "no further detail is given of what this investigation involved, nor indeed of what (if anything) it revealed."
One absence carried particular weight. The government's evidence, the judgment notes, "does not include any assessment that damage had been caused to national security" as a result of the tribunal's April 2025 decision to publish the existence of Apple's claim and its subject matter.

Why the tribunal set the Home Office's assessment aside
UK courts normally give national security assessments by the executive considerable deference. The judgment restates the standard position from Secretary of State for the Home Department v Rehman and later cases: such assessments "should usually be accepted, unless they are shown to be irrational or otherwise vitiated by a public law error."
That deference has conditions. The tribunal quoted the Divisional Court in Attorney General v BBC; R (Beth) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2025] EWHC 1669 (KB) - a panel on which Mr Justice Chamberlain also sat - at paragraph 19: "this respect is warranted only if, and to the extent that, the courts can have confidence without reservation in the processes by which such assessments are prepared and presented."
NCND, the judgment adds, "is not an absolute or blanket rule of law". It quoted the Court of Appeal in Mohamed v Secretary of State for the Home Department: "It is not simply a matter of a governmental party to litigation hoisting the NCND flag and the court automatically saluting it". And there is a further point the tribunal treated as decisive: "If the Government itself has not been consistent in maintaining NCND in respect of a particular matter, the court is unlikely to give weight to an assertion that a departure from NCND would be damaging to national security."
