TITLE: Google Puts AI Above Ads: 10 New AI Mode Ad Markets
OUTLET: shattered.io

Google is putting AI-generated answers above traditional search results and above ads in some searches, according to The New York Times and Engadget. For a company that built its business on ads sitting next to a list of links, that is a structural change. The new Google AI Mode ads are the company’s answer to the obvious question: where does the money go when the answer comes first?
Google says the money follows the user. It has launched multiturn ads in the United States, expanded AI Mode advertising to 10 more English-language countries, and named three more markets for the fourth quarter of 2026. What it has not given is a price, a guaranteed placement, or any figure on how advertiser performance is changing.
This analysis sorts what Google has confirmed from what remains open, and it frames the shift for the engineers, marketers and publishers who live downstream of Search. The pressure is already visible elsewhere: three big US school districts are weighing whether to drop Google search over its AI features, which shows how far the redesign reaches beyond the ad auction.
What Google Changed in Search This Month
The core change is about order. For most of Google’s history, a query returned ads and then a ranked list of web pages. In some searches today, an AI-generated response now sits above both. The New York Times and Engadget described the move as Google putting AI responses ahead of traditional results and ads, and the framing matters because the top of the page is the most valuable real estate Google owns.
Two products carry the shift. AI Overviews appear at the top of some Google Search results pages. AI Mode goes a step further and lets people keep talking to a chatbot after the first AI-generated answer. One is a summary on the page. The other is a conversation that can run several turns, and each turn is a chance to show an ad.
Google has not published a full count of how many searches show these features, and the sources behind this story do not provide one. The company says only that the AI responses appear in some searches. That vague phrasing is typical for a rollout that is still being tuned, and readers should treat any specific “share of queries” figure floating around the web as unconfirmed.
The practical effect is easy to describe even without a percentage. A person who once typed a query, scanned four ads and clicked a link may now read a synthesized answer first. Whether they scroll down to the old results, ask a follow-up, or stop right there is the central unknown for everyone who earns traffic from Google.
How AI Overviews and AI Mode Differ
The two features get lumped together in headlines, but they behave differently and they create different ad opportunities. AI Overviews are a one-shot block at the top of a results page. You see a generated answer, and the familiar links sit underneath. The interaction ends when you click or leave.
AI Mode is a chat surface. After the first answer, the user can type a follow-up, narrow the question, or switch direction. Google’s own description is that people can continue interacting with a chatbot after the AI-generated answer. That continuity is what makes it commercially interesting, because shopping and research questions rarely end after a single exchange.
Think about a typical purchase journey. Someone asks about a laptop for coding, then asks about battery life, then asks about price ranges, then asks which store to use. In the old model each of those was a separate search with a separate ad auction. In AI Mode they can be one running thread, and Google needs a way to monetize the thread without wrecking the experience.
That is the design problem behind the multiturn ads Google has now launched. The format is built for a conversation, not a query. For developers who build on top of search data, the distinction is important, because a conversation does not produce the same signals as a keyword. There is no single query string to log, and a thread can drift far from its first words.
Google AI Mode Ads: What the Company Confirmed
Google has put a handful of facts on the record about Google AI Mode ads. They are worth listing plainly, because much of the surrounding commentary adds detail the company has not supplied.
Sponsored labels and commercial intent
Google says AI Mode can show relevant Search ads when a query has commercial intent. In other words, a question about the history of a programming language should not trigger an ad, while a question about which cloud storage plan to buy might. Google also says ads in AI Mode carry a “Sponsored” label, which keeps the paid content visibly separate from the generated answer.
The label is the minimum viable disclosure. It tells the reader which parts of the page someone paid for, and it is the same basic principle that has governed search ads for years. What the label does not settle is how closely an ad can sit to the text of an AI answer, or how a reader should weigh a sponsored suggestion that appears inside a conversational reply.
Multiturn ads in follow-up questions
Google launched multiturn ads in the United States. These let ads appear in follow-up questions within an AI Mode conversation, not only in the first reply. That is the feature that turns a chatbot session into an ad surface that can refresh as the conversation moves.
Google’s official advertising guidance adds a useful phrase on placement. It says ads may appear “within or alongside” AI-generated responses. The word “may” carries weight. It describes a possibility, not a promise, and no source in this reporting confirms that any advertiser can buy a guaranteed position in an AI response.
The 10-Country Expansion and the Q4 2026 Queue
Beyond the US launch, Google expanded AI Mode advertising to 10 additional English-language countries: Australia, Canada, India, Kenya, Malaysia, New Zealand, Nigeria, Pakistan, the Philippines and Singapore. It also announced plans to bring AI Mode advertising to Brazil, Mexico and the United Kingdom in the fourth quarter of 2026. The table below lays out the rollout as Google described it.
The country list tells a story on its own. Nine of the ten new markets are outside North America, spread across Asia, Africa and Oceania, and the group includes India and Nigeria, two places where search use and mobile commerce are large and growing. Google chose English-language markets first, which plausibly lowers the translation and policy risk of a new ad format, though the company has not explained its reasoning. The Q4 2026 additions bring in Portuguese and Spanish speakers for the first time.
Note what is missing. Google gave no revenue figure for any of these markets and no count of advertisers using the format. The announcement describes reach, not results. That gap is typical of a product in its early commercial phase, and it leaves analysts to infer demand from the speed of the rollout rather than from any hard number.

The key row is the second one. When the AI answer lands above both the ads and the links, the old hierarchy flips. An advertiser who paid for the top slot in a classic results page may now find that slot pushed beneath a generated paragraph. Google has not said how often that happens, and it has not said whether advertisers get a say.
The phrase “within or alongside” also deserves a careful read. Alongside suggests the familiar sidebar or adjacent unit. Within suggests something closer to a native mention inside the generated text. Google’s guidance allows both, which gives the company room to test formats while telling advertisers very little about where they will land on any given query.
What Google Says About Query Growth
Google’s public position is that AI is making Search bigger, not smaller. Google spokesperson Thomas Schoenfelder said: “AI is driving an expansionary moment for Search, with our AI features leading to increased usage and overall query growth, including commercial queries.” The final clause is the one advertisers will underline, since commercial queries are the ones that carry ads.
Schoenfelder also addressed the money question directly. He said: “Our ads experiments have shown promising results, and we’re confident about our trajectory.” That is a confident statement with no number attached. Google did not say what the experiments measured, how long they ran, or which advertisers took part, and the reporting does not fill those blanks.
Read together, the two statements make a coherent defense. If AI features raise usage, more queries means more chances for commercial intent. If commercial queries grow, the ad inventory grows with them. The argument is logical, but it rests on company-reported trends that outside analysts cannot check from the public record.
Skeptics have a reasonable counterpoint. A generated answer can satisfy a question without a click, and fewer clicks can mean fewer ad impressions that matter. Google’s reply, in effect, is that conversation creates new moments for ads that the old results page never offered. Both views can be true at once, and the data that would settle it sits inside Google.

No source gives a specific dollar price for AI Mode ads, no source confirms a guaranteed ad placement, and none states an exact revenue impact or a precise percentage decline in advertiser performance.

If you see it repeated as settled fact, check the original source first.

Why Advertisers Face a Measurement Problem
Search advertising grew up on a clean loop. A user types a keyword, an advertiser bids on it, a click lands on a page, and a conversion gets credited. Each step leaves a trail. Conversational ads blur nearly every link in that chain, which is why measurement may be the hardest practical problem for marketers moving budget into AI Mode.
Start with the keyword. In a multi-turn conversation, the thing that triggers an ad may be a follow-up like “what about the cheaper one,” which means nothing outside its context. An advertiser cannot bid on that phrase in the old sense. Google decides relevance from the whole thread and from the commercial intent it detects, and the advertiser mostly trusts the system.
Next comes attribution. If a person sees an ad in the third turn of a chat and buys a week later through a different channel, which touchpoint earns the credit? The existing models were built around discrete queries. Marketers will need to test, compare against a clean baseline, and resist the urge to trust a single dashboard.
The lack of public benchmarks makes this harder. Google calls its experiments promising, but no cited source publishes conversion rates, cost per click, or lift for AI Mode. Teams that want to know whether the format pays will have to measure it themselves, which is exactly where engineering help matters.
