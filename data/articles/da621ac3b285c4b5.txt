TITLE: Google says 80% confidence is good enough for directional ad tests
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google's Ads Decoded podcast published a 48-minute episode on November 18 in which experiments product lead Sagar Shah and search experiments specialist Al Nevarez explained how Google Ads splits traffic in its campaign tests, how it decides whether a result is statistically significant, and how a newer test of budget increases runs each arm on half of its budget. Host Ginny Marvin closed with a rule of thumb that Google's Ads & Commerce Blog repeated today: an 80% confidence interval for directional changes, and 95% for major structural ones.
In Short
Google used an episode of its Ads Decoded podcast to explain how the experiments built into Google Ads work, from the statistics underneath them to newer tests for budgets, bid targets and AI Max. It matters to anyone who raises a budget or switches on Google's automation, because these tests are Google's answer to whether a change actually worked before it is rolled out across an account. Google's guidance is to start with its pre-built templates, give a test four to six weeks and accept 80% confidence for small changes. Google's own help pages do not fully agree with the episode on test length or confidence levels, and support for shared budgets in experiments is still a pilot.

Experiments in Google Ads let you validate everything from new features like AI Max for Search to daily budget shifts and changes in your Smart Bidding strategies," according to the page. "Trust us" and "prove it"
Marvin framed the episode with a line from Nevarez. "And for me, the overarching theme of this conversation really comes down to something that Al said, to use experiments to move from trust us to prove it," she said. Experimentation, in her words, "isn't just a nice to have, it's really both a safety check and a strategic compass."
Nevarez gave the original version when asked why experiments are worth running. "At the end of the day, I guess it's, it basically comes down to going from trust us to prove it," he said. The platform provides "that scientific rigor in the experiment, um, so you don't have to bring the lab coat to get kind of the lab grade truth from your test."
Shah was more direct about the commercial subtext. "At the end of the day, we wanna make sure that advertisers have the tools they need in order to be able to sort of validate that the features that we build, um, and the sort of the platform is able to do a good job for them," he said.
The arrangement is worth stating plainly. The features under test are Google's, the auction they run in is Google's, and the arithmetic that decides whether they worked is Google's too.
The statistics under the hood
Every system for A/B testing has to answer the same question: whether the gap between two groups is larger than chance alone would produce. Marvin said Google does not "often talk a lot about the methodology" and described the model as "jackknife resampling to calculate confidence inter- intervals and statistical significance in our AB experiments."
The null hypothesis
Using conversions as the example metric, Nevarez said that "an AB experiment platform calculates the probability that the observed uplift in conversions would have occurred if there was actually no difference between the control and the treatment." Put more briefly: "It's the probability that the data matches the null hypothesis." Because advertisers hope a change causes a real increase, "we'd like that conditional probability to be very low, really low."
He described three steps: "Number one, calculate the variance of the data. Number two, calculate the standard error, and number three, calculate the confidence intervals." The jackknife is Google's method for the first step, he said. (The transcript renders the method here as "jackknife-free sampling," a recognition error.) "The jackknife is a versatile method that effectively detects outliers and helps reduce bias," Nevarez said. "It also includes bucketing of the traffic, um, to further increase the accuracy of those confidence intervals we calculate in step three."
Buckets and outliers
According to Nevarez, the split randomisation "together with the jackknife ensures that any given user will be consistently assigned to the same controller treatment arm, and they will also be consistently assigned to the same bucket during that trial." ("Controller treatment" is the transcript's rendering of "control or treatment.") The purpose is protection against unusual users. "Imagine if there's a user who converts many times on your campaign or he has an exceptionally high conversion value," he said. "Here, the jackknife process will protect us against that outlier, uh, thereby helping us avoid false positive results later on."
The resampling is what statisticians call leave-one-out. It means "recalculating the experiment results many, many times, each time leaving out a bucket or some of the users," Nevarez said. "So if the variance changes a lot, when I remove a couple people, that's an indication it's probably an outlier and the system will detect that."
Shah added the comparison with the usual alternative. Without an A/B test, advertisers typically run "a pre-post of some sort and see how things moved," which "gets you into challenges of seasonality or other reasons that may be sort of, you know, not controlled for." A test that misses significance, he said, is "just basically saying, 'Hey, you're seeing a difference between control and treatment, but we don't know that that difference is anything other than noise, right?'"
What the help page adds
The help article linked from the episode, titled "The statistical methodology behind experiments," supplies figures the podcast left out. It states that "Jackknife resampling is applied to bucketed data to calculate the sample variance of the percent change of a metric," and that "Twenty buckets are used in the control arm and twenty buckets are used in the treatment arm." It calls the jackknife "the standard at Google."
Two points in that documentation sit awkwardly next to the episode. First, the page says that "Two-tailed significance testing is then run using the 95% confidence interval," a single fixed level, while the episode and a separate help page describe a choice of levels with 80% as the default. Second, the page distinguishes two kinds of split. In a search-based split, users are placed in either arm at random each time a search occurs; in a cookie-based split, a user stays on one version however many times they search. Nevarez's description of users being "consistently assigned" fits only the second.
The page also says advertisers cannot rebuild the buckets or run the jackknife on aggregated experiment data themselves, and that Google has "no internal tools to do so on behalf of our clients." None of the three Google help pages cited in this article displayed a publication or update date as of November 19.
Templated or custom
When advertisers open the experiments page, Marvin said, "they are presented with options for pre-built goal-based experiments or the option to build custom experiments from scratch." Nevarez said custom experiments let an advertiser "adjust anything you like in that treatment arm," with a caveat: "if you adjust too many levers in this custom experiment, you may arrive at the end of your experiment and you're not really sure which lever caused the change."
Templates test "one thing at a time," and they work differently underneath. "Instead of creating, for example, a new campaign copy or a draft as we do with the custom experiments, we're actually splitting the traffic fifty fifty within the single campaign," Nevarez said. "This helps sometimes to minimize errors, and this approach may also lead to a reduced learning period because you're acting on that same campaign that you started with."
Shah separated "big decisions," such as changes to account structure or measurement setup, from "day-to-day optimizations, which is, 'Hey, how should I change my creative or what roast target should I set? Or what happens if I tweak my budget a little bit?'" ("Roast" is the transcript's rendering of ROAS.) The templates are recent, he said: "We've only had custom options for a long time, right?"
Sync and what the system cannot know
Custom experiments carry their own maintenance problem. Marvin said the sync feature "is on by default for custom search and display experiments" and is "supported by the APIs as well as in the UI." Shah said Google introduced it "four or five years ago now" because of "experiment arms that were just mismatched." In "a six to eight week experiment," he said, "sometimes longer you forget that this thing is running and so you, you change something and then that invariably ends up distorting the results of the experiment."
Sync copies changes made to the control campaign over to the treatment campaign, and it can only be switched on at the start. "We, we can only set it up at the very beginning of running an experiment because that's when both the, the arms are matched and we know what should be synced and what shouldn't be synced," Shah said. The constraint reflects a wider one. With a custom experiment, "our systems don't fundamentally have a deep understanding of the exact set of things that you're trying to test," he said. Google can see that a bidding strategy differs between arms, "But did you intend to actually test that or was that something that just happened because the arms are mismatched? It's not something we, we'll always know."
