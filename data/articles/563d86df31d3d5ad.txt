TITLE: Six arrested for allegedly damaging, illegally excavating archaeological site
OUTLET: The Jerusalem Post

Six people suspected of stealing antiquities and damaging an archaeological site were arrested last weekend, the Israel Antiquities Authority (IAA) announced on Wednesday.
The suspected thieves were caught by the IAA’s Theft Prevention Unit while allegedly illegally excavating an underground ancient cistern at the Horvat Adash site, near the town of Mevo Beitar in the Judean Hills.
Horvat Adash is a declared archaeological site, containing the remains of an ancient settlement that existed for almost 1,000 years, stretching from the Second Temple period in the 1st century BCE to the Early Muslim period in the 8th century CE.
Archaeologists previously discovered a hewn double-entrance public purification bath, building remains, water systems, industrial installations, and an Early Muslim period public building that served as a mosque.
The six had arrived at the site equipped with extensive excavation equipment, including a generator, an electric jackhammer, augers, digging tools, a long ladder, and lighting equipment.
Numerous ancient artifacts were recovered from within the cistern, including fragments of hundreds of pottery vessels, several stone vessels, and a fragment of a zoomorphic vessel with a neck shaped like an animal dating back to the Early Muslim period.
Due to the large quantity of finds from different periods discovered in the cistern, researchers believe the cistern had at some point in antiquity ceased being used for its original function and became a refuse pit.
Authorities suspect that while some members of the group were digging nine meters below ground inside the cistern, the others stayed on aboveground next to a lit campfire, attempting to disguise the activity as a picnic.
Ancient cistern irreversibly damaged
The illegal excavation caused irreversible damage to the site.
Extensive signs of destruction were evident in the area, which the suspects allegedly left during the break-in to the ancient cistern.
Inside the cistern itself, its walls and floor were damaged, breaking ancient plaster layers that had long been preserved in place. The IAA believes that the suspects hoped to find a treasure in the cistern.
The six were arrested on suspicion of damaging an antiquities site, a crime for which the statutory penalty is up to five years in prison, and were then taken to the police station and interrogated.
Upon completion of the investigation, the case will be transferred to the prosecuter's office to consider filing an indictment.
Pushing back against antiquities looting
“Unfortunately, legends about ancient treasures hidden in archaeological sites have recently become widespread on social media. These stories encourage people to go out and dig without a permit at archaeological sites, hoping to get rich quickly,” explained Dr. Amir Ganor, director of the IAA’s Antiquities Theft Prevention Unit.
“However, the reality is completely different: illegal excavations cause irreversible damage to archaeological sites and findings. Ancient layers are destroyed; findings are displaced and disconnected from their archaeological context. That causes valuable historical information to be lost, which can never be restored.”
“It is important that those who see treasure hunting as an innocent adventure should understand that this is a serious criminal offense, which may result in investigation, prosecution, heavy fines and even imprisonment,” Ganor said. “The damage caused to archaeological sites is damage to the heritage and history that belong to us all.”
Heritage Minister Amichai Eliyahu stated, “The ancient sites of the Land of Israel are not a free-for-all. We will not allow these sites to be turned into a playground for treasure hunters at the expense of our shared public heritage.”
“The destroyers of history must and will be brought to justice. We will continue to fight looting, protect our cultural assets, and preserve the history of the Land of Israel for future generations.”
