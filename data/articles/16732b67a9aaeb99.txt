TITLE: Study links higher vitamin D levels to better sleep
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Higher vitamin D levels were associated with longer and less interrupted sleep in middle-aged and older adults, in a study led by researchers at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health in collaboration with scientists at the National Institute on Aging.
The researchers analyzed vitamin D levels from blood tests and sleep data from wrist-worn actigraphs in 460 individuals being followed in an ongoing study. Higher vitamin D levels were associated with longer total sleep times and shorter intervals of waking, while vitamin D deficiency was associated with shorter total sleep times.
The study was published online October 14 in The Journal of Gerontology: Medical Sciences.
“These findings suggest that we should investigate more thoroughly whether boosting vitamin D levels can improve sleep health,” says study senior author Adam Spira, PhD, MA, professor in the Department of Mental Health at the Bloomberg School and a core faculty member of the Johns Hopkins Center on Aging and Health.
The study’s first author, Diefei Chen, is a PhD candidate in the Bloomberg School’s Department of Epidemiology.
The authors also note that the study is serial cross-sectional—a series of snapshots over time—and does not establish a causal relationship between vitamin D levels and sleep quality.  
For older adults, sleep problems are common and have been associated with higher risks of dementia, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and other conditions. Vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency is also very common in older adults, and has been linked to worse sleep quality in prior studies. Some clinical trials of vitamin D supplementation have suggested a benefit for sleep, though those trials have been small and homogeneous.
Chen, Spira, and their colleagues conducted the current study in a relatively large and diverse group, with actigraph-based measures of sleep quality and blood-based vitamin D levels. The study participants were 460 men and women, average age about 71, who were assessed during 2012-19 as part of the National Institute on Aging Intramural Research Program’s long-running Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging.
Sleep and vitamin D levels tend to vary with age, race, sex, and other variables; after adjusting for these potential confounding factors, the researchers found significant associations between higher serum vitamin D concentration and better sleep quality. For example, when they divided participants into three groups based on vitamin D levels, those in the highest-level group slept almost 20 minutes longer per night on average, and had shorter average intervals of waking than those in the lowest-level group.
Similarly, when they grouped participants according to standard clinical definitions of vitamin D levels—deficiency, insufficiency, and normal—the vitamin D–deficient group had, on average, about 23 minutes less total sleep time.
The links between vitamin D levels and sleep measures seemed strongest among Black participants.
“Black adults in the United States have a higher prevalence of vitamin D deficiency and sleep disturbances compared to white adults, and may benefit more from increasing vitamin D levels,” Chen says.
The researchers also tested associations between sleep quality and vitamin B12 levels, since prior studies have hinted that B12 might have an impact on sleep. Women with higher B12 levels had less time awake after they first fell asleep on average, while, among men, the associations seemed to go in the opposite direction—higher levels of B12 were linked to more time awake after falling asleep. The statistical evidence regarding these results remained inconclusive.
The authors note that the study has several limitations. Vitamin sources—dietary intake, sunlight exposure for vitamin D, or supplementation—could not be distinguished. Future studies could examine whether the observed associations differ by vitamin source. Another promising direction is to investigate associations between vitamin D levels and longitudinal sleep trajectories.
The researchers say the results add weight to the idea that maintaining healthy vitamin D levels may improve sleep.
“Association of Vitamin D and B12 Levels with Actigraphic Sleep in Cognitively Unimpaired Middle-Aged and Older Adults” was co-authored by Diefei Chen, Jill Rabinowitz, Fan Wu, Zhikui Wei, Yang An, Jennifer Schrack, Toshiko Tanaka, Chee Chia, Mark Wu, Sarah Wanigatunga, Luigi Ferrucci, Eleanor Simonsick, and Adam Spira.
