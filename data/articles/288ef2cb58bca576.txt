TITLE: Defeated Teaneck councilwoman files 770-page campaign finance complaint against rival slate
OUTLET: New Jersey Globe

Teaneck Councilwoman Hillary Goldberg, who finished seventh in her bid for re-election earlier this month, has filed a 770-page complaint with the New Jersey Election Law Enforcement Commission accusing a rival council slate of violating state campaign finance laws, including allegations involving more than $75,000 in contributions, unreported advertising and questionable payments to candidates.
The ten-count complaint names One Team One Teaneck, the joint campaign of Avi Berliner, Sharon Kolb and Amrit Ramjattan, along with its treasurer, Jacob Zusya Brodsky, and chairman, Emanuel Klein.
Goldberg is seeking an investigation into the campaign’s finances, including whether political contributions were deposited into a bank account belonging to a separate nonprofit corporation, whether advertising expenses were properly disclosed, and whether an outside political organization illegally coordinated its activities with the candidates.
She also wants ELEC to subpoena bank statements, advertising contracts, invoices and campaign communications, and impose civil penalties if violations are found.
Berliner was the only member of the slate elected on November 3, finishing fourth in a nine-candidate field for four seats on the nonpartisan Township Council.
George Fernandez Jr. led the field with 7,371 votes, followed by Denise Belcher with 7,343, Duane Harley with 6,944, and Berliner with 6,715.
Kolb finished fifth, just 141 votes behind Berliner, with 6,574 votes. Ramjattan was sixth with 6,391, while Goldberg received 3,839 votes, finishing 2,876 votes behind Berliner.
The results, which include ballots counted through November 16, remain unofficial.
The complaint includes hundreds of pages of campaign finance reports, corporate filings, newspaper advertisements and social media records. Some of the allegations involve discrepancies in reports filed with ELEC, while others would require investigators to obtain records that have not been made public.
Bank account, PAC contributions and payments to candidates
The largest financial issue involves a Flagstar Bank account the campaign used to receive $75,555 in contributions and pay $74,699.41 in expenses through October 26.
Goldberg alleges that the account belonged to One Team One Teaneck Inc., a nonprofit corporation established on July 1, rather than the political committee registered with ELEC. Corporate records identify Berliner as the nonprofit’s incorporator and registered agent, with Berliner, Kolb, and Brodsky listed as trustees.
The campaign registered with ELEC on June 22 without identifying a bank account and did not disclose the Flagstar account until July 31, despite reporting financial transactions before that date. Its earliest reported expenditure was a $95 payment to Campaign Verify on June 18, four days before the campaign committee registered and nearly two weeks before the nonprofit was incorporated.
Goldberg contends the bank account disclosure was 14 to 32 days late, depending on whether the date of the first contribution or first expenditure is used.
She is asking ELEC to determine when the account was opened, who controlled it, and whether campaign funds were deposited into an account owned by the nonprofit.
Brodsky, whose reported residence is in San Francisco, is also accused of failing to file a required consent to service of legal process in New Jersey.
Another allegation concerns a $5,000 contribution from Raymond Wiss, Teaneck’s appointed labor counsel, that the campaign received on August 4 but did not disclose until October 23.
The contribution was added in a second amendment to the campaign’s 29-day pre-election report, 80 days after it was received.
Earlier that day, the campaign had amended the same report to include an $850 payment to Pasta Factory LLC for food and beverages at a fundraiser.
That filing showed a negative bank balance of $19.64. When the Wiss contribution was added, the reported balance increased to $4,980. Goldberg also questions a $5,000 loan from Klein, dated October 20, that remained outstanding on the campaign’s October 26 report.
The complaint raises separate questions about $7,500 contributed by Prosperity PAC, which gave One Team One Teaneck $4,500 on September 16 and another $3,000 on October 5. Klein had previously contributed $15,250 to Advance Together PAC between August 2025 and April 2026. Advance Together gave $3,700 to Prosperity PAC on May 12.
Goldberg is asking investigators to determine whether money originating with Klein was routed through the two political committees before reaching the Teaneck campaign.
The contributions themselves do not establish that any money was improperly routed.
The campaign’s financial reports also contain a $2,937.66 discrepancy in the final days before the election, according to the complaint.
An amended October 26 report showed $855.59 remaining in the campaign account. On November 2, the campaign disclosed $3,793.25 in additional expenditures without reporting any new contributions on that notice. Debit transactions to Scale to Win and Meta totaling $2,658.99 alone exceeded the previously reported balance by $1,803.40.
The complaint asks whether the campaign received unreported money, incurred unpaid obligations, or failed to properly account for its expenditures. Goldberg also challenges a $7,209 reimbursement to Kolb on October 5.
The payment included $4,265 for billboards and lawn signs and $2,640.46 for fundraising consulting services. The campaign listed Kolb as the recipient without identifying the original vendors, according to the complaint.
Goldberg argues that money advanced by Kolb should have been reported as a loan until it was reimbursed.
She also questions who received the consulting payment. If Kolb hired an outside consultant, the vendor should have been disclosed, she contends. If Kolb retained the money for her own fundraising services, Goldberg argues that it could raise questions about the personal use of campaign funds.
The complaint does not establish which, if either, occurred.
Another $1,167 in candidate-related debit expenditures is also questioned, along with payments to Election Day workers.
Several campaign finance reports were filed late, Goldberg said.
The 29-day pre-election report was submitted at 10:48 PM on October 5, while the 11-day report was filed at 5:36 PM on October 23. Both missed the 5 PM deadline.
An October 26 notice disclosing $8,000 in contributions received three days earlier was filed at 10:29 PM. Whether that exceeded the 72-hour reporting deadline depends on when the contributions were received.
Goldberg also alleges that transactions from October 22 and 23 were improperly included in an amended 11-day report rather than disclosed during the appropriate reporting period.
Newspaper advertising and outside political support
The complaint devotes considerable attention to advertising in The Jewish Link, a newspaper co-published by Teaneck Mayor Mark Schwartz.
Goldberg identified advertisements for One Team One Teaneck appearing on at least nine pages between August 6 and October 29, including a two-page spread and a front-page teaser.
The campaign reported paying the newspaper $1,500. Using the newspaper’s published advertising rates, Goldberg estimated that the space would have cost $8,365, a difference of $6,865.
But that calculation does not account for volume discounts or negotiated advertising packages.
In an October 20 exchange included in the complaint, Jewish Link co-publisher Moshe Kinderlehrer said the campaign had made a larger advertising commitment.
“The One Teaneck campaign committed to many ads upfront, including inserts and multiple double full page spreads, so I wouldn’t use the filings to determine your pricing,” Kinderlehrer wrote.
Goldberg wants ELEC to obtain the newspaper’s advertising contracts and invoices to determine what the campaign actually paid and whether it received any discounts or other benefits that should have been disclosed as contributions.
She also identified advertisements on at least seven pages of the Jewish Standard without corresponding expenditures in the campaign’s reports.
Advertising provided free of charge or at below-market rates can constitute an in-kind contribution under New Jersey campaign finance law.
Some campaign advertisements also omitted, shortened, or improperly displayed the political identification statements required by state law, Goldberg alleges.
The campaign’s Facebook and Instagram advertising is another subject of the complaint.
According to Goldberg, Meta’s advertising library shows 30 paid advertisements sponsored by One Team One Teaneck between September 8 and November 5, with minimum spending of $2,900.
The campaign reported $2,251 in Meta payments, including charges paid directly and through political consultants.
Goldberg says at least $300 in advertising had appeared by September 17, although the campaign’s 29-day pre-election report showed no payments or outstanding obligations to Meta. She is asking ELEC to examine whether consultants or another organization paid for the advertising and whether those expenses were properly disclosed. Some advertising charges incurred after November 2 would not have been due until the campaign’s post-election reporting period.
The complaint also targets the Bergen County Jewish Action Committee, a nonprofit organization established in November 2023 that supported the slate.
Goldberg alleges that the organization provided political support that should have been disclosed as in-kind contributions and questions whether its advertising was independent of the candidates.
Kolb’s relationship with the organization is central to that allegation. Documents submitted with the complaint identify her as a member in November 2025 and again in May 2026.
The organization’s leadership included Emma Horowitz as president, Rachel Cyrulnik as vice president and Chana Shields as treasurer.
Goldberg alleges that Kolb served as an advisory board member and governing leader while running for council.
New Jersey law restricts candidates from establishing, maintaining or participating in the management or control of political committees.
Whether those restrictions apply would depend on the organization’s legal classification, its political activities, and Kolb’s responsibilities. Goldberg wants ELEC to determine whether the group’s political support was independent or coordinated with the campaign.
Fundraising expenses and other allegations
An August 12 campaign kickoff event at Cyrulnik’s Teaneck home is another issue raised in the complaint.
Schwartz, Deputy Mayor Karen Orgen and Councilman Elie Katz signed an invitation published in The Jewish Link promoting a poolside gathering where guests could meet the candidates over food and wine. Goldberg says the event featured a catered buffet, sushi and meat platters, balloons, floral centerpieces, campaign T-shirts, and other promotional materials. She alleges that the campaign did not report expenses or in-kind contributions associated with the event and wants investigators to determine who paid for it.
The campaign also listed office space at 2 University Plaza, Suite 600, in Hackensack, as its headquarters. Goldberg found no rent payments or in-kind contributions for the space in the campaign’s financial reports and wants ELEC to determine who provided the office.
Other expenses Goldberg is questioning include campaign photography, a table, and promotional materials used outside the Richard Rodda Community Center during early voting. Two check numbers, 101 and 109, are allegedly missing from the campaign’s expenditure records. Goldberg wants to know whether the checks were voided, never issued, or used for unreported payments.
The complaint seeks civil penalties against the candidates and campaign officials for any violations ELEC determines occurred.
