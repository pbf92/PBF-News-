TITLE: Medicare's prior authorization: Are we policing the wrong thing?
OUTLET: American Council on Science and Health

A new study finds that Medicare's AI-assisted prior authorization program reduces medical procedures, at least initially. But eliminating unnecessary care and controlling the financial incentives that encourage it may require very different solutions. 
          
In the 1980s, Health Maintenance Organizations (HMOs) used physicians as gatekeepers to keep utilization and costs low, making them financially viable and often lucrative. Physicians, who bore the brunt of explaining to their patients why a particular service or medication would not be provided, rightly rebelled, and gatekeeping was shifted from them directly to an intermediary: documentation.
“…roughly seven in ten US adults with health insurance (69%) say that prior authorization is a burden, and more than a third (34%) say that it is their single biggest burden, beyond costs, when it comes to getting health care”
Prior authorization is today’s gatekeeper, increasingly an algorithmic process through which a health insurer requires approval before a patient receives certain medical care, based on the insurer’s coverage criteria. Except for one year in the mid-80s, Medicare has not required prior authorization for routine care. [1] That changed this year with the implementation of the Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction (WISeR) Model for traditional Medicare.
Medicare Gets a New Gatekeeper
The model is voluntary, involves six states, and targets procedures with “low” clinical value that are costly to Medicare. [2] WISeR's goals are difficult to dispute: spend Medicare dollars on effective care, reduce unnecessary procedures, make coverage decisions more transparent, and use technology to streamline the process. However, its design is more narrowly focused on identifying and preventing potentially inappropriate utilization. Part of the model tests new “AI” technologies that play a screening role in prior authorization, although CMS quickly points out that humans make all final decisions.
As we near the end of the year, the first data on the WISeR model are being published, including a JAMA study that used Cosmos, EPIC’s electronic health record dataset. Specifically, the study measured utilization using billed procedure codes. As the researchers note, the results do not address the appropriateness of services, whether the changes were due to “deterrence (ie, fewer orders or referrals) vs denial of requests,” the impact on administrative burden, or the role of AI. With these limitations in mind, what did the research find?
Fewer Procedures, But Why?
WISer’s low-value procedures broadly include skin substitutes and wound care, implanted nerve stimulators, urinary incontinence devices, erectile dysfunction treatment, and orthopedic pain management, including epidural steroid injections. The researchers chose epidural steroid injections (ESI) to highlight, noting that its volume “exceeded that of all others combined.”
Over the first six months, WISeR was associated with a 15% reduction in epidural steroid injections (Graph A) and nearly a 20% reduction in other procedures requiring prior authorization (Graph B), relative to changes in states not participating in the program. The reductions were most pronounced immediately after implementation but declined over time. For epidural steroid injections, the estimated reduction fell from nearly 21% in the first quarter to 9% in the second. Other procedures showed a more modest recovery. Importantly, services for which prior authorization had been postponed showed no significant changes in utilization, strengthening the argument that WISeR itself drove the initial decline.
The central finding is that prior authorization was associated with an immediate, substantial reduction in medical service use, but its impact decreased over time. Whether this reflects physicians learning to navigate the authorization process, a genuine reduction in unnecessary care, or a combination of the two remains unknown.
Learning to Pass Another Test
The data remind me of what was found in the 1980s Medicare experiment, in which all procedures required pre-authorization by CMS, at least in New Jersey, where I worked. If my memory serves me, only one procedure out of an entire year’s worth of surgical care was denied. Physicians, trained on the SATs, MCATs, USMLE Steps 1 to 3, and a written and oral examination in their field of specialization, are uniquely capable of training to the test. I would argue that the essentially transient dip in utilization reflects the learning curve necessary for physicians and electronic records to learn and document the criteria required for pre-authorization.
Counting Procedures, Counting Dollars
The researchers made an interesting choice by selecting ESI, the highest-volume service. It is certainly not the most costly. According to a KFF review, of the $12.3 billion spent in 2024 on WISeR services, skin substitutes and wound treatment accounted for $10.26 billion, or 83%. Orthopedic pain management, which includes ESI, ranked second at $1.05 billion, roughly 8%. Does the low-hanging fruit lie in volume or cost? From 2019 to 2024, spending on skin substitutes rose 20-fold, while all other WISeR services were essentially flat. The increase in average cost from $2,300 to $21,200 for individual visits was driven by a number of factors.
The Price of Skin in the Game
- 
In outpatient settings, the cost of the substitute skin was bundled into the payment. When used in physician offices, patient homes, or nursing facilities, the skin product was billed separately, with an additional 6% “mark-up” to cover logistics and inventory. We have seen the same situation with ophthalmic eye drops. When faced with equally efficacious products, physicians may choose the more expensive one because that 6% mark-up can be quite significant. Another misalignment in product selection is that billing is per cm2 and subject to some limitations. Wasted substitute was billed and paid – using a larger-than-necessary amount was rewarded or at least not penalized.
- 
Most new products, especially amniotic and placental allografts, came to market through a pathway that requires no premarket efficacy trials. More than 70 cellular, acellular, and matrix products are now available at manufacturer-set prices, with no comparison to existing products.
A study of patients with foot ulcers secondary to diabetes, as well as modeling of the use of skin substitutes for healing from a form of dermatologic surgery (Moh’s), found no correlation between the cost of care and treatment healing.
This year, CMS reclassified these skin substitutes from biologicals, a “medication,” to supplies, now paying a single rate of about $127.14/cm² regardless of product, replacing the cost + 6% markup. That shifts the financial incentive towards lower-cost, equally efficacious products, and more accurate sizing with less waste.
Changing Decisions or Changing Incentives?
Prior authorization seeks to control utilization by scrutinizing individual clinical decisions and determining whether a particular service should be delivered. WISeR adds another layer of review, largely algorithm-driven, that remains more of a black box than a human explanation. This approach places the burden of demonstrating medical necessity on the physician, even as the economic incentives that encouraged excessive utilization of skin substitutes remain unchanged.
Payment reform seeks to control spending by altering the economic incentives that drive those decisions. It also asks why some services have become extraordinarily expensive in the first place. By replacing reimbursement based on individual product prices with a standardized payment, CMS reduces the financial incentive to choose higher-priced products. However, lowering reimbursement does not necessarily prevent unnecessary care; providers may respond by increasing service volume.
The JAMA study demonstrates that prior authorization can change utilization, at least initially. It cannot tell us whether those changes reflect better medicine. Meanwhile, the extraordinary rise in spending on skin substitutes suggests that the more important question may not be whether physicians are ordering unnecessary care, but why the payment system rewards expensive choices when less costly alternatives may be equally effective.
Prior authorization measures its success by procedures avoided; payment reform measures its success by dollars saved. Neither necessarily measures what matters most: whether patients receive appropriate care at a reasonable cost. Until they measure that outcome, both programs remain exercises in cost containment rather than proven improvements in value.
