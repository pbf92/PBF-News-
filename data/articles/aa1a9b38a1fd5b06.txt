TITLE: Lighthouse of Alexandria Ring Rises from the Depths
OUTLET: Biblical Archaeology Society

Lighthouse of Alexandria Ring Rises from the Depths
Find lends new insight into this wonder of the ancient world
The Lighthouse of Alexandria—also known as “Pharos,” the island on which it was constructed—was one of the most extraordinary engineering achievements of the ancient world. Surviving for more than a millennium and a half, and among the tallest human-made structures of its time, the lighthouse was considered one of the seven wonders of the ancient world. Like the Great Library of Alexandria, the lighthouse was a product of Egypt’s early Ptolemaic period in the third century BCE.
Earthquakes, structural deterioration, and a changing coastline gradually contributed to the disappearance of the lighthouse sometime after 1477 CE (when sources recall that Sultan Qaitbay built a fortress on the site). Today, the lighthouse is reconstructed from written descriptions, coins, mosaics, and underwater archaeological evidence.
To that end, Hisham El-Leithy of Egypt’s Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities recently reported the discovery of a bronze signet ring that bears “an inscription depicting the ancient Lighthouse of Alexandria, in a form that resembles the image known on some coins dating back to the second century AD, at the palace on Antirrhodos Island.” Together with the island of Pharos, the now-submerged island of Antirrhodos helped form Alexandria’s Portus Magnus (“Great Harbor”) in antiquity.
The Antirrhodos Palace and the lighthouse both occupied prominent positions within the Great Harbor. The ministry believes the ring may indicate that the palace was where administrative work for the lighthouse was conducted. However, the ring’s original owner, date, and precise function have not yet been determined, so this connection remains hypothetical.
Franck Goddio, Director of the European Institute of Maritime Archaeology and head of the Antirrhodos mission, explained that the work carried out by the mission is concentrated on multiple sites in the eastern port area, including a temple of Isis, the remains of a sarcophagus, and a palace possibly associated with Marc Antony. The current season has already unearthed pottery and glass from the fourth century BCE to the fourth century CE.
Through underwater archaeological missions in the Great Harbor, and through objects like this newly discovered bronze ring, the Lighthouse of Alexandria continues to guide navigation through the ancient world.

She holds degrees in religion from Syracuse University, Duke University, New York University, and Rutgers University, and completed a postdoctoral fellowship on religion and the public conversation at Princeton University.
