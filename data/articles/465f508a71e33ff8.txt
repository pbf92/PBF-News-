Researchers Identify Rare Genetic Night Vision Disorder
A provisionally named condition, L-ORD, impairs rod cell adjustment to darkness while central vision remains intact for years.
Updated on Nov. 23, 2026 in Life Sciences
Researchers have identified a rare inherited disorder affecting night vision and peripheral eyesight, provisionally named L-ORD and linked to a mutation in the EFEMP1 gene. The condition begins in the outer regions of the retina, while affected individuals keep normal central vision for an extended period.
Why it matters
The disorder slows light-sensitive rod cells as they adjust from bright light to darkness, and the functional changes can appear while the retina still looks structurally normal. Further large-scale studies are needed to determine how widespread the mutation is, and studying rare inherited retinal disorders may help scientists understand more common retinal diseases associated with ageing.
The findings rest on the EFEMP1 gene change p.Arg140Trp and on slowed rod adjustment to darkness, which can be measured while retinal structure appears normal. How many people carry the mutation remains unknown, as the study does not report a population-wide count.
The players
University of Pennsylvania's Perelman School of Medicine
A leading medical school in Philadelphia affiliated with the University of Pennsylvania, known for research in genetics and ophthalmology.
The details
Researchers began investigating in 1998 after observing unusual vision loss in a United States family, and similar symptoms and genetic links have since been identified in families across several European countries. The study, led by researchers at the University of Pennsylvania's Perelman School of Medicine, was published in JAMA Ophthalmology.
Timeline
- 1998: Investigation began after unusual vision loss was observed in a United States family.
- November 23, 2026: Study published in JAMA Ophthalmology.
The Big Picture
Rare inherited retinal disorders have long served as natural experiments for common retinal diseases linked to ageing. L-ORD adds a new example in which rod function changes before retinal structure does, a pattern that could guide future studies of age-related vision loss.
For people with night-vision difficulties, this discovery offers a clearer genetic explanation for symptoms that may otherwise go unexplained. Because the retina can look normal while rod function is already impaired, the findings suggest that functional testing may matter for early detection.
The takeaway
Vision changes in the dark that arrive while central sight stays sharp can signal a genetic cause worth investigating. Discussing unexplained night-vision problems with an eye doctor may help clarify whether functional testing is appropriate.
