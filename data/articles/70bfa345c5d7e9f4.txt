TITLE: Somalia Review Reveals Extreme Maternal and Neonatal Mortality Across Fragile Systems
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive structured narrative review led by researchers at the Somali National University and partner institutions has synthesized epidemiological evidence, health-system evaluations, and financing analyses to document the persisting crisis of maternal and newborn survival across Somalia. The investigation analyzed authoritative demographic estimates alongside national clinical assessments to evaluate the complex bottlenecks preventing vulnerable families from accessing life-saving care. According to national modelled statistics published by the United Nations Maternal Mortality Estimation Inter-agency Group, Somalia experienced an estimated maternal mortality ratio of 621 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births in 2020, accompanied by an uncertainty interval extending from 436 to 888 deaths per 100,000 live births. Concurrently, modelled estimates developed by the United Nations Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation established that the neonatal mortality rate reached 35.1 deaths per 1,000 live births during 2022, while the broader under-five mortality rate stood at 106.1 deaths per 1,000 live births. Although historical comparisons reveal substantial long-term reductions from modelled figures recorded in earlier decades, such as a maternal mortality estimate of approximately 1,044 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2006 and a neonatal mortality rate of approximately 54 deaths per 1,000 live births in 1990, the current indicators remain dramatically above international Sustainable Development Goal targets. These documented thresholds highlight persistent vulnerabilities across low-income, fragile, and conflict-affected environments.
To rigorously evaluate the diverse literature, the investigative team deployed a structured narrative review methodology guided by the reporting principles of the PRISMA 2020 statement adapted for narrative synthesis and the SANRA manuscript-level quality assessment framework. A narrative synthesis design was chosen because available primary sources reflected highly heterogeneous research frameworks, encompassing nationally representative household surveys, international statistical models, localized facility-based clinical audits, qualitative inquiries, and humanitarian policy documents that precluded statistical meta-analysis. Electronic searches spanned major bibliographic databases including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar, alongside targeted supplementary retrievals from the World Health Organization Global Health Library, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and official data repositories maintained by UNICEF, the United Nations Population Fund, the World Bank, the United Nations Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation, the Maternal Mortality Estimation Inter-agency Group, and Somali governmental health authorities. The primary search period reviewed published documentation from January 2000 through April 2026, supplemented by a focused policy update in September 2026. The initial identification retrieved approximately 212 distinct records, which underwent thorough screening of titles, abstracts, and methodological frameworks. After excluding commentaries lacking empirical findings and clinical reports unrelated to broader health systems, ninety-eight full-text sources were evaluated for eligibility, culminating in sixty-one distinct sources incorporated into the definitive thematic narrative synthesis.
The synthesized evidence details profound attrition across the maternal continuum of care, illustrating that nominal service utilization rarely translates into continuous, comprehensive clinical oversight. Data extracted from the Somali Health and Demographic Survey 2020, which incorporated records from 15,826 surveyed households and 16,486 individual female respondents, demonstrated that only 9.7 percent of pregnant women completed four or more antenatal care visits, representing a stagnation or decline relative to earlier Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey estimates from 2006 that reported approximately 15.2 percent completion. Although the proportion of women accessing at least one antenatal contact showed slight expansion over time, reaching between 55 and 60 percent across recent institutional surveys, completion of the full continuum remained negligible. Crucially, the national survey demonstrated that only 2.8 percent of women successfully completed the full continuum encompassing four antenatal contacts, skilled birth attendance, and timely postnatal care. Crude skilled birth attendance was documented at 31.9 percent in 2019 data compiled by UNICEF, while the overall modern contraceptive prevalence rate remained exceptionally constrained, moving from roughly 1 percent in 2006 to an estimated 3 percent in 2020. Maternal and newborn postnatal care within forty-eight hours of delivery remained critically low at approximately 10 to 12 percent, leaving severe early postpartum complications unmonitored during the period of highest clinical risk.
Clinical audits and localized institutional studies incorporated into the synthesis underscore the acute severity of obstetric complications treated within referral facilities, while distinguishing facility case-fatality figures from population-wide rates. A mixed-methods investigation conducted at Somaliland’s national referral hospital evaluated 6,658 admitted women and recorded 28 maternal deaths, revealing that direct obstetric emergencies accounted for the vast majority of fatal outcomes. Specifically, severe obstetric haemorrhage represented 46.4 percent of maternal fatalities, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy accounted for 25.0 percent, severe sepsis contributed 10.7 percent, and indirect medical complications explained 17.9 percent of deaths. Qualitative inquiries connected with the clinical audit identified severe referral failures, delayed transport, and inadequate interprofessional coordination as principal operational drivers. Meanwhile, an institutional cross-sectional study conducted in 2026 across two public hospitals in the Benadir region examined 420 postpartum women and found that 33.1 percent experienced at least one adverse birth outcome. Within this clinical cohort, birth asphyxia occurred in 15.5 percent of deliveries, low birth weight was noted in 8.8 percent, stillbirth occurred in 8.6 percent, preterm birth was observed in 3.8 percent, early neonatal death affected 3.8 percent, and congenital anomalies appeared in 2.4 percent. The authors cautioned that the institutional stillbirth proportion of 86 per 1,000 total births documented in Benadir public referral hospitals cannot be directly equated with the national modelled stillbirth rate of 28.0 per 1,000 total births reported by the World Health Organization for 2021, given the highly selected and medically complicated nature of urban hospital admissions.
Parallel assessments of neonatal hospital care illustrated substantial mortality among vulnerable and critically ill infants admitted to tertiary facilities. In a multicentre neonatal study conducted in Mogadishu encompassing 513 admitted infants, researchers observed an overall in-facility case-fatality proportion of 26.5 percent, a metric reflecting institutional outcomes among acute inpatient populations rather than a national neonatal mortality rate. The principal clinical determinants associated with infant death in this cohort included prematurity, post-maturity, birthweight below 2,500 grams, neonatal sepsis, neonatal tetanus, pneumonia, immediate resuscitation requirements, and delayed initiation of breastfeeding. Broader regional literature from sub-Saharan Africa echoed these mechanisms, highlighting that intrapartum-related asphyxia, preterm delivery complications, and severe neonatal infections dominate early infant mortality when antenatal and immediate postpartum monitoring systems fail. The review emphasized that reducing preventable newborn deaths requires continuous institutional capacity to provide thermal regulation, kangaroo mother care, prompt diagnostic evaluation, oxygen delivery, pulse oximetry, and specialized medical interventions tailored to small and sick infants throughout their immediate postnatal hospitalization.
Health-system infrastructure across Somalia exhibits profound fragmentation, marked by severe shortages in personnel and uneven service readiness across public, humanitarian, and private sectors. The review examined the Somalia Harmonized Health Facility Assessment 2022–2023, which established an empirical national census documenting 1,215 operational facilities spanning public hospitals, health centres, primary health units, and private clinics. However, a 2026 secondary analysis of this census revealed that only a small minority of health facilities possessed formal accreditation, underscoring substantial regulatory and quality-assurance gaps across the health sector. Health workforce densities documented by recent human-resource assessments indicated an acute scarcity of core clinical practitioners, recording approximately 4 physicians per 100,000 population, alongside extreme geographic imbalances that concentrate professional personnel within primary urban centres. This shortage severely restricts continuous around-the-clock availability for basic and comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care signal functions, including surgical caesarean delivery, blood transfusion, therapeutic anaesthesia, and neonatal resuscitation. Although targeted programmatic operations supported by the United Nations Population Fund provided operational assistance during 2025 to 26 priority facilities, including 8 comprehensive and 13 basic emergency obstetric facilities, these initiatives represent targeted humanitarian investments rather than universal nationwide coverage.
The structural drivers and systemic inequities shaping maternal and newborn survival were evaluated using the PROGRESS-Plus equity framework, which examined residence, clan and minority identity, education, gender norms, socioeconomic position, displacement, and environmental shocks. The authors observed that race and ethnicity in Somalia operate primarily through social marginalization, linguistic variation, and minority status, noting that marginalized populations such as the Bantu/Jareer and Gaboye face significant barriers to care, although quantitative disaggregations in health data remain sparse. Maternal educational attainment emerged as an enduring structural determinant, with uneducated women experiencing severe difficulties navigating institutional care, recognizing clinical warning signs, and securing timely treatment. Pervasive gender hierarchies and restricted female decision-making autonomy further constrain healthcare access, with national demographic data revealing that approximately one-third of married women reported having no decision-making authority over their own healthcare seeking. Compounding these dynamics, early marriage and premature childbearing shorten intervals between pregnancies and prolong exposure to high-risk obstetric conditions. Internally displaced persons, living in densely populated urban and peri-urban settlements in Banadir, Baidoa, and regional hubs, confront compounded hurdles combining extreme poverty, personal insecurity, transportation costs, and physical distance from functional health centres.
The intersection of recurrent climatic disruptions with deep economic instability exacerbates vulnerabilities across pastoralist and agrarian communities. Recurrent droughts, severe flooding, and rapid environmental variability trigger catastrophic livestock losses, chronic household food insecurity, and mass displacement into informal settlements, directly elevating rates of maternal undernutrition and maternal anaemia.
