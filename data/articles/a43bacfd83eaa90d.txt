Meta Not A Government Agency; Only Contractual Duties Enforceable: Bombay High Court Refuses Plea To Restore Blink Digital's Accounts
The Court found no strong prima facie case for mandatory injunction; allowed Blink Digital to seek restoration from meta, which must decide within 2 weeks.
The Bombay High Court has refused to direct Meta to restore the Business Manager accounts, ad accounts, Facebook page and Instagram account of digital advertising agency Blink Digital India Private Limited, holding that Meta is a private entity, not a government agency, against which only contractual duties can be enforced.
Finding no strong prima facie case for a mandatory interim injunction, the Court noted prima facie material of policy-violating advertisements and conduct, but allowed Blink Digital to approach Meta for restoration by promising to follow its policies, with a decision due within two weeks.
A Single Judge Bench of Justice Sandeep V. Marne observed, “Plaintiff otherwise does not have any right to do business with Meta. Plaintiff cannot insist that it must be permitted to use Meta’s platforms for its advertising business. Meta is not a government agency nor does it owe any public duty. It is a private entity and only contractual duties against it can be enforced…”.
Senior Advocate Janak Dwarkadas appeared for Meta and while Advocate Kunal Dwarkadas appeared for Blink Digital.
Blink Digital operated four Business Manager accounts holding about 9,000 ad accounts, built over roughly 11 years, and was a Meta Managed Agency Partner. On August 4, 2026, Meta disabled them for violating its Advertising Standards and other policies, and disabled the agency's own Facebook and Instagram pages on August 11, while Blink Digital sued for restoration and Rs 1,000 crore in damages.
On October 27, 2026, the Court had rejected Meta's plea for return of the plaint, while the interim application was then heard with the consent of the parties.
Blink Digital argued there was no prior notice or chance to dispute, breaching Rule 4(8) of the Intermediary Rules, 2021, that Meta's reasons shifted, and that 80 to 90 percent of its revenue came from Meta. Meta alleged "ban-proof" and "no spend limit" marketing, rental ad accounts and policy-violating advertisements, adding that it cannot be forced to do business with the agency. Facebook India said it disabled nothing.
Applying Dorab Cawasji Warden v. Coomi Sorab Warden and others (1990) and Deoraj v. State of Maharashtra and others (2004), the Court required a very strong prima facie case in rare and compelling circumstances. It permitted Meta to elaborate its reasons beyond the August 4 email, and held that Blink Digital's explanation for 500 and 140 suspended ad accounts damages its case more than improving the same. Meta owed no duty to warn, and Rule 4(8) was substantially complied with. Even strict non-compliance would not alone create a strong prima facie case. Proportionality could not be gauged now, and damages were available if a violation was proved.
Accordindly, prayers (a) and (b) were rejected, while Blink Digital may seek restoration by promising compliance, and Meta must decide within two weeks of receipt. Meta must preserve the data until then. This protection ends if no request is made within four weeks or if the request is rejected. Costs shall be costs in the suit.
Cause Title: Blink Digital India Private Limited v. Facebook India Online Services Private Limited and Another (Neutral Citation: 2026:BHC-OS:24595)
