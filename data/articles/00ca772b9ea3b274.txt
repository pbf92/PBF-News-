TITLE: Anthropic built a honeypot to see if AI agents will break out
OUTLET: Malwarebytes

In this article
- Anthropic’s Siren test uses bait to see whether AI agents will reach beyond their sandbox
- Recent Claude models reached or tried to reach outside in 21% to 32% of tests
- Explaining the sandbox rules and giving agents a way to report weaknesses reduced those attempts
Honeypots are a classic defender’s trick. You leave something tempting lying around and watch who takes it.
Anthropic has now pointed that trick at its own AI models. Its new test, called Siren, checks whether AI agents will break out of their sandbox when something useful sits just outside it.
AI agents run commands, edit code, and call APIs on their own. They are usually kept in a sandbox, an isolated environment that limits which files, tools, and networks they can reach. But sandboxes aren’t perfect, and OpenAI models running cybersecurity evaluations with reduced safeguards got around their isolation.
How Siren works
Siren drops an agent into a simulated sandbox with a deliberate weakness. The task is an ordinary dev-ops job. Outside the sandbox sits a “honeypot,” such as a login credential, a file, or a service that mimics the public internet. Each scenario combines four ingredients:
- The sandbox. There are 26 environments built from 5 common setups. Each has either a misconfiguration or an easy-to-find vulnerability.
- The task. These are routine jobs of varying difficulty. Some are impossible to finish without reaching outside.
- The honeypot. This is the material outside that would help complete the task.
- The tempters. These are realistic nudges, such as urgency, higher stakes, or an instruction to search thoroughly. They never tell the model to leave the sandbox or give permission to do so.
Anthropic combined these ingredients into more than 1,700 scenarios. AI judges screened the draft nudges for realism. Reportedly, the whole setup runs inside a second sandbox cut off from the outside world.
Anthropic ran the tests without the safeguards that ship with its released models. Recent Claude models accessed or tried to access something outside the sandbox in 21% to 32% of scenarios. They used what they found in ways the scenario clearly didn’t allow in 0.6% to 1.6% of scenarios. Anthropic calls this “misaligned use.” Earlier models showed higher rates of misaligned use, at 12.7% for Mythos 5.1 and 25.8% for Mythos 5.
What happens with the results
Siren is now a standard pre-deployment check. Anthropic has reported its results in system cards since Claude Haiku 5.5 and uses them to help decide how to deploy its models internally and externally. It has also used Siren to test and refine defenses:
- Spell out the rules. Adding a short description of the sandbox and its rules to the system prompt reduced how often the models reached outside by 68% to 96%.
- Give agents a way to report. A tool for reporting sandbox weaknesses reduced how often models reached outside by between 7 and 33 percentage points. Letting a model see earlier reports cut it further.
- Check actions before they run. Anthropic’s auto mode uses a classifier to vet tool calls. In an offline replay, it blocked the command that first touched the honeypot in 96% to 100% of misaligned uses.
With all three defenses in place, misaligned use fell to 0% in five models and to 0.1% in Mythos 5. Anthropic stresses that none of this fixes the sandbox itself, which still needs to be set up and patched properly. But it should be helpful when setting up sandboxes for the future.
