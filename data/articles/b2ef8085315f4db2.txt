TITLE: Only 18% of auto advertisers say CTV reports show cars sold, Jamloop finds
OUTLET: PPC Land

Jamloop today released survey results showing that 73% of 120 automotive advertisers use their own sales data or vehicle registration records to target streaming TV ads, while only 18% say their reporting can show vehicles sold. The same survey found that 63% of respondents consider vehicle sales a key measure of success, and that 77% plan to increase their investment in the channel.
In Short
Most car advertisers in Jamloop's survey already use sales and registration records to decide which households see their streaming TV ads, but fewer than one in five say their reports can tell them whether a car was sold afterwards. That matters because nearly two-thirds of the same group say vehicle sales are how they judge success, and more than three-quarters plan to spend more anyway. The practical takeaway is that, in this group, spending is growing faster than the ability to show what that spending sells.
Four figures from 120 advertisers
The findings come from a survey of 120 automotive advertisers conducted by Jamloop, a company that sells performance-focused connected TV advertising. According to Jamloop, the figures point to a blind spot in how the category measures success: the data that guides targeting is not reaching the reports that judge results.
The respondent counts are PPC Land's arithmetic on the published percentages and a sample of 120; the headline findings are given as percentages only, so rounding means each count could be off by one.
Two gaps stand out in the numbers. By PPC Land's calculation, the share of respondents using sales or registration data for targeting is 55 percentage points higher than the share whose reporting shows vehicles sold. The share naming vehicle sales as a key success measure is 45 points higher than the share able to report on it. Those are differences between headline shares, not counts of specific people. The results do not say how many of the 73% who target with sales data fall inside the 18% who can report sales, so the overlap between the two groups cannot be worked out from what has been released.
Data on the way in, not on the way out
The asymmetry is the core of the story. Car sales and registration records are among the most concrete forms of first-party data the category holds. A dealer group knows which households bought from it; registration records show which households own which vehicles, and how old those vehicles are. Feeding that information into a streaming TV campaign lets an advertiser aim at owners of ageing models, at households that bought from a competitor, or away from customers who have just purchased.
Using the same records to report results is a different task. A sale has to be matched back to a household that was exposed to the ad, often weeks later and usually at a dealership rather than online. That loop runs through a different set of systems from the one that builds the audience in the first place. According to Jamloop's survey, most automotive advertisers have completed the first half of that work and not the second.
Jamloop's own automotive marketing addresses that second half directly. The company's page for auto advertisers describes sales match-back and registration data used to connect streaming exposure to vehicles sold, and closed-loop attribution that covers website visits, showroom traffic and verified sales. It says it draws on auto intent and ownership data from more than 40 partners, which it does not name. The survey finding and the product pitch point in the same direction.
More money, same blind spot
Spending, according to Jamloop, is not slowing down. The 77% planning to increase their connected TV investment is the largest of the four figures, larger even than the share already using sales data for targeting.
This is not the first time a Jamloop survey has found budget growth running ahead of confidence. In July, Jamloop's survey of 120 senior brand and agency marketers found that only 33% fully trusted most platform-reported CTV performance claims, 62% expressed some skepticism, half said their CTV budgets had grown year over year, and more than 70% said they would invest more if measurement, attribution and proof improved. That study was not limited to automotive. The automotive results also come from 120 respondents, and the figures released do not say whether they are a separate study or a cut of the same panel.
The pattern in both surveys is consistent: buyers keep adding money while saying they cannot fully verify what it produces. Why would advertisers raise spending they cannot tie to sales? The released figures do not ask, though reach, brand metrics and site visits remain measurable even where a vehicle sale is not. For a category where the transaction is a five-figure purchase, the missing link is a large one.
The streaming-versus-linear claim
Jamloop frames its findings against a broader shift, stating that automotive advertisers spent more on streaming TV than on traditional TV for the first time last year. The source it links for that statement is iSpot.tv's Q1 2026 Auto Video Ad Snapshot.
The public summary of that iSpot report covers January to March 2026 and does not include a streaming-versus-linear spend comparison. It says that linear TV ad reach for auto brands dipped year over year in the first quarter of 2026, that 64% of new auto ads took a cinematic approach, that Jeep led streaming impression share of voice while ranking 10th on linear (the largest gap in the top 10), and that Tubi and Paramount EyeQ led 30-day web conversions. The full report sits behind a registration form.
Other iSpot figures place the crossover later than "last year." iSpot data cited by MediaPost describes auto brands spending more on streaming than on linear TV for the first time in 2026, with $1.41 billion in streaming against $1.30 billion in linear cross-platform spend through the third quarter. For 2025, WardsAuto reported in December, citing iSpot's 2025 Automaker TV Advertising Snapshot, that automakers spent an estimated $2.1 billion on linear TV between January and early December, down 9.45% from 2024, with no streaming spend figure given.
Whichever year the line was crossed, the direction is not in dispute. Across all categories, streaming took 48.74% of estimated US national TV ad spend in the third quarter of 2026 on just 21.93% of impressions, according to iSpot's Q3 2026 snapshot, which also reported a 3.49% conversion rate for streaming against 0.69% for linear across 30 brands. By that measure, streaming inventory costs far more per impression than linear, which raises the stakes on the kind of outcome reporting the automotive survey says is missing.
Who is asking the question
Jamloop is a performance CTV platform based in Walnut Creek, California. It sells outcome-based connected TV advertising through its own bidder, with access to inventory across more than 300 national networks and more than 800 local publishers, available as a managed service, self-serve or white-label product. That local reach matters in automotive, where dealer groups and regional dealer associations buy alongside manufacturers.
The company has built much of its 2026 positioning around proof. In February it launched ActiveVoter, a political product that removes households that have already voted from CTV delivery using state-reported early and absentee voting records. On October 1, it introduced Verified Supply, alongside a review in which Jounce Media classified 98% of audited Jamloop CTV spend as premium direct; PPC Land noted at the time that "premium direct" is not one of Jounce's six supply labels and that Jamloop did not disclose the number of partners, the share of spend covered, or the review period. The automotive survey extends the same argument to a single vertical: buyers want evidence, and most say they lack it.
Why the gap persists
Measurement on the television set has been a stubborn problem for the whole industry, not just for car brands. In March, an IAB Europe CTV working group discussion on the conversion gap named cross-device attribution, fragmented identifiers and inconsistent standards as the main barriers to treating connected TV as a measurable performance channel. The same article cited figures showing CTV's share of media budgets doubling from 14% in 2023 to 28% in 2025.
Industry bodies have tried to standardize the plumbing. In October 2025, the IAB published a report on conversion APIs for CTV, finding that two-thirds of advertisers reported improved return on ad spend after implementing them, while 72% of publishers cited technical complexity as a major obstacle. Purchases were the most common signal, tracked by 75% of publishers. Car sales do not fit neatly into that model, because the purchase rarely happens on a website.
Vendors have moved in with their own answers. Teads launched CTV Performance in October 2025 to track site visits, leads and sales tied to exposure. In August 2026, Lifesight published a report claiming CTV exposure lifted paid search conversion rates by 22.3%, though it gave no sample size, time period or method. Each of these approaches still leaves the question of incrementality open: whether a car would have been sold anyway, without the ad.
Trust in where the ads run is a separate but related issue. In July, the IAB found that 57% of CTV buyers had high confidence in inventory transparency for direct deals, against 33% for the open exchange. An advertiser who cannot confirm where an ad ran, and cannot confirm whether it sold a car, faces two separate verification problems at once.
What the survey does not show
The released findings do not state when the survey was fielded, how respondents were recruited, or how the 120 divide between manufacturers, dealer groups, dealer associations and agencies. They give no geography, question wording, margin of error, budget sizes or size of the planned increases, and do not define whether "reporting" means a platform's own dashboard, a third-party measurement provider or an internal system.
