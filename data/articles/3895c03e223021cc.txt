TITLE: The Lancet: Sex in Britain has changed significantly, especially among young people, suggests once-in-a-decade survey
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

- People in Britain are having less sex – adults under 45 years old report having sex twice a month on average, compared with four times a month in 1990, while one in three 16–24-year-olds now report never having had sex, up from around one in five between 1990 and 2010.
- Reporting of sex with a partner of the same sex has risen over the past decade, continuing a trend among women but marking a new shift among men.
- Online sexual behaviours are widespread, especially among young people, with half of young women and almost nine in ten young men reporting watching pornography in the past year.
- Women’s choices around having children and contraception are changing – the proportion of women without children who say they definitely don’t want any has doubled in the past decade, while use of hormonal contraception has declined among young women.
- The British population has substantial and wide-ranging sexual and reproductive health needs – from sexually transmitted infections (STIs) to contraception to sexual wellbeing – yet many people who could benefit from services are not being reached.
- Authors call for sustained investment and a joined-up, inclusive approach to sexual and reproductive healthcare and public services that responds to changing needs, tackles inequalities and supports sexual wellbeing.
The sex lives of people in Britain have shifted markedly since 1990, and their sexual and reproductive health needs are substantial and varied, according to the fourth National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal-4). The first five papers from the survey – one of the largest studies of sexual behaviour in the world – are published in The Lancet.
"We need scientifically rigorous studies like Natsal to shed light on this fundamental part of our lives and our society,” says Prof Pam Sonnenberg, Principal Investigator on Natsal-4, University College London (UCL).
“Without a study like Natsal, we’re left relying on assumptions, anecdotes, or extreme narratives. The data helps distinguish what’s common, what’s changing, and what actually needs attention,” adds Dr Wendy Macdowall, Associate Professor, the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM).
Around 6,700 people aged 16–59 were randomly selected from across Britain (England, Scotland and Wales) to take part in interviews or online surveys for Natsal-4 between September 2022 and January 2024. Three previous Natsal surveys took place in 1990, 2000 and 2010. To compare like with like over time, the researchers looked at changes among 16–44-year-olds, the age range covered by all four surveys.
Natsal is delivered by researchers from UCL, LSHTM, the University of Glasgow and the National Centre for Social Research (NatCen).
“Britain’s sex lives are changing. While most adults are having sex, people are having sex less often and more young people have never had sex. There is also a higher proportion of people reporting same-sex experiences, and online sexual activity is now part of everyday life for many,” says Soazig Clifton, Academic Director for Natsal, UCL.
“Crucially, less sex for some doesn’t mean less need for sexual and reproductive health services overall. Many people in Britain are still at risk of STIs or unplanned pregnancy, or have concerns about their sexual wellbeing, and too many of them aren’t getting the support they need,” says Prof Nigel Field, Professor of Infectious Disease Epidemiology, UCL.
The sex lives of people in Britain have changed
Most adults are sexually active: 77% of 16–59-year-olds report having sex in the past year, and 89% have had sex at least once in their lives. On average (the median [1]), people report five sexual partners in their lifetime.
However, people are having sex less often than they used to. Among 16–44-year-olds (including those who have never had sex), the average number of times people had sex in the past four weeks has fallen gradually across the surveys, from four in 1990 to three in 2010 and two in 2022–24.
Among 16–24-year-olds, this decline is largely explained by a rise over the past decade in the number of young people who have never had sex. Almost two in five young women (38%) and more than one in four young men (28%) now report never having had sex, compared with around one in five in earlier surveys. But the fall is not limited to young people: among 35–44-year-olds who have had sex, the average frequency also fell over the past decade, from three times a month to twice.
“Surveys like Natsal can document changes, but can’t tell us what’s driving these shifts, and – although it may be tempting to blame a single factor, such as the online environment - it's likely there are multiple, complex factors at play, especially for young people," says Dr Wendy Macdowall.
She continues, "On the one hand, more open discussion of consent may mean young people feel better able to decide whether and when to have sex. On the other, these findings coincide with broader changes in young people’s lives, including rising rates of mental health conditions, declines in drinking alcohol, more time spent online and a greater proportion living at home with their parents."
For the first time, Natsal asked about online sexual behaviours. Watching pornography in the past year was reported by 37% of women and 81% of men aged 16-59, rising to 50% of women and 86% of men aged 16–24. Pornography use and masturbation were less common among people who had never had sex, which the authors say suggests solo and online activity is not simply replacing in-person sex.
Among 16–44-year-olds, the proportion who report ever having had sex with a partner of the same sex doubled among women (from 7% to 14%) and tripled among men (from 4% to 12%) over the past decade, with increases across all age groups. For women, this continues a trend seen since 1990; for men, it marks a new shift.
“The increase in reporting same-sex experience has been observed in similar countries to Britain [2]. However, the size of the increase may be somewhat overestimated in our study because people who identify as lesbian, gay or bisexual appear to have been slightly more likely to take part in this latest survey than in earlier ones,” says Soazig Clifton
Sexual and reproductive health needs in Britain are substantial and diverse
Many people in Britain are at risk of STIs, particularly young people. More than one in four sexually experienced [3] 16–24-year-olds with only opposite-sex partners (26% of women and 29% of men) reported behaviours associated with STI risk – such as having four or more partners or condomless sex with two or more partners in the past year or overlapping partners in the past five years.
Additionally, across all ages more than half of men who have sex with men (57%) and of women who have sex with both women and men (60%) report behaviours associated with STI risk.
Around one in five women (19%) at risk of an unintended pregnancy report not using an effective method of contraception. Among women aged 16–24, use of hormonal contraception fell overall from 65% to 50% over the past decade, partly reflecting a fall in use of the contraceptive pill, while use of the hormonal coil rose from 4% to 10% across all age groups.
Two in five pregnancies in the past year (42%) were either unplanned (12%) or ones the woman had mixed feelings about (30%).
Among women aged 16–44 who have not had children, the proportion who say they definitely don’t want any doubled over the past decade, from 8% to 17%. This was mostly driven by women under 35; among 25–34-year-olds, it rose almost fourfold, from 4% to 17%. Additionally, there was an increase in the proportion of women aged 16–44 without children, from 29% to 36%, who said they ‘don’t know’ if they want children or not.
“Our survey didn’t look at the reasons behind the increase in women saying they definitely don’t want children. Understanding what’s driving this – whether economic insecurity, climate anxiety or changing life priorities – should now be a priority for future research and policy, as it has real implications for planning health, care and family services,” says Dr Laura Oakley, Associate Professor of Epidemiology, LSHTM.
Separately, the proportion of women aged 16–54 who have had an abortion in their lifetime rose from one in six (16%) to one in five (21%) over the past decade. The increase was mainly among women aged 35–54 and was only seen among women without a university degree or living in the most deprived areas.
The authors say the increase in women who have had an abortion does not appear to be driven by more unplanned pregnancies, which were stable, and may reflect reduced stigma, easier access to medical abortion through telemedicine, or social and economic barriers to parenthood.
Women without a university degree, those living in the most deprived areas and Black women were more likely to report not using effective contraception, while women in more deprived areas or with below-degree-level education were less likely to seek help for infertility.
“Healthcare professionals should take note of the range of experiences and preferences women may have and ensure conversations around having children, contraception and abortion are always centred on choice and autonomy,” says Dr Neha Pathak, Consultant Clinical Academic in Sexual and Reproductive Health, UCL.
Natsal-4 also measured sexual wellbeing – defined as feeling respected, safe and comfortable with sex and sexuality and having self-esteem, agency and support in one’s sex life - for the first time.
Low sexual wellbeing was more common among young people, sexual and gender minorities and people on lower incomes, and was linked both to having sex less often and to behaviours associated with STI risk. One in seven women (14%) reported ongoing upset with others about things done to them in their sexual past, and one in seven men (14%) reported feeling shame about some of their sexual thoughts and desires.
“The survey finds that having good sexual health is not always the same as having good sexual wellbeing. Yet sexual wellbeing concerns are rarely discussed by healthcare professionals. Doing so would address a broader range of patient concerns and might improve engagement with services,” says Prof Kirstin Mitchell, Professor of Social Science and Public Health, University of Glasgow.
Progress has been made, but gaps remain
Fifteen years after Britain introduced HPV vaccination, infections with the two HPV types that cause most cervical cancers were 86% lower (from 8.7% to 1.6%) among women aged 18–29 than before the programme began, and all infections were among unvaccinated women. Most eligible women are vaccinated and attend cervical screening.
“The progress made in HPV and cervical cancer prevention highlights what joined-up health services can achieve. However, women born outside the UK, from some ethnic minority groups or who are more religious are less likely to be vaccinated and screened despite remaining at risk, highlighting how services need to ensure they are reaching a range of communities so no one falls through the gaps,” says Dr Emily Trickett, Research Fellow in Epidemiology, UCL.
There has also been progress in sexual health support for men who have sex with men. Among those reporting behaviours associated with STI risk, use of sexual health services in the past year rose from 30% to 53% over the past decade – although this leaves nearly half still not using services. HIV testing among men who have sex with men overall also rose from 21% to 34%.
However, in other groups testing has fallen. HIV testing in the past year among Black men with only opposite-sex partners dropped from 15% to 5%, although the authors note surveillance data for England suggest testing in this group, which decreased during the COVID-19 pandemic, had largely recovered by 2024.
