TITLE: IAB Australia: without a control group, ad results don't prove the ad worked
OUTLET: PPC Land

IAB Australia's Advertising Effectiveness Council today published a 47-page guide on how advertisers can test whether their advertising caused a result, or whether the sale, sign-up or shift in brand opinion would have happened anyway.
In Short
IAB Australia today released a guide on testing whether advertising really causes sales and brand gains, instead of taking credit for things that would have happened without it. It matters to anyone buying ads on Google, Meta, Amazon, Netflix or a retailer's network, because the guide says attribution reports, return on ad spend and platform "lift" figures that lack a randomly chosen comparison group do not prove the ads caused anything. The practical change is a standard disclosure form, which IAB Australia recommends completing for every such test before its results are used to move money between channels.
What was published
The paper is titled "Measuring True Advertising Impact: A Guide to Incrementality Experiments." It went up on IAB Australia's website on November 26, 2026, under the Research & Resources section, together with three companion files: a short summary called a "liftout," a briefing template for planning experiments and a transparency template for reporting them. The main report is an 8.7 MB PDF. The two templates are Word documents.
The council behind it brings together media owners, agencies, advertisers, measurement firms, research companies and technology vendors. Sixteen companies are thanked for contributing content: Amazon Ads, Billups, Coles360, dentsu, Google, Kantar, Lifesight, Mars Petcare, Meta, Monks, Netflix, Prophet, RyvalMedia, Samba, Seven Media Group and Tracksuit. The paper adds a caveat that matters given that list: "The contents of this guidance paper are not necessarily reflective of individual company policies, rather it is a collaborative effort of IAB Australia councils and working groups to provide greater understanding to the market."
Dave Goodfellow, Head of Marketing Science at Tracksuit and Chair of the IAB Australia Ad Effectiveness Council, framed the problem in a statement printed in the guide. "Marketers are being asked to show that their advertising does more than take credit for sales that would have happened anyway, and incrementality measurement is the most direct way to answer that. Yet different incrementality methods vary widely in rigour, and finding the best fit carries a number of crucial considerations. This paper was written to help with that choice."
The structure runs across nine sections and two appendices. It moves from definitions, to a ranking of methods by how well they prove cause, to test design, to channel-by-channel notes, to how experiments should feed other measurement tools, and ends with a fill-in template.
Why the question is getting louder
The guide opens with the pressure marketers face. According to IAB Australia, privacy changes, signal loss and platform restrictions have made it harder to follow individual people from ad to purchase, while economic uncertainty has raised the demand to justify every dollar.
The paper defines incrementality in plain terms as the extra value generated by marketing, beyond what would have happened without it. An experiment estimates that value by comparing a group that saw the advertising with a comparable group that did not.
Two US surveys are used to show how much weight the industry now puts on these tests. The first is the "2026 Marketing Decision Confidence Index" from Haus, a company that sells incrementality testing, dated March 18, 2026, as reported by eMarketer, which asked US senior decision-makers which measurement solutions they trust most; the guide's chart is labelled January 2026. The second is the IAB US State of Data Report 2026. Both are cited by IAB Australia, and the figures below are as the guide presents them.
When PPC Land covered the IAB's State of Data 2026 report in February, the report put use of those three tools at between 67% and 76% of buy-side decision-makers and expected incrementality testing to climb from three to five tests a year to 11 or more.
IAB Australia's own Future of Measurement Report 2026, published in September, found "strong industry support" for combining methods, according to the guide. The emerging framework it describes gives each tool a job: marketing mix modelling for setting budgets, attribution for day-to-day tuning where appropriate, experiments for proving cause and checking the other tools, brand and quality metrics for longer-term effects, and headline business numbers for context.
The ten principles
The executive summary condenses the paper into ten points "every marketer should know before commissioning, running or acting on an ad experiment." They are grouped under three headings.
Principle nine carries one of the guide's sharpest lines: "A significant result is not necessarily commercially valuable, and an inconclusive one is not proof of zero impact." The point is practical. A test can show a real but tiny effect that does not cover its cost. Another can fail to find an effect simply because it was too small to see one.
The summary closes with a single sentence that could serve as the whole paper's thesis: "A reported lift alone is not proof of impact."
What counts as proof
IAB Australia has adopted the definition used by IAB US and IAB Europe in their Guidelines for Incremental Measurement in Commerce Media, which PPC Land covered when IAB and IAB Europe released that framework in November 2025. The wording is: "Incrementality measures the causal impact of marketing by identifying the additional business outcomes directly driven by a campaign or tactic, compared to what would have occurred in the absence of marketing activity."
For any method to claim it shows cause, that framework sets three requirements, according to the guide:
- A credible comparison. There must be a realistic "what if not" scenario, or a clear split between a group that receives the advertising and one that does not. Without it, "measured differences may reflect correlation rather than true campaign impact."
- Controlling for bias. Seasonality, pricing, promotions, competitor activity and other campaigns running at the same time can all distort a result if they are not accounted for.
- Separating signal from noise. A lift that falls inside the normal random wobble of the data "is not reliable for decision making."
The guide also summarises the Media Rating Council's Outcomes and Data Quality Standards from 2022. That standard draws a hard line on what may be called an experiment: "Any approach that does not control the treatment of individuals and/or does not do this at random is not an experiment and would be considered an observational approach." The MRC also asks for comparable groups, clearly defined treatment, checks that the control group was not exposed by accident, validation that the randomisation worked, and results reported with their margin of error.
Four levels of evidence
Not every method gets the same grade. The guide ranks them in four families, from strongest to weakest proof of cause, following the IAB and IAB Europe framework.
A few of those names need translating. In a holdout test, a slice of the people who would have seen an ad is randomly kept from seeing it, so their behaviour shows what happens without the ad. Ghost ads are a version of this in which the ad system records the ad a held-back person would have been shown, which keeps the two groups comparable. A matched-market test runs ads in some regions and not in similar ones. Synthetic control builds an artificial comparison group from people or places that did not see the ads, weighted to look like those that did.
Marketing mix modelling, a statistical method that estimates each channel's contribution from years of sales and spending data, sits in the econometric band. The guide notes it gains "stronger causal strength when calibrated against experiments."
The ranking is not absolute. "A poorly implemented randomised study can be less useful than a high-quality model-based counterfactual design with excellent exposure identification, matching, sufficient sample and transparent statistical treatment," according to the guide.
What the guide says does not prove cause
For performance marketers, the most consequential page may be the one titled "what incrementality is not." It lists common measures that describe activity without showing that the advertising caused anything. The guide is careful to say these tools still answer legitimate questions. They just answer different ones.
The second row will land hardest with search and retail media buyers, whose dashboards are built on return on ad spend. The point is narrow but blunt: a ROAS figure records revenue that followed ads. It does not say how much of that revenue the ads created.
Where experiments fit, and where they do not
Experiments are "not the right tool for every measurement question," according to the guide, which rates 23 common marketing tasks on a three-step scale.
Comparing every channel against every other in one test is "rarely practical given ecosystem silos and fragmentation," the guide says, and cross-platform ROAS figures are not comparable "without normalising for attribution windows, identity coverage and closed-loop data." On budget cuts, it warns that "a statistically credible lift can still be too small to justify its cost."
The limitations are spelled out too. Tests need a large enough sample, enough spend and enough outcomes. Holdouts carry an opportunity cost because some people are deliberately not shown the ads. Control groups can be contaminated when people see the campaign through another channel. And results "may not automatically generalise" to other audiences, markets or time periods.
Ben Oliver, Media & ICP Lead ANZ at Mars Petcare, put the design question this way in the guide: "I say this to our agency partners all the time; a bad result is better than no result. A test is only as credible as the design that underpins it."
How the guide says a test should be built
According to the guide, a reliable experiment rests on five design choices, made in this order.
- Groundwork before the test. Large campaigns run just before the test window can spill over into it, so what came before "is just as important as the experiment period itself." The guide calls for briefing the measurement partner early and running a feasibility check before the media plan is set, covering regional availability, minimum duration and spend, the outcome data required, the ability to measure exposure and hold people back, and any other campaigns that need controlling. The creative should match the outcome being measured: "If the experiment is measuring response to a call to action, that call to action needs to appear in the creative."
- A reliable baseline. For sales tests, the period before the test should be long enough to capture normal trading, "typically around four to 12 weeks depending on the category." In regional tests, test and control markets should show similar sales patterns beforehand, known as a parallel-trends check. Where possible, a full 52 weeks of sales history should be used to judge whether markets are comparable, and any shorter period disclosed.
- The test period. Before launch, the test should set its minimum detectable effect, "the smallest lift it is designed to reliably detect." Regional experiments "typically require at least four to six weeks of continuous advertising." Pricing, promotions, creative and media weight should stay consistent between test and control areas.
- The level of spend. Too little spend may hide the effect. Too much may push a channel into saturation, where each extra dollar does less. "Results from a high-spend test should not automatically be assumed to apply at lower levels of investment."
