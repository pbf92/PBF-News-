TITLE: What if rheumatoid arthritis could be prevented before the pain begins? CU Anschutz researchers explore how AI could help predict disease
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) begins years before someone experiences the joint pain, swelling and stiffness associated with the disease. Now, researchers at the University of Colorado Anschutz are exploring how artificial intelligence and advances in biological research could help identify the immune mechanisms underlying those early warning symptoms and signs and potentially prevent the disease before it starts.
In a review published recently in Nature Reviews Rheumatology, University of Colorado Anschutz researchers and collaborators examine how RA develops, where the earliest biological changes may originate and how combining information about genetics, the microbiome at mucosal sites, immune cells, proteins and environmental exposures could help predict who is most likely to develop the disease.
The paper brings together research from across the field to explore a future in which scientists can identify people at risk and potentially intervene before the disease causes lasting joint damage.
"What we're learning is that RA is not simply a disease that begins in the joints. There are a number of biological processes that are occurring years before someone develops symptoms,” said Michael Holers, MD, professor of medicine and rheumatology at the CU Anschutz School of Medicine and first author of the publication. “If we can better understand those processes and identify the individuals who are at risk, we have an opportunity to change the way we approach the disease. The long-term goal is to move toward prevention, not just treatment after the joints are involved and damaged."
What happens before rheumatoid arthritis symptoms appear?
Researchers at CU Anschutz have helped uncover what happens during this hidden phase of RA, beginning in 2001 with the formation of the Studies of the Etiologies of Rheumatoid Arthritis (SERA).
In a study published in Science Translational Medicine in 2025, researchers followed people with anti-CCP antibodies, a biological marker associated with increased RA risk. They identified widespread inflammation and changes in immune cells before participants developed the swollen joints that characterize clinical disease.
The findings helped reveal how the immune system can begin changing years before someone experiences symptoms. They also highlighted an important challenge, not everyone with biological risk markers develops RA.
"By the time someone's joints become inflamed, the immune system may have been changing for years. Understanding those earlier changes gives us an opportunity to investigate RA before it reaches that point, and ultimately prevent disease," said Kevin D. Deane, MD, PhD, professor of rheumatology at CU Anschutz School of Medicine and director of the CU Anschutz Autoimmune Disease Prevention Center (ADPC).
Could artificial intelligence help predict who develops rheumatoid arthritis?
One of the biggest challenges in preventing the disease is that there may not be a single warning sign that tells doctors who will eventually develop it. Instead, clues may be scattered across a person's biology including genetic information, proteins in the blood, changes in immune cells, inflammation in different parts of the body and environmental exposures.
The new paper highlights how artificial intelligence and advanced data analysis could help researchers connect these pieces of the puzzle. By integrating complex biological data, artificial intelligence and advanced analytical tools could help researchers identify patterns associated with rheumatoid arthritis, better understand what drives the disease and discover new opportunities to prevent it.
"We're trying to understand how different biological signals fit together as someone moves from being at risk for RA to developing clinical disease. There is a tremendous amount of information available, from gene expression and proteins to immune cell populations,” said Fan Zhang, PhD, an assistant professor of medicine in rheumatology with a secondary appointment in the Department of Biomedical Informatics at CU Anschutz, and senior author of the publication. “Machine learning gives us new ways to bring that data together and find patterns we would otherwise miss. We still have work to do before these approaches can guide clinical decisions, but what excites me is that we may be able to see the disease coming years before it arrives."
Can rheumatoid arthritis be prevented?
RA cannot yet be reliably prevented, but researchers are exploring whether earlier identification and intervention could delay or prevent the disease.
This work builds on years of investment in understanding how RA and other autoimmune diseases begin. The CU Anschutz ADPC brings together researchers as well as health-care providers and members of the community to provide education and research designed ultimately to develop strategies to prevent autoimmune disease. Not simply treating it after symptoms appear, but understanding what triggers it and whether its development can be interrupted. For people already living with RA, this work could also reveal new treatment targets and help explain why the disease progresses differently from one person to another.
The CU Anschutz ADPC is working now on RA, but also has the broader goal to change how medicine approaches all autoimmune diseases.
What researchers want people to know
- Rheumatoid arthritis begins developing years before joint pain and swelling appear.
- Researchers are investigating how genetics, changes in the microbiome and mucosal tissues, immune cells, environmental exposures and microorganisms contribute to disease.
- AI could help identify patterns across complex biological information and improve understanding of who is at risk.
- Preventing rheumatoid arthritis is not yet routine clinical practice, but earlier identification may create new opportunities for intervention.

Innovative, interconnected and highly collaborative, CU Anschutz delivers life-changing treatments, exceptional patient care and top-tier professional training.
