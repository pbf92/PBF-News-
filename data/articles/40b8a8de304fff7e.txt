TITLE: TikTok bans devices after Maldita finds child abuse posts with 48m views
OUTLET: PPC Land

TikTok on November 20, 2026 published a statement in Spanish and English saying it had permanently banned devices, removed "thousands of comments" and updated the image-matching libraries it uses to block child sexual abuse material, after what it called "recent reports coming out of Spain." The statement does not name a source. The reports in question began with Maldita.es, a Spanish non-profit fact-checking foundation, which on November 9 published an investigation describing more than 1,000 explicit videos and images on TikTok that had drawn some 48 million views.
The 11 days between the investigation and the statement were crowded. Spain's Ministry of Youth and Childhood asked the country's data protection agency to suspend TikTok in Spain. The agency declined to go that far. The State Attorney General's Office took up the case, and a prosecutor filed a complaint against TikTok in a Madrid court on November 13. Banco Santander, Moeve, Eroski, El Corte Inglés and several other brands paused advertising on the platform after Maldita reported that ads from 21 well-known brands had appeared next to such material.
Part of TikTok's statement is addressed to those advertisers.
In Short
TikTok says it banned devices, deleted thousands of comments and tightened its detection of child sexual abuse images after a Spanish investigation found such material on the app with about 48 million views. The findings led Spain's government to seek a temporary suspension of TikTok, prosecutors to go to court, and a string of Spanish brands, from Santander to Eroski, to pause their ads. TikTok's answer to those brands is a list of existing ad filters, while its statement gives no figures for how many accounts or devices it actually banned. A separate Maldita investigation from September, on sales of the drug known as pink cocaine, describes the same tactics: coded words on TikTok and the actual deal moved to messaging apps.
What TikTok said on November 20
The statement, titled "An update on our work to combat CSAM" in English and "Actualización sobre nuestro trabajo para combatir el CSAM" in Spanish, was filed under the Safety, Company and Product categories of TikTok's Spanish newsroom. CSAM is the abbreviation for child sexual abuse material. The English text sits below the Spanish one on the same page, so the quotes in this article come from TikTok's own English version.
It opens with a position, not a number. "Child sexual exploitation and abuse is abhorrent, and we have zero tolerance for it on TikTok," the company wrote. "We want to address recent reports coming out of Spain to give our community and partners as much information as we can about what we have established, the action we have taken, and the work that continues."
According to TikTok, a task force began analysing the information "as soon as this information was made available to us." Its first steps were to extract "all identifiable signals" to find content that broke TikTok's Community Guidelines, and to update its hash-matching libraries. A hash is a digital fingerprint of an image. If a new upload carries the same fingerprint as a known abuse image, the upload is stopped. "This means that if an attempt is made to upload CSAM, or similar content, it will be blocked," the statement says.
The central claim follows. "While one instance of this on our platform is one too many, our preliminary review found no evidence to suggest that large volumes of CSAM were not appropriately detected and acted upon."
TikTok then describes the behaviour it says it found: "We have uncovered bad actors going to extreme measures to avoid detection and trade this material off TikTok, most often on platforms that are end-to-end encrypted." End-to-end encrypted apps are messaging services where only the sender and the recipient can read the content, so the platform running the service cannot see it.
"As a result of our work in recent weeks," the company wrote, "we have taken further aggressive action against accounts that we believe were attempting to facilitate the trade of CSAM."
The measures, as TikTok lists them
The only quantity in the list is "thousands." There is no count of banned devices, removed accounts, removed videos or reports sent to the authorities, and no date for the additional measures.
How TikTok describes its detection system
The statement also sets out the detection system TikTok says it runs at all times, not only in response to Spain. According to TikTok, it works in five layers:
- Hash lists from outside bodies. TikTok uses hash lists compiled by the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC), a US organisation, and the Internet Watch Foundation, a UK charity. These lists "contain unique digital fingerprints of known CSAM imagery," which TikTok says lets it "prevent previously identified violative content from being shared on our platform."
- Matching tools from other technology companies. TikTok names Microsoft's PhotoDNA, YouTube's CSAI Match and Google's Content Safety API. It says these support "the identification of known, new, or suspected CSAM at the point of upload."
- Checks at upload. Both of the steps above run when content is uploaded, according to the statement, rather than after it has been published.
- A specialist team. TikTok says its "dedicated Child Safety Team comprises experts with backgrounds in youth protection, law enforcement, analytics, and forensic investigations."
- Removal and reporting. "We take the threat of CSAM extremely seriously and have no incentive other than to detect, remove, and report it as quickly as possible," the company wrote. It does not say how many reports it has filed, or to whom, in connection with the Spanish findings.
Fingerprint lists catch copies of images that have already been identified. TikTok says its other tools also look for new or suspected material. The Maldita investigation was about material that had stayed up anyway: clips that were edited, disguised or hidden inside otherwise ordinary videos.
What Maldita reported
Maldita.es published its investigation on November 9, 2026. Its reporters spent 35 days, from September 11 to October 15, 2026, using the platform the way an abuser looking for material would, in Maldita's description: training the recommendation algorithm, using the search bar, joining livestreams and following sellers to outside messaging apps.
The security software company Bitdefender summarised the findings on its HOTforSecurity blog on November 11. "Maldita.es investigators say they have found over 1,000 public posts containing explicit child sexual abuse material, with some 48 million views," according to Bitdefender. Maldita's own figure is 1,183 videos and images. Bitdefender added a caveat that applies to every figure in this section: "Please keep in mind that these are Maldita.es' findings, not the outcome of any judicial process determining who the people behind those accounts are."
Maldita says the sellers it tracked pointed buyers to channels on Telegram and Zangi, and less often to WhatsApp or TikTok's own direct messages. Clips on TikTok carried sales text, emojis placed over explicit parts of an image, or short non-explicit clips advertising material held elsewhere. Some videos hid an abusive clip between two harmless ones.
The reporting test
To check TikTok's reporting system, Maldita flagged a sample of 50 videos through the app's own reporting tools and appealed some of the rejections. It rechecked the results on October 28.

According to Maldita, the 50 reported videos had about 8.8 million views and nearly 150,000 likes. Seventy-two hours after the reports, 38% of the accounts behind them were still active.
TikTok's first public answer came before publication. In comments obtained by the Spanish news agency EFE and cited by Bitdefender, TikTok "said Maldita.es hadn't provided the full scope of its investigation and as a result it was unable to identify and remove the material or report it to the authorities." Maldita's account of the exchange is that it emailed TikTok on Friday, November 6, that TikTok replied the same afternoon asking for the accounts, videos, hashtags and links, and that Maldita declined before publication and later handed its evidence to prosecutors.
Livestreams and gifts
Bitdefender's summary also describes the livestream findings. "Researchers documented 91 live streams involving minors in 17 countries where viewers had requested that they do things (some of which were sexual) in return for money," according to the blog.
The money runs through TikTok's gift system. "Users can purchase Coins to send Gifts, and eligible creators can earn Diamonds from the Gifts they receive during a live stream," Bitdefender explains. "TikTok says users must be adults to access these features." TikTok's November 20 statement does not mention livestreams or gifts.
The ads next to the material
On November 13, Maldita published the part of its work that reached advertisers directly. On the morning of November 5, in about two hours of scrolling one test account's For You feed, its reporters recorded 21 ads from well-known brands appearing just before or after videos of child sexual abuse, posts from accounts selling such material, or posts where commenters sexually harassed minors.
The brands named by Maldita were Adobe Creative Cloud Pro, Samsung Spain, Starbucks Spain, Colgate Spain, Banco Santander, Milka, Eroski, Holded, Booking, Cheetos, Verisure, Kia, Nescafé Dolce Gusto, Apple, JD Sports, Philips, Cola Cao, Pringles, Moeve, Ralph Lauren and El Corte Inglés. Maldita does not say any brand chose those placements. Its point is that brands pay TikTok for ad space and that their ads can end up beside material the platform's own rules prohibit.
Spain's government, the data regulator and the prosecutors
The government moved within a day of publication. On November 9, the Ministry of Youth and Childhood asked the State Attorney General's Office to consider every measure needed, including interrupting the service, according to Maldita. On November 10, Minister Sira Rego met Maldita.es and then TikTok, and announced that she had asked the Spanish Data Protection Agency (AEPD) to suspend TikTok in Spain.
"Spain's Ministry of Youth and Childhood (Ministerio de Juventud e Infancia) has asked the country's data protection regulator (AEPD) to temporarily suspend TikTok following an investigation that found child sexual abuse material on the app," Bitdefender reported. The blog described the request as "a proposed temporary suspension rather than a full ban." Citing Maldita, it added that "the ministry invoked an emergency mechanism under EU data protection rules that would allow provisional restrictions to be put in place for up to three months in Spain."
That mechanism is Article 66 of the General Data Protection Regulation, the urgency procedure. Normally a company that operates across the EU answers to the data protection authority of the country where it has its main European base, under the GDPR's one-stop-shop system. For TikTok, that is Ireland's Data Protection Commission. Article 66 lets another national authority act alone in urgent cases, for a limited time and only on its own territory.
The AEPD answered on November 11. Its press note did not adopt a suspension. It said measures under Article 66 are limited to specific, exceptional cases, and it pointed to the Irish authority's lead role. In place of a suspension, it set out a narrower plan: ask Maldita for the list of links, require TikTok to explain what it had done to remove the content, and then order access blocked to those specific links and posts.
