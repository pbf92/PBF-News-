TITLE: Tyler Perry’s Christmas Movie Is Everything You’d Think It Would Be
OUTLET: Christianity Today

Whether or not you enjoy a Tyler Perry movie depends on what you expect going into it.
That is, if you don’t expect a cinematic masterpiece. And not necessarily a compelling plot either. (See the list of “Madea” movies: Madea Goes to Jail, A Madea Christmas, the aptly named Boo! A Madea Halloween.)
But if you anticipate a predictable, feel-good, sometimes-corny-yet-ultimately-encouraging film with Christian themes and messages, you might find Perry’s work endearing. It’s no different with his new holiday film, The Gospel of Christmas.
The movie, the latest from Perry’s multiyear deal with Netflix, follows a washed-out R&B singer named Isaac who unexpectedly finds himself in ministry after agreeing to his father’s dying wish of reviving a dilapidated church in a rough New York City neighborhood.
When Isaac (played by real-life musician Durrell “Tank” Babbs) and his family move up north from Georgia, they discover the church—a Black Baptist congregation once pastored by Isaac’s great-grandfather—has few to no members and is months behind on its mortgage. A deacon who lives in the church’s attic (a secret angel, we learn, played by John Gray) reveals to the family that the community’s decline is connected to the deterioration of the church, which needs to collect $58,000 in a short period of time to remain open.
So the family gets going. They paint the building and canvass the neighborhood, only to find a chilly reception among locals. Isaac bombs his first sermon. Yet he refuses to pray because, he tells the angel-deacon, of the disappointment he feels about other unanswered prayers. The rest of the 104-minute movie explores whether Isaac will turn into a prayer warrior like his wife, Rebecca (Fantasia Barrino), or rely on his own strength to solve the impending crisis.
A bright spot in The Gospel of Christmas is the cinematically abrupt yet lovely music, and the cast, made up of a who’s who of gospel music.
Perry, who wrote and directed the film, was happy to put their voices to use: Isaac’s aunt (Dorinda Clark of The Clark Sisters) starts off the musical numbers with “Going to Heaven to Meet the King”; Shirley Cesar—playing one of several unseen angels in the church’s attic—offers the war chant “Satan We’re Gonna Tear Your Kingdom Down”; and Mrs. Johnson (CeCe Winans) gives a nice rendition of Bethel Music’s Goodness of God. Cast members Yolanda Adams and Jekalyn Carr bless us with a Christmas classic. The legendary Fred Hammond, who plays Isaac’s father, returns from death as an angel (theological questions remain) to sing “No Weapon” before disappearing in a puff of CGI doves.
If you think that’s one angel too many, you’re mistaken. More show up, and so does the Devil incarnate. The movie, decidedly made for the Black church, also pulls in one person from the wider world of evangelicalism: Carl Lentz, who briefly appears as a man who made major life mistakes and teaches others to lean into God’s grace.
So should you, dear reader, go see this film? As I said earlier, it all depends on what you expect from it.
Perry is famous for writing and directing a big chunk of his movies and TV shows, often on speedy timelines, and the quality of his work suffers as a result. Is he, as one writer once suggested, purposefully “playing in our face” by making subpar movies? I don’t think so. But does he need to slow down so he can give his audience a proverbial nice dinner instead of a fast-food meal? Yes.
That said, most people can enjoy fast food. There’s something satisfying about a quick burger and fries. And if that’s true with food, it can be true with art.
The Gospel of Christmas shows the power of prayer and announces the fact that Jesus saves and God, as is often said, is in the miracle-working business. That’s not a typical message coming out of Hollywood. Yet it is one that Perry—one of the biggest filmmakers in the industry—feels excited to share. So I’ll take it.
Haleluya Hadero is the Black church editor at Christianity Today.
