TITLE: Problematic internet use: from individual concern to global public health challenge
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Problematic internet use may affect more than 10 percent of the world's population, according to a comprehensive report by an international expert commission published in The Lancet Psychiatry. Three researchers from Eötvös Loránd University's Institute of Psychology – Zsolt Demetrovics, Andrea Czakó and Orsolya Király – also contributed to the report. The experts argue that addressing the problem requires more than changing individual behaviour: the way digital platforms operate, as well as prevention and healthcare, must all be reconsidered.
The internet and digital technologies have become an integral part of our everyday lives, but their use is not always problem-free. According to a report by an international expert commission published in The Lancet Psychiatry, problematic usage of the internet (PUI) may affect more than 10 percent of the world's population. Determining the exact prevalence is challenging, as studies use different definitions and measurement methods, making their findings difficult to compare.
Problematic internet use encompasses much more than excessive social media use or gaming addiction. Experts use it as an umbrella term that includes, among other things, problematic online shopping, compulsive online information seeking, excessive internet-based health research (cyberchondria), and certain forms of online sexual behaviour. It is important to distinguish between intensive but still healthy use of digital technologies and patterns of behaviour that negatively affect everyday life, relationships or work.
The report places particular emphasis on protecting children and young people. Problematic digital use has been associated with a range of issues, including sleep disturbances, anxiety, depression and declining academic performance. However, the researchers stress that the direction of these associations is not always clear: mental health difficulties may contribute to the development of problematic internet use, while excessive use may also exacerbate these difficulties.
One of the experts' key findings is that problematic internet use cannot be explained solely by individual vulnerability or a lack of self-control. The design of digital services can also play a significant role in reinforcing excessive use. Infinite scrolling, autoplay videos, personalised recommendations and notifications can all encourage users to return to apps repeatedly. Advances in artificial intelligence may further intensify these mechanisms.
The commission therefore calls for coordinated international action. Alongside developing prevention strategies and evidence-based treatment options, it considers the regulation of digital platforms essential. Its recommendations include stronger protections for children, restrictions on potentially harmful features, safer service design and stronger user rights. The report also highlights that existing treatment options and the preparedness of healthcare professionals have yet to keep pace with the scale and significance of the problem.
The Commission further emphasises that effective preventive interventions must be developed and evaluated alongside regulatory measures. In this context, it highlights the BootStRaP project as an example of an initiative testing an app-supported preventive model designed to promote healthier internet use habits among children and adolescents. In the Commission's assessment, interventions of this kind may play an important role in addressing PUI at the population level.
The work of the international expert commission provides a comprehensive overview of the background and consequences of problematic internet use, as well as approaches to prevention and potential regulatory measures. The authors emphasise that, although further research is needed in many areas, the scientific evidence already available warrants immediate action.
