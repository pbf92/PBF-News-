TITLE: Google says most 404 errors won't hurt a site's ranking
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google today rewrote the part of its crawling troubleshooting guide that deals with deleted web pages, moving in advice on 404 errors that until now sat on a separate Search Console Help Center page. The expanded section on Google Search Central explains when site owners can leave 404 errors alone, says that known 404 addresses do not meaningfully use up the time Google spends crawling a site, and warns against two common habits: blocking dead pages from its crawler, and sending visitors of a deleted page to the home page.
A 404 is the short code a web server sends back when someone asks for a page that does not exist. Most people know it from the "page not found" screen. For anyone who runs a website with pages that come and go, from online shops dropping products to publishers retiring old campaign pages, those codes pile up in Google's Search Console reports. Google's view, in its own words: "In most cases, 404 errors are a normal part of how the web works."
In Short
Google today expanded its official guide on 404 errors, the "page not found" responses a website sends when a page is gone, and moved the advice there from an older help page. It matters to anyone whose site loses pages over time, such as shops with discontinued products, publishers, and advertisers with expired campaign pages. Google says most 404s need no fix and do not hurt rankings, but removed pages should come out of sitemaps, should stay visible to Google's crawler, and should not be redirected to the home page.
What Google changed today
The change was logged on the Search Central documentation changelog under the entry "Expanded guidance on handling 404 errors," dated November 27, 2026. According to that entry, Google "Updated the crawling errors troubleshooting guide with a video and guidance on when to ignore 404 errors, how 404 URLs affect crawl budget, updating sitemaps for removed URLs, and avoiding common pitfalls such as blocking 404 pages in robots.txt or redirecting them to your home page."
The stated reason was administrative: "To consolidate our 404 troubleshooting guidance from the Search Console Help Center (now redirecting) into Search Central documentation." The Help Center page in question, titled "404 (Page Not Found) errors," lived at support.google.com/webmasters/answer/2445990.
The new material sits in the guide titled "Troubleshoot Google Search crawling errors," which now carries the stamp "Last updated 2026-11-27 UTC." Its placement is worth noting. The 404 advice lives under the heading "Fix soft 404 errors," in a subsection called "The page and content are no longer available." That subsection now embeds a short Google Search Central video whose title asks whether a 404 is something to be scared of, followed by a new block headed "Decide whether to fix 404 errors in Search Console."
How much is new? Copies of the same guide in German and Japanese, last updated on November 18 and November 19, still show the earlier version of the subsection. There, the guidance on removed pages ran to two sentences - return a 404 or a 410 and let search engines drop the page - followed by tips for designing a custom error page. The video and the whole decision block are additions.
Google's five points, word for word
The new block opens with a reassurance and a condition. "Seeing 404 errors in Search Console can be concerning, and it's common to wonder whether you need to fix every one of them," the guide states. Returning a 404 or 410 HTTP status code "for pages that no longer exist or never existed is expected and won't trigger a manual action or harm your site's overall indexing or ranking." A manual action is a penalty applied by a Google reviewer rather than by an algorithm.
The condition comes next: "After you confirm that the page wasn't removed by mistake or moved to a new URL, whether to act is a user experience and business decision (for example, if people are still visiting the old URL from external links)."
Five points follow, quoted here in full:
- "Unknown URLs are normal": "You might see 404 errors in Search Console for URLs that never existed on your site, such as mistyped links or URL-like strings that Google's systems extracted. Generally, you can ignore these errors and leave the URLs returning a 404 status code. However, if a commonly mistyped URL gets meaningful traffic for an existing page on your site, see The page or content is now somewhere else."
- "Known 404 URLs don't drain crawl budget": "Unlike 5xx server errors and 429 status codes (which signal server overload and reduce your crawl capacity limit), 404 and 410 status codes don't lower your site's crawl capacity, and already-crawled 404 URLs in Search Console don't meaningfully consume crawl budget."
- "Update your sitemaps": "If a removed URL is still listed in a sitemap, remove it from the sitemap. Note that once Google has crawled a URL, our crawlers might continue checking it occasionally even after it returns a 404 status code. Returning a 410 status code generally leads to less frequent crawling (for indexing, Google treats 404 and 410 status codes the same)."
- "Don't block 404 URLs with robots.txt": "Google needs to crawl a removed URL to see the 404 or 410 status code and remove the page from the index. Blocking 404 URLs in robots.txt prevents Google from seeing the status code."
- "Don't redirect removed pages to your home page or serve placeholder content": "Redirecting nonexistent URLs to an unrelated page (such as your home page) or creating fake placeholder content instead of returning a 404 or 410 status code can confuse users and search engines, and Google treats these as soft 404 errors. Only redirect when you have an equivalent replacement page."
How the guide sorts a missing page
According to Google, a page that has dropped out of a site falls into one of three situations, each with its own response. Read together with the new block, the documentation describes the following sequence.
- Check the cause. The guide asks site owners to confirm "that the page wasn't removed by mistake or moved to a new URL" before deciding anything else.
- Page moved, or a clear replacement exists. "If your page has moved or has a clear replacement on your site (including when a commonly mistyped URL points to an existing page), return a 301 (permanent redirect) to redirect the user." The guide points to the URL Inspection tool in Search Console to check that the address actually returns the right code.
- Page removed, with nothing similar on the site. The instruction is to "return a 404 (not found) or 410 (gone) response (status) code for the page." A 410 tells the requester the page is gone for good. According to the guide, both codes signal "that the page doesn't exist and you don't want search engines to index the page."
- Clean up the sitemap. A sitemap is the file that lists a site's pages for search engines. Any removed address still listed there should come out of it.
- Leave the address open to Google's crawler. The guide warns against using robots.txt, the plain-text file at the root of a site that tells crawlers which paths they may fetch, to hide the dead address. Google has to fetch the page to see the 404 or 410.
- Page still exists but was flagged. If a working page shows up as a soft 404, meaning Google judged it to be an error page even though the server reported success, the guide says it probably "didn't load properly for Googlebot, it was missing critical resources, or it displayed a prominent error message during rendering," and again points to the URL Inspection tool.
- Decide on the rest. Beyond those steps, the guide leaves further action to the site owner as "a user experience and business decision."
What a 404 does and does not cost in crawling
The crawl budget point is the most technical part of the update, and probably the one with the most practical weight for large sites. Crawl budget is the number of pages a search engine is able and willing to fetch from a site in a given period. It depends on how much load the site's servers can take and how much Google wants to see. For large news sites and shops, it decides how quickly new and changed pages are noticed.
The guide draws a firm line between two kinds of error. Server errors in the 5xx range and the 429 "too many requests" code tell Google the site is struggling, and they shrink the crawl capacity limit. A 404 or 410 does not. That distinction has come up before in Google's material. In October, Google Search Central told sites patching PHP to serve 503, not 404, to keep pages indexed: a 404 leads Google to remove the page from its index, while a 503 holds it there for a time. Google's crawling documentation update in November 2025 warned against returning 500, 503 or 429 codes for longer than one to two days. And in March, Google engineers described 403 and 404 responses as routine client errors that do not slow Googlebot down, while a 503 triggers extra slowing.
The new text uses careful words. Already-crawled 404 addresses do not "meaningfully" consume crawl budget, and Google's crawlers "might continue checking" a dead address "occasionally." Neither word comes with a figure, and some site operators read their own logs differently. In July, a classifieds site with about 10,000 new short-lived listings a month said more than half of Googlebot's visits went to 404 pages. The guide does not address how a high share of visits to dead pages squares with "don't meaningfully consume."
The 410 detail is a genuine refinement. The old Help Center page said Google treated a 410 the same as a 404. The new wording keeps that for indexing, where both codes lead to removal, but adds that a 410 "generally leads to less frequent crawling." For sites that retire large numbers of pages, it is the only point in the update that touches how often Google comes back.
Sitemaps, robots.txt and the home page
Two of the five points concern mistakes that are easy to make with good intentions.
The first involves robots.txt. Blocking a dead address there may look like tidying up. According to Google, it has the opposite effect: Google cannot fetch the page, never sees the 404, and so cannot remove the address from its index. Google's video hosts made a related point in October, when the second episode of the Non-Canonical Cabinet series explained that robots.txt controls access to pages, not what happens to content already collected.
The second involves redirects. Sending every dead address to the home page can look like a way to keep visitors on the site. Google calls the result a soft 404: a page that looks like an error or has nothing relevant on it, while the server reports success. The guide says these redirects "can confuse users and search engines," and that only a real replacement page justifies a redirect. Google's John Mueller and Martin Splitt said much the same in August 2024, when they advised against redirecting deleted pages to the home page or to loosely similar content.
The sitemap point is simpler. A removed page listed in a sitemap keeps inviting Google back. Sitemaps have been a recurring theme this autumn: in October, Mueller said Google may skip sitemaps on sites it deems low quality and uses the last-modified dates in them only when they look reliable. A sitemap full of dead addresses does little to earn that trust.
Custom error pages
The guide keeps its advice on custom 404 pages, the branded "not found" screens many sites show. According to Google, a good one "helps people find the information they're looking for, and also provides other helpful content that encourages people to explore your site further." The guide lists four design tips: state clearly that the page cannot be found, in friendly language; match the look and navigation of the rest of the site; consider a search box and links to popular articles, categories and the home page; and think about a way for users to report a broken link.
