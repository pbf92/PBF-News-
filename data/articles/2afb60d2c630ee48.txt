TITLE: YouTube and Netflix lift Polish streaming to 10.8% of TV time
OUTLET: PPC Land

Streaming's share of television screen time in Poland rose from 10.4% in September to 10.8% in October 2026, according to Nielsen's The Gauge: Poland report for October, published in November 2026. YouTube climbed from 2.3% to 2.5% and Netflix from 1.5% to 1.7%, while other streaming services as a group held at 6.6%, and average daily viewing grew by 11 minutes to 3 hours and 49 minutes.
In Short
Poles watched more television in October than in September, and streaming won back most of the share it lost a month earlier. The two named services, YouTube and Netflix, account for the whole gain on Nielsen's figures, while the rest of streaming stayed flat as a group, which matters to anyone buying video in Poland as Netflix prepares to bring its ads plan to the country in 2027. Traditional television still held almost 82% of TV screen time, and terrestrial broadcasting made the biggest jump of the three delivery routes.
What Nielsen reported
Nielsen's page carries no day of publication. It is dated "November 2026" and labelled a two-minute read, so the exact release date cannot be established from the document. The figures cover October 2026, and the report follows the September edition, in which streaming fell from its August record.
Nielsen opens with the weather. "October's autumn weather clearly encouraged Poles to spend more time in front of their screens, resulting in both longer viewing times and a higher overall number of viewers," the report states. Average daily TV viewing reached 3 hours and 49 minutes, 11 minutes more than in September and, according to Nielsen, "just 2 minutes less as of October 2025." That wording implies an October 2025 average of 3 hours and 51 minutes, a figure the report does not print on its own.
The chart in the report gives the full total-day split for persons aged 4 and over. The change column is a PPC Land calculation from the September and October editions.
The five main categories in the October chart add up to exactly 100.0%, and the three streaming lines add up to the 10.8% headline. The document is internally consistent.
Streaming recovers most of September's loss
"In percentage terms, however, the video online segment recorded the largest growth, recouping a significant portion of its September losses," according to Nielsen. Streaming had dropped 0.6 points in September. October returned 0.4 of them, which leaves the segment "edging closer to August's record high of 11.0%," in Nielsen's words, but still 0.2 points short.
The claim of the largest growth "in percentage terms" holds up. Measured against their September base, streaming's share rose by about 3.8% and terrestrial's by about 3.4%, on PPC Land's arithmetic. In points, terrestrial moved further. Both readings are correct; they simply answer different questions.
Nielsen says the growth "spanned the market's major players." YouTube "remained the leader," rising from 2.3% to 2.5%, and Netflix improved from 1.5% to 1.7%. The report does not comment on the rest of the streaming segment, and that is where the more telling detail sits. Other streaming services, the bucket that covers every platform except the two named ones, stayed at 6.6% for a second month.
So October is close to a mirror image of September. In September, YouTube and Netflix lost a combined 0.9 points while the rest of streaming gained 0.3. In October, the two leaders gained 0.4 points between them and the rest did not move. On the rounded figures, the two services have driven the monthly movement in Polish streaming for two months running, in both directions.
The five most recent editions show how narrow the band has been.
June figures come from the June 2026 edition, July from the July 2026 edition, and August and September from the September 2026 edition. In July, the three streaming lines summed to 10.8% against a stated 10.7%.
YouTube's 2.5% in October matches its July level and sits below August's 2.8%. Netflix's 1.7% matches July too. For the SVOD service, that remains a thin slice of Polish TV time, and lower than the 1.9% it held in June and August.
Shares versus minutes
Shares hide what happens to the total, and October's total grew by 11 minutes. Applying each month's shares to each month's daily average gives a rough count of minutes per day. Nielsen's shares are based on average minute ratings rather than a simple split of the daily average, so the figures below are PPC Land approximations, useful for direction rather than precision.
Every category gained time. Cable lost a full point of share yet, on this approximation, added about two minutes a day, because the pie itself grew. Terrestrial gained the most minutes, close to four. Streaming added about two minutes, and on this measure other streaming services did add time in October, roughly 0.7 minutes, even though their share did not move.
Terrestrial's jump and cable's slip
"Among traditional signal sources, terrestrial television saw the biggest jump," according to Nielsen, with its share rising from 20.5% to 21.2%. Cable dropped by 1 percentage point, to 37.6%. Satellite was almost unchanged at 23.1%. Taken together, the three delivery routes for linear TV held 81.9% of TV screen time in October, against 82.3% in September.
These categories describe how a signal reaches the home, not which broadcaster sent it. The report does not explain why terrestrial gained while cable fell, and it does not link the shift to any programme.
What it does name is the content. According to Nielsen, the most-watched programmes "continued to be dominated by well-known hits": the series "M jak Miłość" and "Na dobre i na złe" on TVP2, the news programme "Fakty" on TVN, and Polsat's "Dancing with the Stars." Sport contributed again. "September's sports trend was kept alive by UEFA Nations League football broadcasts on TVP1," the report states.
Those three groups are the same ones that lead Nielsen's separate distributor ranking for Poland. In September 2026, Polsat took the top spot with 19.05% of total video time across four screens, ahead of Warner Bros. Discovery, which owns TVN, at 18.41% and TVP at 18.03%. The Gauge measures something narrower, television sets only, but the programmes Nielsen names in October all come from those three owners.
Two Octobers apart
The Nations League pattern is not new. In October 2024, matches between Poland and Croatia and between Poland and Portugal helped lift daily viewing by six minutes compared with September of that year. UEFA Nations League football, a textbook case of appointment viewing, has now featured in the October edition two years running.
The underlying mix has moved a long way in those two years. The comparison below uses PPC Land's report on the October 2024 edition; the change column is a PPC Land calculation.
Two things stand out. Satellite has lost more than 7 points of share in two years while cable has gained more than 6, a swap between delivery routes that dwarfs anything streaming has done in the same period. And Netflix's share of Polish TV time is lower than it was two Octobers ago. Streaming's growth since 2024 has come from YouTube and, above all, from the long tail of other services, which grew by 1.8 points.
Daily viewing tells a steadier story. September 2024 averaged 3 hours and 38 minutes, so October 2024's six-minute rise puts that month at about 3 hours and 44 minutes, on PPC Land's arithmetic. October 2025 came in at 3 hours and 51 minutes, as implied by Nielsen's comparison, and October 2026 at 3 hours and 49 minutes. Polish TV-set viewing in October has stayed within a range of about seven minutes across three years.
How the numbers are built
According to Nielsen, the October report is produced in the following way:
- The data comes from Nielsen's single-source panel "consisting of 3,500 households and almost 9,700 panelists."
- The Gauge: Poland "is based on monthly AMR (Average Minute Rating) audience share data."
- The data covers "people over 4 years old." The chart labels the universe "Total day, Persons 4+."
- Viewing is "broken down into cable, satellite, terrestrial television (both linear and shifted in time up to 7 days), and viewership from streaming." Time-shifted playback therefore counts towards the delivery route, and the chart labels the measure "TV Consolidated+7."
- "Live streaming viewership of TV stations on OTT platforms is classified as streaming viewership." A broadcaster's channel watched live through its own app sits in the streaming slice, not in cable, satellite or terrestrial.
- "The 'Other' category includes views of unrecognized content."
The chart's source line reads: "October 2026, Nielsen Single Source Panel Data (Linear TV & Streaming) - TV set AMR data, TV Consolidated+7." The measurement covers television sets only. Viewing on phones, tablets and computers falls outside it, which matters most for YouTube.
Point 5 complicates the "linear versus streaming" reading. Part of the 6.6% held by other streaming services is broadcaster content, so the report's streaming figure is not a clean measure of time spent away from Polish broadcasters. The October report does not break the bucket down.
The methodology section again ends with the sentence "Nielsen Poland created this iteration of The Gauge using methodologies comparisons," unchanged from the September edition and still without explanation. Nielsen first published The Gauge in the US in May 2021, according to the report.
Why this matters for marketers
The Netflix figure carries more weight than its size suggests. At its upfront on May 13, 2026, Netflix said its ads plan would reach 15 additional countries starting in 2027, Poland among them. Its share of Polish TV screen time has ranged between 1.5% and 1.9% across the five 2026 editions in the table above. Those readings measure Netflix's share of Polish TV-set time ahead of the launch, though they say nothing about viewing on other devices or about how many Polish subscribers might choose the ads plan.
The gap with the US remains wide. In Nielsen's US Gauge for August 2026, streaming took 49.6% of TV time and YouTube a record 15.8%. Poland's October streaming share is about a fifth of that level, and YouTube's Polish share about a sixth. The two reports are built from different panels and methods, so the comparison is directional. The US series has an interruption of its own: Nielsen will publish the US September and October intervals together after a one-time methodology adjustment to align The Gauge with its TV currency data. The Polish edition has kept to its monthly schedule.
For planners, October's data adds a second seasonal signal after September's. Streaming in Poland dipped when autumn schedules started and rose again when total viewing climbed, but other streaming services did not move in either direction. The fluctuation sits with YouTube and Netflix. Whether November carries streaming past August's 11.0% will depend, if the last two Octobers are any guide, on how much sport and how many returning series the broadcasters put on the schedule.
What the report does not say
The report gives no day of publication. Nielsen cites "a higher overall number of viewers" but publishes no reach figure, and it attributes the rise to autumn weather without data to support the link. It names programmes but gives no audiences for them, gives no October 2025 shares, does not break down the 6.6% held by other streaming services, and does not list Canal+ separately as the June edition did.
