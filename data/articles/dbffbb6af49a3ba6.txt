TITLE: Google's second sexual content rewrite in 33 days puts lingerie on both sides
OUTLET: PPC Land

Google today revised the Sexual content policy article in its Advertising Policies Help Center for the second time in 33 days. The update adds lists of allowed examples to the four categories that had none - nudity, partial nudity, sexual merchandise and sexual entertainment - and rewrites how each of them is defined. According to the change log entry, the purpose is "to provide additional examples and clearer information to advertisers." The same entry ends with the sentence that frames everything else: "This does not change enforcement of the policy."
In Short
Google rewrote parts of its Google Ads help page on sexual content today, adding examples of what is allowed in four categories and naming restricted cases such as nudity visible through sheer clothing, vacuum erection devices and phone sex. The change matters to lingerie, swimwear, sexual wellness, menopause care, publishing, gaming and fitness advertisers, because an ad that falls inside a restricted category can only run on Google Search, for some users and in some countries. Google says enforcement has not changed, so according to the company the page now describes the line in more detail rather than moving it.
What Google posted today
The notice is two sentences long. Titled "Update to Sexual Content policy article (November 2026)", it sits in the 2026 section of the Advertising Policies Help Center change log and reads, in full: "Google has updated the Sexual content policy article to provide additional examples and clearer information to advertisers. This does not change enforcement of the policy."
Those two sentences are identical, word for word, to the entry Google posted on October 14 under the title "Update to Sexual Content policy article (October 2026)". After the October revision, the policy page carried six subsections of restricted and allowed examples on sexually suggestive elements and themes, from moaning in audio to fruit used as a stand-in for body parts. Today's revision leaves that section untouched. It turns instead to the four categories above it, which until now listed only what was restricted.
Neither entry carries an effective date or a transition period, and neither identifies which lines are new. The help center repeats a standard caveat at the top of both pages: translated versions exist, but "The English version is the official language used to enforce Google Ads policies." The comparison in this article sets the October text of the policy page against the text Google published today.

What changed, line by line
The opening paragraph and the serving conditions
The first paragraph of the policy was rewritten. In October it read: "Ads should respect user preferences and comply with legal regulations. Google Ads restricts certain kinds of sexual content in ads and destinations, which will only show in limited scenarios based on user search queries, user age and local laws where the ad is being served. Ads must not target minors."
Today it reads: "Google Ads restricts certain kinds of sexual content in ads and destinations. These kinds of ads will show in limited scenarios based on the user's preferences, search queries, and age, as well as local laws where the ad is being served. Ads must not target minors."
The standalone statement that ads "should respect user preferences" is gone. Preferences now appear as one of the named factors that decide whether an ad serves. The word "destinations" survives, which keeps landing pages in scope alongside the ad itself.
The serving conditions for each tier keep their substance but change their order. In October, strongly restricted content would "only serve based on the following conditions", listed as the user's age, local laws, SafeSearch settings and, last, "The user's sexual content search queries". Today the list reads: "Sexual content search queries", "Age", "Local laws (where the ad is being served)" and "SafeSearch settings". Search queries have moved from last to first. Google does not say whether the order carries any weight. Moderately restricted content keeps three conditions - age, local laws and SafeSearch settings - with no search query requirement.
A disclaimer changed with them. October's "All the examples provided in this article are non-exhaustive" became "Restricted and allowed examples are intended for guidance. They are not complete lists." The new sentence names both lists, which matches the page now that every category has two.
The warning regime is unchanged in substance. October's "A warning will be issued at least 7 days prior to any suspension of your account" now reads "Google will issue a warning at least 7 days before suspending your account." The seven days remain; Google is now the named actor.
Definitions, then and now
Examples, counted
Counts are a PPC Land tally of the bulleted examples on each version of the page.
Nudity: sheer fabric in, stick figures out
The three restricted examples for nudity are unchanged: "Visible genitalia", "Visible female breasts" and "Visible buttocks". The definition is not. Exposed intimate body parts now include those "visible through sheer clothing", alongside the blurred and censored representations already listed in October. A see-through garment, in other words, does not move an image out of the strongest tier - the one where an ad serves only when the user has typed a sexual content search query.
Two allowed examples appear for the first time. The first covers "Simple or unrealistic sketches (like stick figures or line drawings) of figures without clothes that don't include details of intimate body parts and don't look like real nudity". The second covers "Depictions of nudity in the context of classical or historic art (like paintings, drawings, or sculptures), which has a wide general audience for all ages".
The sketch exemption carries two conditions, not one. A drawing must lack intimate detail and must not "look like real nudity". On that wording, medium alone does not decide the question - realism does. The art exemption matters to museums, auction houses, galleries and publishers of art books, whose ads can depict classical nudes without falling into the strongly restricted tier.
Partial nudity: thong versus tanga
Both restricted examples were reworded. "Lower or outer curve of the female breast exposed" is now "Lower or side curve of an exposed female breast". "Majority of buttocks exposed" now continues: "such as a person depicted wearing a thong or g-string".
The allowed list draws its line through the underwear drawer. "Majority of buttocks covered, such as a person depicted wearing bikini cut or tanga cut underwear" is allowed. So is "Partial breast nudity in an educational context, such as how to perform a breast self-exam, or in the context of breastfeeding."
For swimwear and underwear retailers, the cut of a garment shown on a model now decides, in writing, which side of the line a product image sits. Breast health charities, screening services and makers of breastfeeding products have a sentence they can point to. Partial nudity remains moderately restricted rather than banned: ads in the category can still serve, subject to age, local law and SafeSearch.
Sexual merchandise: what the product is for, and how it is sold
The definition grew by two words, from merchandise "intended to enhance sexual activity" to merchandise "intended to enhance or facilitate sexual activity". The restricted list grew from three short entries to four longer ones:
- "Sex toys, such as intimate massagers and insertive devices"
- "Devices that aid in enabling sex, such as vacuum erection devices or sex position pillows"
- "Condoms promoted in terms of enhancing sexual pleasure"
- "Sexual fetish lingerie, primarily intended for sexual experiences, which may include features like hardwear, latex materials, or openings to accentuate particular body parts"
The page spells "hardwear" that way. One October example, "Sexual enhancers", no longer appears on the list. Google does not explain the removal, and the page states that its examples "are not complete lists", so the omission alone does not establish that such products are now treated differently.
Five allowed examples are new: "Pelvic floor muscle trainers"; "Hormone replacement therapy, where there may also be secondary sexual benefits"; "Non-sexual health or wellness treatments focused on genitalia, such as feminine hygiene wipes or yeast infection treatments"; "Condoms promoted in terms of preventing pregnancy or STIs, without sexually suggestive marketing"; and "Lingerie generally intended to be worn under clothing, such as corsets, garter belts, and bras that incorporate lace or similar decorative elements".
Condoms are the clearest case of a rule that follows the pitch rather than the product. The same box sits on the restricted list when the ad sells pleasure and on the allowed list when it sells protection - provided the marketing is not sexually suggestive. Lingerie works the same way. Lace bras, corsets and garter belts are allowed; garments built "primarily" for sexual experiences, with latex or openings, are not.
The hormone replacement therapy entry is relevant to menopause care brands, which often discuss libido among other symptoms. The policy page does not address how its allowed examples interact with Google's separate rules on healthcare and prescription medicines, which continue to govern ads for prescription treatments. On the sexual health side, Google in 2024 expanded its sexual health and wellness policy so that personal lubricant ads could run on the Display Network and YouTube in nine markets, and allowed prescription treatments for erectile dysfunction and hypoactive sexual desire disorder in Canada, New Zealand and the US.
Sexual entertainment: from live streaming to webcams
The old definition, "Online or offline sexually suggestive entertainment", has been replaced by "Products or services designed for adult entertainment or sexual arousal." The restricted list grew from four entries to seven:
- "Strip clubs"
- "Erotic dancing like lap dancing, pole dancing, burlesque, and stripteases"
- "Adult film festivals"
- "Published erotica, such as books or magazines, that include sexualized language or descriptions of sex in their promotion"
- "Sexually suggestive phone sex and webcams"
- "Adult games with sexualized narratives, including role-playing games and video or board games"
- "Sexualized gifts and party favors"
Two October entries changed in the process. "Sexually suggestive live streaming" no longer appears; "Sexually suggestive phone sex and webcams" does. "Sexually suggestive role-playing games" became the broader "Adult games with sexualized narratives", which reaches video games and board games by name.
Three allowed examples follow: "Non-sexualized games with references to love or romance without more passionate imagery"; "Erotic dancing for exercise or athletics, such as pole-dancing classes"; and "Publications such as books or magazines with sexual content that are promoted without sexualized language or descriptions of sex".
The publishing example is the most consequential for advertisers outside the adult industry. A book's content does not decide its tier; its promotion does. A novel with sex scenes can be advertised without restriction under this policy, as long as the ad copy stays clear of sexualized language and descriptions of sex. That mirrors the suggestive language subsection, which already allows euphemisms such as "making love" and "spending the night". The pole dancing pair mirrors the body movements subsection too, where "Sexy dancing for athletics or exercise" was already allowed. A pole routine on a club stage and the same routine in a fitness class sit on opposite lists.
