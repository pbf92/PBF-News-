TITLE: AWS puts fraud filters and device data inside the ad auction
OUTLET: PPC Land

Amazon Web Services on November 19, 2026, made AWS RTB Fabric Modules generally available through AWS Marketplace, allowing ad tech platforms to attach outside vendors' fraud screening, device data and placement quality scores to bid traffic as it crosses Amazon's private auction network. The first three modules come from Pixalate, WURFL by ScientiaMobile and Picnic, and their charges appear on the customer's AWS bill instead of on separate vendor invoices.
In Short
AWS now lets companies that buy and sell ads over its RTB Fabric network add data from outside vendors, such as fraud screening from Pixalate, device details from ScientiaMobile and placement quality scores from Picnic, to each bid request as it passes through. That matters to ad tech platforms and publishers because each new data source has until now needed its own integration, contract and engineering work, and AWS says the modules run within single-digit milliseconds. The change is in the plumbing and the paperwork: vendors list once on AWS Marketplace and AWS does the billing, although no prices have been published and a link holds only two modules under the default quota.
What AWS put on the shelf
The announcement appeared on the AWS for Industries blog under the headline "Introducing AWS RTB Fabric Modules available through AWS Marketplace: AdTech partner intelligence, inline in the bid path." Three people signed it: Sergio Serra, the product leader for AWS RTB Fabric; Jonathan Harmms, a product marketer for the advertising and marketing industry at AWS; and Trevor Dyck, head of product for AWS RTB Fabric. According to AWS, the modules entered general availability to give ad tech companies and their partners "ad intelligence and decisioning capabilities" inside their real-time bidding workloads.
AWS frames the problem in terms of signals. Brand safety, fraud, device and inventory quality data determine "which impressions advertising technology (AdTech) companies win, what they pay, and whether they overpay for the wrong ones," according to the post. Moving those signals from specialist vendors into the path of a bid has been slow and expensive. "Each new partner means a custom integration, its own contract, and weeks to months of engineering work, and each one adds latency to a bid path where every millisecond counts," the post states. The result, AWS argues, is engineering teams maintaining integration code when they could be building their own platforms.
The proposed fix is a subscription. Demand-side platforms, supply-side platforms and publishers running on RTB Fabric can now, according to AWS, "discover, configure, and deploy partner signals directly to their RTB workloads in minutes, with no custom integration, running inline at single-digit millisecond latency." Third-party module providers, on the other side of the transaction, can reach RTB Fabric customers "from a single AWS Marketplace listing."
AWS lists four benefits for the platforms that buy modules. They can be found on AWS Marketplace and attached to an RTB Fabric link without custom integration code. They filter invalid requests before the bidder deserializes and evaluates them, which AWS says cuts wasted compute. They run inline on the RTB Fabric network, colocated with the bidding workload, "with no extra network hops." And partner charges appear on a single AWS bill, so ad operations and procurement teams manage fewer vendor contracts and invoices.
For vendors, the offer is distribution. Module providers gain access to "programmatic advertising workloads that were previously difficult to reach through traditional integrations or a software catalog," according to the post. A provider builds a module once and reaches customers through one listing, and AWS says it keeps full control of its data and intellectual property, with no per-customer integrations and no colocated infrastructure of its own.
Three vendors, three kinds of signal
Modules plug into RTB Fabric links to filter, enrich or observe bid requests before they reach a bidder, according to AWS. Each module is a separate building block, which means a platform can choose a different provider for each signal "instead of accepting whatever comes bundled in a single stack."
Pixalate, which AWS describes as a global platform specializing in privacy compliance, ad fraud prevention and digital ad supply chain data intelligence, supplies a module called IVT Blocking, Bid Enrichment & CTV Show-Level Intelligence. It screens invalid traffic before it reaches the bidder and adds the show, channel, genre and content rating to connected TV bid requests. Coverage, according to AWS, spans more than 770,000 US streaming TV shows across more than 17,900 FAST and linear channels.
Those numbers have moved quickly. When Pixalate released its OpenEPG DB 1.0 and Pre-Bid API on September 21, 2026, the database covered more than 13,900 channels and more than 580,000 shows. By PPC Land's calculation, the November figures are about 4,000 channels (29%) and 190,000 shows (33%) higher in under two months, although all four numbers are floors rather than exact counts. The September release already described an in-bidstream version for RTB Fabric with a latency of about 5 milliseconds at the median and 12 milliseconds at the 99th percentile, against a 10-millisecond median target for Pixalate's separate REST interface. Weeks later, the company added a clawback warning label to its ratings of apps and sites, based on buyers' invalid traffic disputes.
WURFL by ScientiaMobile draws on more than 120,000 device profiles. Its module, WURFL Device Signals, recognizes the device from the request's own fields and adds consistent attributes to OpenRTB bid requests as they pass through RTB Fabric: device type, make and model, operating system, browser, screen and bot flags. On AWS's product page for modules, ScientiaMobile states that more than 99% of requests are enriched, a figure it attributes to web bid traffic.
Picnic Inventory Quality, or PIQ, scores inventory across more than 100 signals "right down to placement level, using proprietary data," according to AWS. The module matches every bid request to the most specific placement it can and returns a PIQ Score in real time. It works in two ways. In filtering mode, PIQ decides which requests are worth passing on, which reduces the volume a platform pays for and processes; in enrichment mode, the score and its signals are added to each request so that the bidder can weigh placement quality alongside audience and price. The module covers display and video, with "no feed or integration to build."
How a module reaches a link
According to the AWS RTB Fabric User Guide, attaching a vendor module happens in two stages, subscription and attachment. The console path the guide describes has six steps:
- Under Module marketplace in the console's navigation pane, the Discover modules page lists available modules, searchable by name, vendor or function and filterable by category. Choosing View details opens a module's page.
- On that page, View subscription options, or Try for free where a vendor offers it, leads to pricing and terms. Accepting the offer creates an AWS Marketplace agreement, and the charges appear on the customer's AWS bill.
- Subscribed modules appear on the My modules page, where a Source column shows whether each one came from AWS Marketplace or is built in. Subscribing alone does not change traffic, according to the guide; a module processes requests only once it is added to a link's flow.
- From My modules, Attach to link opens the attachment screen. Gateway type stays on Responder gateway, the gateway and link are selected (only active links are shown), and Configure flows opens the flow editor.
- On the Configure flow screen, under "Modules you can add", the module version is selected and Copy snippet produces a configuration fragment. That fragment is pasted into the modules list in Flow configuration (JSON), adjusted according to the vendor's instructions, and submitted.
- The change then rolls out asynchronously. The link keeps serving its previous flows until the update applies, and if the update fails, the link returns to an ACTIVE state with its old configuration.
The command line follows the same sequence in three calls, according to the guide: get-link to retrieve a lockToken, put-link-flows with that token and the new flow definition, which returns a PENDING_UPDATE status and an updateId, and get-update to track progress. Renewals and cancellations are handled in the AWS Marketplace console, and the guide notes that AWS Marketplace and AWS License Manager permissions are needed alongside RTB Fabric permissions.
Rules written into the documentation
The guide is precise about limits, and some of them qualify the marketing. "A link has at most one flow," it states, and the modules in a flow count against a "Modules per flow quota, which is 2 by default." Going over the quota returns a ServiceQuotaExceededException. With three vendor modules on sale at launch, a link on the default quota can run at most two of them at the same time, by PPC Land's reading of the guide; the page does not say how a higher quota is granted.
Order matters too. In a sequential pipeline, "modules run in order, and each module sees the previous module's output." In a phased pipeline, "phases run in order, and the modules in a phase run at the same time on the same request." In either design, "A response from any module, such as a no-bid, ends the flow."
Failure handling is set per module through a failureMode field: "OPEN continues the flow, and CLOSED answers the request with a no-bid response." Vendor modules "default to OPEN." In the guide's worked example, a module call that fails or times out lets the flow continue without that module's result, while the link's own timeout still applies to the whole request. An activationPercentage value between 0 and 100, defaulting to 100, sets the share of requests a module sees, sampled independently for each request. Vendor enrichment is written into the bid request under ext.rtbfabric.modules followed by the module's name, with an outcome of ALLOW or DROP.
Changes are all or nothing. "Submitting replaces the link's whole flow," the guide warns, and "The update can take up to 30 minutes, and longer in some cases." That is a ceiling for applying a configuration change, not the engineering timeline AWS compares itself against when it promises integration "in minutes, not months," but it is a longer wait than the "few clicks" Serra described in a LinkedIn post.
Vendor modules join modules that AWS supplies itself. According to AWS's general availability notice of October 23, 2025, RTB Fabric launched with three built-in modules aimed at optimizing traffic, improving bid efficiency and increasing bid response rates. The guide currently documents three built-in modules.
The network underneath
RTB Fabric is just over a year old. AWS launched the service on October 23, 2025 as a managed network for bidding workloads in six regions - US East (N. Virginia), US West (Oregon), Asia Pacific (Singapore), Asia Pacific (Tokyo), Europe (Frankfurt) and Europe (Ireland) - priced per billion transactions with no upfront commitments. The November post calls it "a fully managed service purpose-built for real-time bidding workloads that delivers single-digit millisecond latency." According to AWS, Amazon Ads, GumGum, Kargo, MobileFuse, TripleLift, Viant, Yieldmo and others use it to reach supply and demand partners on a private network and "save up to 80% compared to standard networking costs, without colocated infrastructure or upfront commitments."
The service has picked up features and customers since. AWS let bidders keep their own domains in May 2026 by pointing existing endpoints at a regional RTB Fabric gateway through a CNAME record, so that partners change nothing on their side.
