TITLE: KPMG could earn up to $5.1m finding Character.ai a California tax refund
OUTLET: PPC Land

Character Technologies, the bankrupt company behind the Character.ai chatbot app, asked the federal bankruptcy court in Delaware on November 24 to approve KPMG LLP as its tax adviser on terms that pay only if money comes back from California. KPMG's task is to look for refunds of California corporate tax linked to how the company handled income from its August 1, 2024 licensing agreement with Google. The fee would be a falling share of whatever the state returns: 30% of the first $2 million, stepping down to 10% on amounts between $18 million and $35 million. At the top of that scale, KPMG would collect $5.1 million, according to PPC Land's calculation from the filing's fee table.
In Short
Character.ai wants a judge to let it pay KPMG a cut of any California tax refund it wins on income from its 2024 Google deal, with nothing owed if no refund comes. Any money recovered would go into the bankrupt company's estate, the pot that pays its creditors, among them the tech and marketing vendors the app relied on. The judge is due to consider the request on December 15, but the filing's own timeline shows that a refund the state disputes could take up to 10.5 years to arrive.
What the company is asking for
The request is Docket No. 244 in case 26-11603 (BLS) at the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware. It runs to 55 pages: an 11-page application signed by chief executive Colin M. Adams, a two-page notice, KPMG's engagement letter and standard terms (Exhibit A, 22 pages including two conflict-check schedules), a sworn declaration by KPMG's Oksana G. Jaffe (Exhibit B, 13 pages) and a proposed court order (Exhibit C, seven pages).
The company asks the court to do three things. The first is to authorize "the retention and employment of KPMG LLP" under the engagement letter, "effective as of the Petition Date," meaning October 12, 2026, the day Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11 with Disney's $20 million bid for a license to its intellectual property already lined up. The second is to waive the information requirements of Local Rule 2016-1(d), which in practice means KPMG would not have to keep the detailed hour-by-hour time records that bankruptcy professionals normally file for the refund work. The third is the usual "related relief."
The company still runs its own affairs during the case as a debtor-in-possession, which is why the request comes from management rather than from a trustee. According to the application, the US Trustee appointed a three-member official committee of unsecured creditors on November 4, 2026, and nobody has asked for a trustee or examiner to be appointed.
The notice sets an objection deadline of December 8, 2026, at 4:00 p.m. ET. A hearing is scheduled for December 15 at 10:00 a.m. ET before Judge Brendan Linehan Shannon in Courtroom No. 1 on the sixth floor of 824 N. Market Street in Wilmington. The notice states that "IF YOU FAIL TO RESPOND IN ACCORDANCE WITH THIS NOTICE, THE COURT MAY GRANT THE RELIEF REQUESTED IN THE APPLICATION WITHOUT FURTHER NOTICE OR A HEARING."
The Google money behind the refund claim
The engagement letter is dated May 15, 2026, five months before the bankruptcy. It was signed through DocuSign by John Chatowski, senior legal counsel at Character Technologies, and by Oksana Jaffe for KPMG, and is addressed to David Rothenberg, the company's general counsel, at 2114 Broadway in Redwood City, California. Its first task reads:
"Review and analyze Client's California 12/2024 franchise tax return to identify potential tax refunds in connection with the sourcing of income related to the Google Licensing Agreement dated August 1, 2024."
Some plain words help here. California charges companies a franchise tax, which works much like a state income tax on corporate profits. A company that earns money from customers in more than one place has to decide how much of each stream of income belongs to California. Tax specialists call that step "sourcing." If more of a payment was assigned to California than the rules require, the California bill for that year was too high, and the difference can be claimed back. The timeline attached to the letter makes the method explicit: the second step is to "Prepare and file amended return with sourcing position and refund claim amount."
The Google agreement is the deal that reshaped the company two years before it failed. PPC Land reported in December 2024 that the $2.7 billion deal announced in August 2024 combined a licensing agreement with the return of Character.ai's founders to Google. Arrangements of that kind, in which a large company takes a start-up's people and a license to its technology without buying the company, are often described as an acqui-hire. The KPMG filing does not mention the $2.7 billion figure. It does not say how much of the money was taxable income for Character Technologies, how the company sourced it on its 2024 California return, or how much California tax it paid.
The request reached the docket on the same day that the US Trustee, the Justice Department official who oversees bankruptcy cases, filed an objection to the Disney sale that, among other points, said the company had not explained what became of the money received from Google in August 2024. The KPMG application does not answer that point. It does show that income tied to the Google agreement appeared on the company's 2024 California return, and that the company thinks part of the tax paid on it may be recoverable.
How the fee works
The engagement letter states: "These services are provided on a contingent basis. The contingent fee will be equal to the percent of the "Benefits Received" as indicated in Exhibit A attached." The fee table itself sits in Exhibit B to the letter, and the application reproduces it.
Exhibit B lists a minimum fee of "$0." A footnote explains how the bands work: "The contingency fee only applies to the incremental aggregate Benefits Received equal to the "Start $" amount and up to but not including the "Up to $" amount." Put simply, each rate applies only to the slice of money inside its own band, the way income tax brackets work. KPMG does not get 30% of everything; it gets 30% of the first $2 million, 20% of the next $8 million, and so on.

For scale, KPMG's maximum would be about a quarter of the $20 million Disney has offered for its license, and nearly three times the $1.75 million sale fee the court approved for investment bank Houlihan Lokey on November 23. Both comparisons are PPC Land calculations.
What counts as a "benefit"
The fee is only as large as the "Benefits Received," so the definition carries the weight. The engagement letter states:
"For purposes of this Agreement, "Benefits Received" includes refunds received plus interest received from the state taxing authority thereon, credits taken to offset proposed assessments, and reductions in assessments, interest, and penalties resulting from issues identified by KPMG that are substantively considered and ultimately sustained or otherwise allowed by the findings of the state taxing authority."
So the base includes the refund itself, the interest California pays on it, tax credits used against any new bill the state proposes, and any cut in assessments, interest or penalties that follows from KPMG's work.
Several lines set limits or close gaps:
- Research credits are carved out. "Benefits Received" do not include "any changes to Client's earned or claimed California R&D Tax Credits." Work on those credits is billed "based on the actual time incurred to complete the work at KPMG's standard hourly rates," and only after written approval from the company. Any other benefit outside the scope is billed the same way.
- Refunds not pursued do not count. "Benefits Received" do not include "refunds identified but ultimately not pursued, either after their identification or through the applicable refund process."
- Unusable benefits still count. "Fees are not dependent upon Client's ability to utilize such "Benefits Received" on future return filings." For a company that may stop operating, that sentence means the fee is not reduced if there are no future California returns on which to use a credit.
- A refund dropped and later claimed still pays. "If Client decides to forego refunds identified and claims for those refunds are subsequently filed, used as credits to reduce or offset assessments or proposed assessments, or otherwise used to benefit Client, Client agrees to pay KPMG according to the above contingent fee arrangement."
- A fallback exists. If a contingent fee came into conflict with professional standards or law, the parties would agree in writing on another fee arrangement, and KPMG would stop work until they did.
When KPMG would be paid
Payment waits for the state. The letter's payment schedule reads: "Fees will be billed by KPMG only after the relevant taxing authority authorizes or approves the Benefits Received and the refund payment is made by the State of California. If the findings of the relevant taxing authority concerning the issues in Client's claim for refund are later revised, the fee will be adjusted accordingly."
On top of the contingent fee, the company would pay "a technology recovery fee equal to 2.5% of the fees for the Services, to be applied up to a maximum fee for Services amount of $1,000,000, payable at the same time the Benefits Received payment is made." Read literally, the charge applies to fees up to $1 million, which would put the most it could reach at $25,000. That is a PPC Land reading of the wording. The application and the Jaffe declaration both print the threshold as "$1,00,000," a figure that is not a valid dollar amount; the engagement letter, which the application says controls in any inconsistency, shows $1,000,000.
KPMG would also be reimbursed for expenses. The application lists "travel, lodging, third party duplications, messenger, and telephone charges." The declaration's list is broader, including "meals, lodging, travel, photocopying, delivery service, postage, vendor charges, value added tax, reasonable and documented outside counsel legal fees and expenses." The declaration adds that KPMG "may also seek compensation for its professional reasonable fees incurred in connection with this Application and future fee applications, if any." The standard terms say expenses are billed at cost, with "no markup."
The steps, and how long they could take
According to the engagement letter, KPMG's work on the 2024 return follows seven steps:
- Review and analyze the company's California franchise tax return for the year ending December 2024 to identify potential refunds linked to the sourcing of the Google license income.
- Work with the company to decide which refund opportunities go into a "claim for refund" filed with the California Franchise Tax Board.
- Prepare one or more "claim for refund" packages or amended returns, as needed.
- Coordinate processing of the claims, or file the amended returns, with the Franchise Tax Board.
- Assist with any audit inquiries and any protest, appeal or settlement dispute that follows.
- Develop an approach for handling any audit, protest, appeal or settlement, and meet the board's staff as needed.
- Represent the company before the Franchise Tax Board and, if needed, the Office of Tax Appeals, or in alternative dispute resolution if the matter is not resolved at the examination level.
