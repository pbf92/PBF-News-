TITLE: ‘It’s Not Just a Song on the Radio’: How Music Became A Main Character In ‘Grand Theft Auto VI’
OUTLET: Billboard

For decades, Rockstar Games was chasing one song.
In 2002, when the gaming behemoth was developing Grand Theft Auto: Vice City, the fourth installment of its iconic series, it had successfully secured classics like Michael Jackson’s “Billie Jean” and Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five’s “The Message” for the game. But one record remained tantalizingly out of reach: The Cars’ 1984 single “Drive,” the highest-charting of the power-pop band’s career. A favorite of Rockstar co-founder Sam Houser, the haunting, synth-driven song encapsulated the neon-soaked ’80s world the developer was expanding. Rockstar couldn’t clear the track then — but more than two decades later, the wait is finally over.
On Nov. 19, Rockstar made its long-awaited return to Vice City with the epic, stunningly cinematic Grand Theft Auto VI. Even before breathless reviews of the game flooded the internet, GTAVI was predicted to be the biggest entertainment launch in history, with possibly more than $5 billion in first week sales alone. And when players turn on one of the game’s defining features — the in-game radio — “Drive” is the first song they hear.
For Rockstar vice president of music Ivan Pavlovich, clearing “Drive” wasn’t just a licensing victory — it was a testament to the company’s unwavering prioritization of music, the relationships it has cultivated throughout the industry and the meticulous curation that has transformed Grand Theft Auto into one of the past 20 years’ most culture-saturating, riveting and lucrative products. “Music,” Pavlovich says, “is the through line through all of that.”
That’s never been more true than with GTAVI, which Pavlovich calls “the biggest project we’ve ever done,” with, by his count, “over 800 songs in the game right now.” With 23 radio stations — 21 for music and two for talk radio — along with in-game podcasts to crank up as you’re cruising through the streets of Leonida (GTA’s Florida stand-in), plus even TV shows to watch when you’re not, it’s Rockstar’s most ambitious music-driven experience yet.
In 1997, video game developers David Jones and Mike Dailly helped bring the first Grand Theft Auto to life at Scottish gaming studio DMA Design (now Rockstar North). Originally published by BMG Interactive, the 2D game introduced players to an open-world experience built around driving, shootouts and criminal escapades. Four years later (and following 1999’s Grand Theft Auto 2), the series underwent a seismic transformation with Grand Theft Auto III, which introduced a fully realized 3D environment.
The following year, Rockstar upped the ante with 2002’s Grand Theft Auto: Vice City, recruiting acclaimed actor Ray Liotta to voice main character Thomas “Tommy” Vercetti. Set in the fictional, Miami-inspired Vice City during the 1980s, the game captured the era’s drug-fueled excess, flashy nightlife and colorful personalities — and music became a major part of its appeal, as Rockstar leaned into the decade’s eclectic sounds, spanning pop, rock, metal, hip-hop and R&B. With a slew of radio stations available in-game — from pop-driven Flash FM, to the hip-hop of Wildstyle Pirate Radio — players could cruise through the city while switching between their favorite records, all licensed for Rockstar’s use in the game.
With 2004’s Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas, Rockstar moved into the ‘90s, leaning heavily into West Coast hip-hop culture and partnering with Interscope Records for the game’s soundtrack releases. San Andreas kept Vice City’s radio stations concept intact but added more star power, recruiting Public Enemy’s Chuck D, Parliament-Funkadelic’s George Clinton and Guns N’ Roses’ Axl Rose as DJs. By 2008, when Grand Theft Auto IV arrived with another robust musical lineup, including DJ Premier and New York radio maestros DJ Mister Cee and DJ Green Lantern helming their own radio stations, Rockstar wasn’t simply licensing songs — it was deepening its relationships with artists, producers and labels as it created original music, too.
“Music is part of the DNA of these games,” says Pavlovich, who credits Houser’s passion for music as a crucial driver behind GTA‘s musical identity. And as Grand Theft Auto evolved into one of gaming’s most successful series, Rockstar in turn grew more confident in its ability to create original music. In 2013, Grand Theft Auto V introduced the series’ first original score, which Pavlovich recruited hip-hop producer The Alchemist, pioneering electronic group Tangerine Dream, rapper Oh No and composer Woody Jackson to craft together. Together, they made more than 20 hours of original music. Rockstar also gave producers like Flying Lotus their own in-game radio stations and issued music from the game on CD and vinyl through Mass Appeal.
And then, Rockstar pressed pause. After two decades of regular GTA releases, the series went more than a decade without a new mainline installment — due to the company’s prioritization of developing Red Dead Redemption 2 (which came in 2018), its ambition to scale GTAVI and production challenges during the pandemic — which meant that the stakes, and anticipation, for GTAVI grew exponentially.
Early projections from market research firm Newzoo estimated that GTAVI could generate $3.25 to $5.2 billion in first-week sales (in fact, its first day sales were $3.79 billion). And with music playing such a pivotal role in the series’ success, expectations were equally high for how Rockstar would soundtrack its biggest release yet. With GTAVI, Rockstar had two major missions of its own to execute: creating a score that would capture the emotional journey of the game’s protagonists, the Bonnie and Clyde-esque couple Jason and Lucia; and building a musical world reflective of a reimagined Vice City and the booming global sounds that now define it.
The latter would be especially time-intensive, but Rockstar had an advantage: multiple global superstars love Grand Theft Auto, having grown up with the series. For the 34-track soundtrack, Rockstar and its label partner Atlantic Records recruited a stacked roster including SZA, Tame Impala, JENNIE, Dua Lipa, Lana Del Rey, Young Thug, Cardi B and many more. As for the score, Pavlovich always had a gut feeling that French dance duo Justice had the right tools to not only reflect the ’80s era but also elevate the sound of Leonida with a modern edge.
“We went to them as fans first,” says Pavlovich. “There was nobody else who could do this but Justice. Maybe it took a little while to find it, but it was always Justice. It was their score to make.”
On Nov. 20, a day after the release of the game itself, Grand Theft Auto VI: The Album landed on streaming services. On Spotify’s coveted New Music Friday playlist, Dua Lipa landed the leadoff spot with “Go Easy” from the album, followed by new songs from Playboi Carti and Lana Del Rey, along with a new Justice song from the official score and one featuring a certain iconic crooner. And on the morning of Nov. 23, Atlantic and Rockstar surprise dropped GTA VI: The Album (Deluxe Edition), with 23 more new, original tracks — adding artists like Slayyyter, Anitta, The Dare, Offset, 21 Savage and many more to the mix. GTAVI’s musical takeover had officially begun.
On an otherwise regular morning, Justice’s Xavier de Rosnay and Gaspard Augé found a text from their managers with a simple question: would they like to compose the score for the biggest video game in history?
“Our first reaction was, ‘Of course we want to,’ because we knew it would be an anticipated piece of entertainment,” says de Rosnay. It would also be a commitment that ultimately occupied years of their lives. “When we started working, I had one kid,” de Rosnay says with a laugh. “When we finished, I had three.”
De Rosnay and Augé had long been interested in scoring a film, television show or video game, but GTAVI would be their first time actually doing so. “Rockstar liked the idea that we’ve never properly worked on a score before, because that could mean we don’t have the habits of people who think, ‘but that’s the way we do it,’ ” de Rosnay says. “We were coming completely fresh.”
The GTA series has a long, rich history with electronic music, with artists including Soulwax, Moodymann, The Blessed Madonna, Solomun and many more contributing to the game’s radio stations over the years and some even appearing as themselves in the game. Rockstar also has a long partnership with Ibiza’s venerable CircoLoco, and the party’s founder co-hosts GTAVI’s CircoLoco Records Radio. Meanwhile, the soundtrack’s dance roots go far beyond Justice itself, featuring contributions from Fred again.., PinkPantheress and other dance stars, and Miami’s hallowed nightclub Space appears in the game as The Vault.
Creating the score for GTAVI was a particularly daunting task for Justice, given that “it took us 20 years to release four albums that accumulate to maybe three and a half hours of music,” says de Rosnay. The team at Rockstar, also famous for taking a long time to make things, said they weren’t worried about it. To get started, de Rosnay and Augé went to Rockstar’s New York headquarters, where they spent several days watching drafts of the game and wrapping their minds around Leonida, Jason and Lucia and how their music would fit into the sprawling world Rockstar was building.
“We thought we had an idea of what the game was,” says de Rosnay, “and actually spending a week looking at it, we left the office with a completely different view of what needed to be done” in terms of mood and how their music would be used, particularly with respect to each character’s narrative arc and the game’s overall scale.
“I would guess that usually when you start a score, you start defining a sound that’s going be [central] to it,” de Rosnay continues. “That’s impossible in a game like this. The score has to cover as many styles and genres as there are parts of the cities, situations and variety of people.”
Justice worked in near-total secrecy with a tiny team at Rockstar made up of Pavlovich, Rob Nelson, the co-studio head of Rockstar North and one of the three creative leads on GTAVI, and Houser. (When they spent a month in New York working on the game earlier this year, de Rosnay’s sister-in-law — having no idea what he was doing in town — thought he was going into the city every day to work at Google.) Justice joined the project when big sections of the game were still being written, rewritten and moved around. As such, the duo worked by scoring large swaths of the game piece by piece, returning to each as Rockstar further developed it.
“We would be revisiting constantly and adapting it to new developments in terms of writing,” says de Rosnay. “We cycled through all of this for years, improving on and changing things, because what worked two months ago didn’t work anymore.”
The scope of that task was staggering. For each scene, Justice had to account for all the ways a player might experience it. “We would watch a scene and determine with the team that an average player is going to spend, like, five minutes on this,” de Rosnay says. “Someone who knows what they’re doing, they’re going to spend one minute, and someone who has no idea and gets lost spends 15 minutes.” To cover all scenarios, he says they “would deliver maybe 20 to 25 minutes of music, just to make sure that when things are being interactive, there’s no wrong parts or things that sound artificial.” Those minutes multiply across all the game’s branching possibilities — which is why, de Rosnay says, “we have no idea” how many hours of Justice’s music are in the game.
Much of this work happened while Justice was on the world tour for its 2024 album Hyperdrama — and de Rosnay and Augé had never previously produced on the road. With GTAVI, however, de Rosnay says that “every minute we were not doing standalone Justice stuff or making kids was spent working on the score” in hotel rooms, airplanes and studios in Paris and New York.
