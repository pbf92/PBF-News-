TITLE: Maternal Vitiligo Associated With Higher Spontaneous Abortion Risk in Observational Review
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating maternal and perinatal outcomes has found that pregnant women diagnosed with vitiligo experience a higher risk of spontaneous abortion and a lower likelihood of live birth compared to women without the chronic autoimmune skin condition. Derived from retrospective cohort data, these findings identify elevated statistical associations for early pregnancy loss, though the review investigators emphasize that the overall certainty of evidence remains low. Furthermore, the observational evidence demonstrated no statistically detectable associations between maternal vitiligo and stillbirth, gestational diabetes mellitus, pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, intrauterine growth restriction, or caesarean delivery.
The investigation was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 standards and prospectively registered in PROSPERO under identifier CRD420251041206. Researchers systematically searched PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and ScienceDirect from database inception dates through 15 April 2025 to identify comparative cohort studies evaluating pregnancy outcomes between women with vitiligo and women without the condition. Because the retrieved cohort studies reported different effect measures across their individual analyses, adjusted effect estimates were prioritised and synthesised narratively, while an exploratory harmonised random-effects estimate was calculated specifically for the spontaneous abortion outcome.
In total, three retrospective cohort studies conducted in Israel, Korea, and Taiwan met the systematic review inclusion criteria, encompassing a combined sample of 243,820 pregnancies. Within this broad observational population, 5,913 pregnancies occurred among women diagnosed with vitiligo. The investigators reported that the total number of pregnancies contributing to each clinical outcome varied substantially across the literature. Notably, only two of the three studies, representing a combined subpopulation of 57,598 pregnancies, recorded and contributed evaluable comparative data regarding the specific outcomes of spontaneous abortion and live birth.
In the two studies reporting adjusted estimates, maternal vitiligo was consistently linked with an increased risk of spontaneous abortion and a reduced likelihood of achieving a live birth. For spontaneous abortion, one cohort reported an adjusted odds ratio of 1.25 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 1.15 to 1.36, while the second reported an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.16 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 1.10 to 1.26. An exploratory harmonised random-effects model calculated by the team yielded a pooled risk ratio of 1.18 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.13 to 1.24. For live birth, the cohorts showed an adjusted odds ratio of 0.87 (95 percent confidence interval 0.82 to 0.93) and an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.80 (95 percent confidence interval 0.73 to 0.82).
Beyond early pregnancy loss and live birth rates, the meta-analysis identified no statistically detectable associations between maternal vitiligo and stillbirth, pre-eclampsia or eclampsia, gestational diabetes mellitus, intrauterine growth restriction, or caesarean delivery, although several confidence intervals remained wide. For preterm delivery, the evidence was inconsistent and imprecise, showing a crude pooled risk ratio of 1.01 (95 percent confidence interval 0.57 to 1.78) and substantial heterogeneity with an I-squared value of 81 percent.
Methodological quality and study-level risk of bias were formally appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, while the certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the GRADE system. Certainty of evidence was rated as low for spontaneous abortion, live birth, and caesarean delivery, and very low for all remaining outcomes. Crude risk ratios were pooled only as exploratory sensitivity analyses utilizing DerSimonian–Laird random-effects models, with restricted maximum likelihood estimation and Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman adjustments applied as further sensitivity analyses to evaluate statistical stability.
The authors concluded that while a small observational evidence base suggests a possible association between maternal vitiligo and pregnancy loss, significant methodological caveats remain. In particular, residual confounding—especially confounding arising from coexisting autoimmune disease—and potential outcome misclassification within administrative data cannot be excluded. The current evidence does not support changes to routine antenatal care, and the researchers emphasized that adequately adjusted prospective studies employing standardised outcome definitions are needed to clarify these maternal and perinatal associations.
