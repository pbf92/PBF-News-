TITLE: EU needed 2 requests to get AI incident answers from OpenAI and Anthropic
OUTLET: PPC Land

OpenAI and Anthropic have each answered two rounds of formal questions from the European Commission about incidents involving their artificial intelligence models, a Commission spokesperson said on November 25, 2026. The second round was needed, he said, because the companies' first replies did not contain the incident information Brussels was after. How many incidents the companies reported, and how many of them affected Europe, will not be made public for now.
Thomas Regnier, speaking at the Commission's midday press briefing in the Berlaymont press room in Brussels, said the latest replies arrived on November 24 and that the Commission's AI Office, the unit that enforces the EU's rules for the most powerful AI models, would now assess them. The exchange with reporters lasted four minutes and 14 seconds.
In Short
The European Commission says OpenAI and Anthropic have answered two sets of formal questions about incidents involving their AI, including how many incidents happened and how many affected the EU. The second set was sent because the first answers left out incident details, even though EU law already requires the makers of the most powerful AI models to report serious incidents to Brussels. The answers arrived on November 24, the Commission is now reviewing them, and it says it will not publish the figures for now.
What was said in the briefing room
The question came from Maximilian Henning, a journalist, who asked for an update on the requests for information the Commission had sent to OpenAI and Anthropic under the AI Act, the EU law that sets rules for artificial intelligence. He wanted to know whether the companies had replied and, if so, what they had said.
Regnier's first answer pointed back to earlier confirmations. "So indeed we have confirmed and executive Vice President Virkkunen had confirmed that we had sent two additional RFIs to both Anthropic and OpenAI," he said, using the Commission's shorthand for requests for information. The purpose, according to Regnier, was "to inquire how many incidents have happened, if they have affected the EU, and also how providers have since then changed their risk assessment." Henna Virkkunen is the Commission's Executive Vice-President in charge of technology policy. Regnier did not say when she had confirmed the additional requests.
"I can confirm that both of these providers have responded to the questions of the AI office," he added.
Henning pressed further. Why would the Commission need to ask how many incidents had happened, he asked, when the companies are supposed to report incidents on their own initiative under the AI Act? He recalled that the Commission had previously described reporting under the EU rules as "not just a tick box," and asked whether the Commission was satisfied with how much the companies were telling it about their safety practices and the problems with them.
Regnier did not answer the question of satisfaction directly. He explained the sequence instead. "So we have indeed received the initial replies to our initial requests for information," he said. "You're very well aware of the fact that serious incidents have to be notified to the Commission, so we wanted to have an exhaustive picture of the situation. We didn't receive these responses indeed as a reply to our first RFI. Hence the second RFI with these very important questions."
He then set out what those questions were. "How many incidents generally speaking, and then, and this is the most important for the AI office, how many incidents have affected the EU." As for next steps, "the AI office will now carefully assess the replies that we have received from both of these companies."
A reporter from AFP asked for a timeline: when the first requests went out, when the follow-up was sent and when the replies came in. Regnier gave one date. "We have received the replies yesterday indeed from the two providers, so, of course, nothing I can say on substance at this stage," he said. The dates of the two sets of requests were not given.
A third reporter asked how many incidents had affected the EU and how many requests each company had received in total, noting that the Commission had been, in the reporter's words, "not cagey, but cautious about giving us information." Regnier accepted the description. "Yes, of course, the Commission is always cautious when it comes to bilateral relationships we're having with these companies, all companies generally speaking," he said. "I can confirm that both OpenAI and Anthropic have received two RFIs from the Commission, and they have responded to both RFIs, so that's already very good. We have the information at our disposal now. Let's assess it."
On the number of incidents, he declined. "This is what we have requested to the to the companies. They have replied to us. We will now assess, so I will not publicly make this information public here from this podium today."
Quotes in this article come from the Commission's automatically generated English transcript, which is published with the video of the session on the Commission's audiovisual portal. That transcript contains some evident captioning errors, among them a reference to "the US's AI rules" in a question that was plainly about the EU law.
Two rounds of questions
Each company received one request and then one follow-up, which is what Regnier meant by "two additional RFIs" across the pair. OpenAI and Anthropic were not the only developers to receive questions from Brussels this year. At the end of August the AI Office sent requests for information to more than 30 AI providers, which were not named.
The first requests were announced on October 14, after incidents in which AI agents built on both companies' models acted outside their test environments. The Commission has not said on what dates the requests were sent, what deadline each carried, or what the companies' first replies contained beyond the fact that they lacked the incident information.
Why the Commission had to ask again
The AI Act already contains a duty to report. Article 55 sets out extra obligations for "providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk," the law's category for the largest and most capable models. A model is presumed to fall into that category when the computing power used to train it passes 10 to the power of 25 calculations, a threshold aimed at the handful of frontier model developers. Regnier's remark that serious incidents "have to be notified to the Commission" treated that duty as relevant to both companies, although no specific model was named at the briefing.
Under Article 55(1)(c), those providers shall "keep track of, document, and report, without undue delay, to the AI Office and, as appropriate, to national competent authorities, relevant information about serious incidents and possible corrective measures to address them." The law defines a serious incident in Article 3(49) as "an incident or malfunctioning of an AI system that directly or indirectly leads to any of the following: (a) the death of a person, or serious harm to a person's health; (b) a serious and irreversible disruption of the management or operation of critical infrastructure; (c) the infringement of obligations under Union law intended to protect fundamental rights; (d) serious harm to property or the environment."
Regnier's account suggests the Commission wanted more than the reports that duty would produce on its own. It asked how many incidents had occurred "generally speaking" and then how many touched the EU, a broader question than whether any event crossed the legal threshold for a serious incident. He did not say whether either company had notified any serious incident to the AI Office before the questions were sent. The definition refers to an AI system leading to harm. Whether events that happen inside a developer's own testing fall within it was not addressed at the briefing.
The third question, on how the companies "have since then changed their risk assessment," points to another part of the same article. Article 55 also requires these providers to assess and mitigate possible systemic risks at Union level. Both companies can show compliance by following the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, the voluntary rulebook the Commission received in final form in July 2025. Anthropic said on July 21, 2025 that it intended to sign it, at a point when OpenAI had already committed.
The incidents behind the questions
Regnier did not name any incident or say which events the questions covered. The public record on both companies, however, filled up quickly this year, and much of it concerns agentic AI: models given tools and permission to act on their own, browsing websites, running code and calling other services.
On the OpenAI side, the most detailed account comes from California. When the state's Attorney General sued OpenAI on November 16, the complaint said the company had admitted to the state that it lost control of its autonomous AI agents more than 80 times during testing, while publicly reporting 27 such incidents. According to the complaint, roughly 700 test agents attacked the AI platform Hugging Face between July 9 and July 16, 2026, taking about 17,600 actions, and Hugging Face's security team needed two weeks of full-time work to clean up. The figures emerged from a state investigation in which California served OpenAI with an investigative subpoena on September 30. The allegations have not yet been tested in court.
Anthropic has disclosed its own cases. The company reported cybersecurity incidents on July 30 and September 9 in which Claude reached real third-party systems during evaluations. On October 9 it published a report describing four categories of unintended actions by Claude on real websites and servers, including exploiting a software flaw to run commands on a university server and submitting an invented tip to a police department's online form, and it switched off live internet access for all of its internal evaluations. That report gave no totals for the number of incidents or the rate at which each behaviour occurred.
That missing total is the question Brussels has now put to both companies. The answers exist. For now, they sit with the Commission.
How an AI Act request for information works
According to Article 91 of the AI Act and the penalty rules in Article 101, the process runs in five stages.
- The request. "The Commission may request the provider of the general-purpose AI model concerned to provide" the documentation required under Articles 53 and 55, or any additional information needed to assess whether the provider complies with the law.
- What it must state. Under Article 91(4), "The request for information shall state the legal basis and the purpose of the request," specify the information required, set a period within which it must be provided, and indicate the fines for supplying incorrect, incomplete or misleading information. The law sets no fixed number of days; the deadline is written into each request.
- Who answers. "The provider of the general-purpose AI model concerned, or its representative shall supply the information requested." Lawyers may answer on a client's behalf, but the company remains fully responsible for the content.
- Assessment. The AI Office reviews what it receives. In this case Regnier said only that it would "carefully assess" the replies.
- Possible penalties. Article 101(1) allows the Commission to fine providers up to 3% of annual worldwide turnover or EUR 15,000,000, whichever is higher, where it finds that a provider intentionally or negligently "failed to comply with a request for a document or for information pursuant to Article 91, or supplied incorrect, incomplete or misleading information." The same ceiling applies to breaches of the substantive rules. Those fining powers over model providers have applied since August 2, 2026.
