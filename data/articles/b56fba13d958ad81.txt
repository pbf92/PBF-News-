TITLE: Trial Innovation Network (TIN): What It Does
OUTLET: CASRAI

On this page
The Trial Innovation Network (TIN) is a national collaboration within the NIH Clinical and Translational Science Award (CTSA) Program, launched in 2016 and supported by the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS), that helps investigators plan and run multicenter clinical trials. It combines Trial Innovation Centers, a Recruitment Innovation Center and more than 60 CTSA hubs, and offers consultation, a single IRB system, standard contracting agreements and recruitment support. It is a support network for trial operations, not a single site or a trial-funding program.
Definition and scope
NCATS describes the network’s vision as addressing “roadblocks in clinical trials” and speeding the translation of interventions into treatment, and its working focus as operational innovation, excellence and collaboration that draw on the CTSA Program (NCATS, 2026-11-21). The founding perspective from NCATS staff, published in 2017, gives the mission as increasing the efficiency and effectiveness of clinical trials by “inventing and deploying new ways of doing the work of clinical trials” and by executing that work reliably, cost-effectively and within agreed timeframes (Shah et al., Clinical and Translational Science, 2017).
The scope is deliberately operational. The 2017 perspective lists the delays the network was built to address: prolonged institutional review board (IRB) review and contract procedures at trial start-up, infrastructure rebuilt for each study, protocols that demand many procedures and heavy data collection, and trial budgets that do not match the real cost of data collection, data management and study administration. The network therefore concentrates on how trials are run (review, contracting, budgeting, data management, recruitment and site selection), not on any one disease area. Over its first years it received proposals spanning 76 therapeutic areas across 20 NIH Institutes and Centers (Harris et al., JAMA Network Open, 2023).
NCATS also describes the network as “a national laboratory” in which CTSA hubs and investigators can study the process of conducting trials. That second role matters: the network does not only deliver services, it is meant to test operational ideas, measure them and spread the ones that work.
Who runs it: the components of the network
NCATS lists the network as composed of Trial Innovation Centers (TICs), a Recruitment Innovation Center (RIC), more than 60 CTSA hubs and hundreds of clinical trial partner sites. The table below maps each component to the role the sources assign it.

The 2017 and 2023 papers describe three Trial Innovation Centers and one Recruitment Innovation Center, so the number of TICs depends on the date of the source.
How the network works in practice
Intake and consultation
According to the 2023 account by network investigators, an investigator registers a request for TIN engagement through an online submission form. A series of meetings between the relevant experts, coordinated by the TICs, the RIC and the investigator team, follows, and the investigators receive written guidance customised to the trial. The TIN then makes a collaborative decision on whether the consultation ends with the recommendations report, continues with local hub resources, receives network resources (the paper names recruitment material development, sIRB support, contracting and study design labs) or proceeds to a comprehensive consultation in which the network may serve as a data or clinical coordinating center.
The same paper distinguishes two entry points. Applicants who want guidance and resources for an existing multicenter trial receive an initial consultation. Applicants planning a new multicenter trial are eligible for a comprehensive consultation with experts from a TIC and the RIC to guide proposal development before the project goes to funders. By April 2023 the network had received 401 proposals, 94% of them from principal investigators at 62 CTSA organisations.
Single IRB review
NIH’s 2016 expectation that federally funded multisite trials use a single IRB drove one of the network’s first deliverables. The TIN implemented the Streamlined, Multisite, Accelerated Resources for Trials (SMART) IRB master reliance agreement through consultations and demonstration projects, and created the IRB Reliance Exchange (IREx) as a web-based platform to document reliance and local considerations. For background on the review body itself, see the dictionary entry on the institutional review board (IRB).
Standard contracting agreements
Two master agreements were developed in the network’s first year. The Network FDP-CTSA Standard Agreement template is used for federally funded, fixed-price, domestic clinical trials. The Network Umbrella Confidential Disclosure Agreement allows study and protocol information to be shared across network sites. These are network-specific tools for the pre-award and start-up phase, a different thing from the sponsor-site clinical trial agreement that governs an individual study.
Expression of interest and site selection
When a study needs sites, the opportunity is communicated to the hubs’ liaison teams through a centrally managed Expression of Interest (EOI) process. Hubs review and respond using their own local processes, which is how the CTSA Program functions as a network while keeping local control. The 2017 perspective adds that the network aimed to use data-driven approaches to identify hubs and sites with suitable patient populations and to let potential sites comment on protocol feasibility and study budgets before funding decisions are made.
Recruitment and retention support
The RIC states its aims as providing a national home or “storefront” for recruitment education and best practices, catalysing enrollment through new technical and procedural approaches, supporting patient education and online study-finding tools, and running rigorous studies of recruitment methods (Wilkins et al., Journal of Clinical and Translational Science, 2021). Its authors cite the figure that nearly one in five studies either terminate for failed accrual or finish with underpowered numbers, which is the problem this support targets. By the time of that paper, the RIC had delivered customised recruitment support for 173 studies, using site feasibility assessments, community input sessions, recommendations on recruitment materials and social media campaigns.

TIN compared with a CTSA hub and with a trial network
The sources support three distinctions that search results often blur.
- TIN versus a single CTSA hub. A hub is one institution within the CTSA Program, with its own services and a Hub Liaison Team. The TIN is the cross-hub layer that connects more than 60 hubs to the TICs and the RIC. Many university pages describe local “TIN” liaison contacts: those are the hub’s entry point to the shared network, not a separate network.
- TIN versus a disease-specific trial network. The TIN is organised around operational services applicable across disease states (the 2023 review says the TICs work “across a broad range of disease states”). A disease-specific consortium is organised around a clinical question or population; see, for example, the guide to Type 1 Diabetes TrialNet, and the general explainer on hub-and-spoke clinical trial site networks.
- TIN versus the funder. NCATS funds the network’s centers; the sources describe what investigators receive as consultation, tools and operational support, and say that comprehensive consultations take place before proposals are submitted to funders. Background on the institute is in the guide to NIH NCATS.
Where it matters for research offices and investigators
For a research administration team, the network’s tools map onto familiar start-up bottlenecks. The 2017 perspective names IRB review and contracting as the main sources of start-up delay, and the 2023 review reports network-wide answers to both (the SMART IRB master reliance agreement and the FDP-CTSA template). A pre-award office can use the table above to see which network resource corresponds to which step, and which of them the investigator’s local hub liaison would normally arrange.
Three further points follow from the sources.
- The timing of the request matters. The comprehensive consultation is positioned before proposal submission, and the 2023 review reports that 65 initial consultations completed before application submission resulted in 37 funded grant applications.
- Budgeting is part of the service. The TICs list trial budgeting guidance among their consultative services; for negotiating the budget itself, see the guide to clinical trial budget negotiation.
- Applicants were mostly senior faculty. Of the 401 proposals counted by April 2023, 55.9% came from professors and 25.6% from associate professors, with the remainder from assistant professors, instructors and one postdoctoral fellow.
Common misunderstandings
It is not a single research center
The TIN is a collaboration of centers, hubs and partner sites. No one building is “the Trial Innovation Network”, and a site joins a given study through the expression of interest process.
It does not replace local review
The EOI process lets hubs respond through their own locally established processes, and the IREx platform exists to document local considerations alongside the single IRB reliance. The network streamlines review and contracting; it does not remove institutional responsibilities.
Its reported results are self-reported
The savings in contract negotiation time and the counts of supported studies come from the network’s own investigators. They are useful operational benchmarks, but the papers compare against historical averages and do not describe a randomised comparison.
The set of centers has changed over time
Early descriptions, which dominate older search results, give three TICs. The NCATS page as of 2026-11-21 lists two TICs and one RIC.
