TITLE: Businesses stay liable for what their AI agents tell shoppers, ACCC says
OUTLET: PPC Land

Australia's consumer regulator today urged shoppers to check what AI tools tell them against reliable sources, and to set firm limits before letting an AI agent buy anything on their behalf, as the Black Friday sales got under way. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission named three such products already on the Australian market - Meta's Muse, OpenAI's ChatGPT dots and Google Tasks - and reminded businesses that they are responsible for what their chatbots and agents say and do.
In Short
Australia's consumer watchdog says AI shopping assistants can give wrong answers, can be manipulated and may not spot a scam, so it wants people to double-check big purchases and to decide in advance how much an agent can spend and when it must ask first. Businesses that use chatbots, or that sell AI agents, are as responsible under consumer law as they would be if an employee gave the wrong information, and shoppers keep the same refund and repair rights when AI is involved. The ACCC points businesses to a six-part government guide on managing AI and says a report on AI agents and online shopping is coming in the next few months.
What the ACCC said
The warning came in media release 128/26, published today, on Black Friday itself, and filed under the topics "Buying and selling products and services" and "Digital platforms and services." Its title is a plain instruction: "Think and check before letting AI shop on your behalf."
The starting point, according to the ACCC, is that many Australians already use AI tools to compare products and services, and that "increasingly capable agentic products are entering the Australian market, including AI assistants and agents able to carry out tasks such as shopping on consumers' behalf." These products, the regulator notes, "can integrate with services such as consumers' email, cloud storage and banking and they can act for a consumer."
ACCC Chair Gina Cass-Gottlieb set out both sides. "Many consumers value the ability of AI to quickly compare different options and make recommendations, and newer AI products are able to make purchases," she said. "But while using AI can make life easier, AI results can be wrong. Consumers need to take care when using AI for research and when asking AI agents to act on their behalf."
Her advice on purchases was specific. "Before allowing an AI agent to buy products, consumers should check what personal information it can access, and think carefully about the permissions and instructions they give it so they can control how much it can spend," she said. "Consumers should check and set the points at which they want the agent to get their approval."
That last point describes what is sometimes called an approval threshold: a rule that makes the agent stop and ask before it spends above a set amount or takes a certain kind of action.
Three agents, named
The release goes beyond a general warning and names products. "There are many 'personal assistant' AI agents already available on the Australian market, such as Meta's Muse, OpenAI's ChatGPT dots and Google Tasks," according to the release, which adds that these agents "have increasingly powerful capabilities and require close and informed engagement from consumers."
Two of the three changed status in Australia on the same day, November 17.
Muse. Meta brought Muse to Australia on November 17 with nine local partners: Canva, Domain, Kogan, MECCA, Officeworks, realestate.com.au, SWEAT, ShopBack and Ticketek. Meta says Muse checks with the user before sensitive actions such as sending an email or making a purchase. When Muse first launched in September, it was a US-only agent that could complete purchases using a one-time card for each transaction.
Google Tasks. On the same day, Google made its Gemini task mode free in six countries, Australia among them, alongside the US, Japan, South Korea, Canada and India. Google described a hard spending cap that can be set upfront and a confirmation step before a purchase, though PPC Land noted at the time that this wording described how the feature is designed rather than a guarantee. The ACCC calls the product "Google Tasks." Google's own pages call it Tasks in Gemini, and the name is also used by a separate to-do list app Google has run since 2018, a clash PPC Land covered when the Gemini agent took on the name of the older app.
ChatGPT dots. OpenAI's always-on agents are the means by which ChatGPT buys things. When OpenAI introduced ChatGPT Wallet on November 13, purchases worked only through dots, and only in the United States, for ChatGPT Pro subscribers aged 18 or over with eligible US cards. Spending limits for delegated purchases were described as a later step, with no date. The ACCC release lists dots as available in Australia but does not say which of its features work there.
What can go wrong, according to the regulator
The release lists several distinct risks. The first concerns accuracy. "Consumers using AI tools to research products should remember an AI tool can sound confident and certain despite being wrong," Cass-Gottlieb said. A confident but false answer is the failure usually called hallucination. She went further: "An AI tools' answer to questions can be different at different times, and it can also be open to manipulation or reflect biases in its training data."
The practical conclusion the ACCC draws is to go to the source. "So, we are advising consumers to also check information directly - such as on a sellers' website - especially if they are considering a significant purchase," she said.
The second group of risks involves fakes. Shoppers should "watch for AI-cloned storefronts and deepfake brand or celebrity advertisements and exercise caution before completing purchases," according to the release. A deepfake is a fake image, video or voice made with AI to look like a real person or brand. The release adds that "product images and descriptions created using generative AI can make products appear of a higher quality than they are."
The third risk sits with the agents themselves. "AI agents may also not accurately identify something is a scam and may present a scam product or service alongside legitimate products or services," the ACCC says. In other words, an agent that searches the open web on a shopper's behalf can return a fraudulent listing with the same confidence as a genuine one.
Five checks for consumers
The release ends its consumer advice with five tips, introduced with the line "Before relying on AI powered tools to compare, choose, buy or get advice, consumers should remember." Each one carries a short explanation. The table below records them in short form.
The fourth tip goes furthest. According to the ACCC, consumers should "always verify financial transactions and other important actions before an AI agent takes these" on their behalf. Read literally, that asks for a human check on every payment, not only on large ones. It is stricter than the Chair's own wording, which speaks of setting the points at which an agent must ask for approval.
Businesses answer for their bots
For businesses, the message is that AI does not create a gap in responsibility. "Businesses are responsible for the actions their AI chatbots and agents take," according to the release.
Cass-Gottlieb spelled out what that means in law. "Consumers should know that even if they use AI in a transaction, their consumer guarantee rights under Australian Consumer Law do not change," she said. Consumer guarantees are the automatic rights Australian law attaches to products and services, such as a remedy when something bought turns out to be faulty. "Businesses are liable if they breach consumer law by providing misleading information, including about consumer guarantee rights, or engaging in manipulative practices - whether that is done by employees, in writing or through AI tools."
Three further obligations follow, according to the release. Businesses "must ensure their AI systems are fit for purpose, including testing them to make sure their outputs comply with consumer law." They should give consumers clear ways to get help, "including by access to review by a human representative if the AI tool can't resolve an issue." And companies that supply AI agents and other AI tools "must also ensure that they don't mislead customers about the agent's or tool's capabilities."
That last line reaches beyond retailers. It applies to the companies building the agents - by the ACCC's own examples, Meta, OpenAI and Google - and to any claim about what their products can do.

The government guide behind the advice
The guide the ACCC links to is "Guidance for AI adoption: foundations," published by the National Artificial Intelligence Centre in October 2025. It is an 11-page document aimed at "businesses and organisations in the early stages of adopting AI," with a longer companion version, "Implementation guidance," for step-by-step detail.
The foundations guide replaced an earlier framework. According to the document, it is "the first update to Australia's Voluntary AI Safety Standard (VAISS), launched in 2024." Three things changed in that update: the authors "condensed 10 guardrails into 6 essential practices," "removed redundant language" and "expanded our audience to developers as well as deployers." In plain terms, the guidance now addresses companies that build AI as well as those that buy and use it. It is guidance, not law.
The guide treats AI governance - the rules and responsibilities an organisation sets for its use of AI - as operating at two levels. Some practices apply to the whole organisation, such as having an AI policy. Others apply to each AI system in the context it is used. The document gives an example that marketers will recognise: "using ChatGPT to draft marketing emails is different from using it to assess job applications. Each use case needs its own governance checks."
It also insists on records. "Remember that documentation is key," the guide says, so that governance can be audited and reviewed. It states that it "does not replace" existing frameworks for data, privacy and cybersecurity, which should already be in place and updated for AI.
How the guidance works: the 13 starting actions
According to the National Artificial Intelligence Centre, organisations do not need to implement everything at once. The guide says to "get started across all six essential practices to establish basic responsible AI governance" and to add more as AI use grows. The 13 "Getting started" actions are:
- Name an owner (1.1). "Assign a senior leader as the overall AI governance owner," with enough authority and understanding to oversee all AI use in the organisation.
- Write a policy (1.2). An AI policy that sets out how the organisation will use AI responsibly and advises staff on AI risks. The guide offers an AI policy template.
- Assess who is affected (2.1). A stakeholder impact assessment identifying the groups an AI system may affect, with "particular attention to vulnerable or marginalised cohorts," covering harms such as unfair decisions, inaccurate or harmful information and overreliance.
- Open complaint channels (2.2). "Set up channels for people to report problems, challenge or question AI decisions," with the strength of the channel matched to how serious the impact is.
- Screen for risk (3.1). A process "to identify and flag AI systems and use cases that pose unacceptable risk or require additional governance attention," supported by an AI screening tool.
