TITLE: Amazon wants health ad endorsements backed by proof under 24 months old
OUTLET: PPC Land

Amazon Ads has set a 24-month limit on the age of the evidence behind health endorsements, comparative health claims and environmental claims in its advertising. The "Claims and substantiation" page in the Amazon Ads Support Center, updated on Sunday, November 15, 2026, requires medical or professional society endorsements and comparative claims on healthcare products to carry a source and a date within 24 months, unless the claim already appears on the product packaging. Environmental claims face the same window. Claims about how well a healthcare product works also need a source and a date, but, according to Amazon, with "no age requirement."
The page is short. It covers, in Amazon's words, "general claims, including books and healthcare," and is split into four parts: a one-line policy requirement, six prohibited claim types, a set of claims that are permitted only with substantiation, and country rules for 30 markets - the 27 member states of the European Union, Brazil, Singapore and the United Kingdom. It also contains an exemption that has no equivalent in the claims sections of the older policy documents Amazon still publishes: in the UK, an advertiser can replace a study and its date with a postal or email address.
In Short
Amazon's ad policy page on claims, updated November 15, 2026, says health endorsements and comparisons in ads need a source and date no older than 24 months, and so do green claims. Brands selling healthcare products, supplements, books, large appliances and organic or gluten-free foods on Amazon are the ones most affected. A claim already printed on the product box needs no extra proof, UK advertisers can give a contact address instead of a study, and weight-loss ads cannot use before-and-after images or promise permanent results.
One sentence of policy, six bans
The core obligation fits on one line. According to Amazon, "Advertisers are required to ensure the accuracy of claims and provide factual substantiation if requested by Amazon." Everything else on the page either prohibits a type of claim outright or sets the conditions under which a claim can run.
The prohibited list opens with "We prohibit the following claims in all ads." Half of the list concerns weight management and body image. Amazon's wording is as follows.
Two details in that list deserve a closer look. The before-and-after ban sits in the weight-management cluster, yet its wording begins with "Ads must avoid" rather than "Ads for weight management," so the sentence as written is not limited to diet products. And the ban on "regulatory endorsement claims on healthcare products" is not qualified. The page does not say whether an accurate statement that a regulator has cleared or approved a product counts as an endorsement claim.
The book rule is the only entry aimed at content rather than at a product's own performance. It covers any book that claims to cure a "high-risk disease or ailment," whatever the ad itself says. The page does not define which diseases are high risk.
What can run with proof
The "Restricted content" section works the other way round: it lists what is allowed, then the evidence attached to it. According to Amazon, "We permit environmental claims, origin claims, warranties or guarantees, and claims that describe health or quality ingredients on ingestible products such as gluten free or organic on Amazon retail supply. These claims must be substantiated on the ad creative, product detail page, or landing page, and environmental claims must be dated within 24 months."
That paragraph is limited to "Amazon retail supply," a term the page does not define. The next one is broader. "We also require substantiation for the following types of claims on healthcare products (excluding personal care products) across all Amazon ad products and all on supply," the page says. "This requirement also applies to over-the-counter (OTC) products when making certain claims."
Three claim types follow, each with an example Amazon supplies.
Amazon describes effectiveness claims as those that "describe the effectiveness of a product" and "are typically based on objective evidence or scientific data." Comparative claims are those "that compare a product or service to a competitor's product." Each of the three healthcare entries ends with the same carve-out: "If the claim is on the product packaging, no further substantiation is needed."
That exemption shifts the burden away from the ad and onto the box. Under the page's wording, a brand that prints "#1 physician recommended" on its packaging can repeat the line in an ad without a citation; a brand that only uses the phrase in advertising has to show where it comes from and when.
How the healthcare check works
According to the page, the substantiation test for a healthcare claim runs in five steps.
- Scope. The rule applies to healthcare products, "excluding personal care products," across all Amazon ad products and all supply. It also applies to over-the-counter products "when making certain claims," which the page does not list.
- Classification. The claim is sorted into one of three types: product effectiveness, product endorsement by a medical or professional society, or comparative claim.
- Packaging check. If the same claim appears on the product packaging, no further substantiation is required.
- Citation. Otherwise, the source and date must appear in the ad creative, on the product packaging or on the landing page. Endorsements and comparisons need a date within 24 months; effectiveness claims have no age limit.
- UK alternative. For health-related efficacy claims in the UK marketplace, an advertiser can instead give a PO box mailing address or a manufacturer email address, as set out in the country section below.
Amazon's general paragraph on restricted claims names the product detail page as a place where substantiation can sit. The healthcare paragraph does not; it lists the ad creative, the product packaging and the landing page. The page does not say whether a product detail page counts as a landing page for this purpose.
Country rules for 30 markets
The country section is the most operational part of the document. It covers energy labels in Europe, organic and diet claims in Brazil and Singapore, and a UK-only alternative to clinical citations.
Energy labels
On energy labels, the obligation is split between Amazon and the advertiser. For ads built from a product's ASIN, Amazon's catalogue identifier, the company says it "will ensure that ASIN-based ads have compliant labels." For the rest, the advertiser carries the responsibility, and Amazon says it will remove noncompliant ads reactively - after they run, not before. There is a cost to the automated route as well: "products may be blocked from some ads placements until auto-labeling is developed for that placement." The page does not name those placements or give a timetable. It links to the European Union's guidance on energy labels and to the UK government's guidance on the energy labelling of products.
The European list names exactly the 27 EU member states. Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein, the three non-EU members of the European Economic Area where EU product rules generally apply, are absent.
The UK email option
The UK entry is the most unusual clause on the page. "For substantiation of health-related efficacy claims made in the UK marketplace, advertisers have the option of either conforming to the worldwide policy outlined above, or, instead of providing a study and date, they can provide a PO box mailing address or manufacturer email address for consumers to have a means of contacting the advertiser," according to Amazon. The example given reads: "For verification, please email manufacturerXYZ@gmail.co.uk."
In practice, a UK advertiser making an efficacy claim can point consumers to a contact route instead of a citation. The page does not say what the contact address must deliver, how quickly, or whether Amazon checks it.
What differs from Amazon's older policy text
The Support Center page carries no change log, so it does not say what it replaced. Amazon still publishes two older, undated documents that cover the same ground: the Sponsored Ads Guidelines and Acceptance Policies, whose section 3.7 is titled "Claims and substantiations," and the Creative acceptance policy for display ads, whose section 6.4 covers product display ads, Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands. Both were live on November 16, 2026. Read side by side, the new page differs from them in several places.
Some of these differences loosen the rules. A 24-month window is twice the 12 months the creative acceptance policy sets for comparative claims in Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Products and product display ads, and the packaging exemption removes a citation requirement for anything already printed on the box. Others tighten them: the regulatory endorsement ban carries no equivalent of the older "improperly" qualifier, and weight-loss ads may not guarantee a rate of loss. The older documents also leave the age of most evidence open; the creative acceptance policy's own sample citation is a Consumer Reports issue from July 2017. The new page replaces that open standard with fixed windows for three claim types.
Whether the older documents still bind advertisers where they conflict with the new page is not addressed. The creative acceptance policy remains online with its 12-month rule and its ban on naming competitor brands.
Five pages, one date
The claims page is one of at least five Amazon Ads policy pages in the Support Center stamped "Updated on Nov 15, 2026." The others are Animal related products and services, Real estate, News and journalism, and Pricing and Promotions. The claims page lists 16 related policy topics in its sidebar, from Alcohol to Event days. Three days earlier, Amazon revised its Gambling and Games page, putting prediction markets through the same verification as online casinos and sports betting.
The Support Center format gives each category its own page and its own update stamp. Rules that sat inside long guideline documents are now split by category, which also means the rules for a single campaign can sit across several pages updated on different days.
Why the rules matter to advertisers
Amazon's ad policies sit on top of rules that already apply to the listings themselves, and the seller side has been tightening throughout 2026. On January 14, Amazon told sellers that supplement listings overstating ingredient weights - 10,000mg of raw herb against 500mg of actual extract, for example - faced deactivation from March 31, 2026, and that listing images had to show the Supplement Facts Panel. Since July 24, 2026, sellers in categories that require third-party testing have filed test request forms in bulk, with dietary supplements among the categories named.
The packaging exemption ties the advertising rule to that listing machinery. A claim that has passed through labelling and listing checks can be repeated in an ad without a separate citation. A claim that exists only in the ad must carry its own source.
Regulators have acted on several of the claim types the page covers. The FTC sent warning letters to Amazon, Walmart and four manufacturers on July 8, 2025 over false Made in USA claims, citing the standard that all or virtually all of a product's content must be domestic. A class action filed on November 3, 2025 targets Black Rifle Coffee's American branding on coffee sourced and processed abroad. In Australia, the ACCC published guidance on October 20, 2026 saying ads cannot present bought carbon credits as emission cuts, and Hismile paid A$138,600 across seven infringement notices in June 2026 over staff posing as shoppers and misleading claims about stains. Weight-loss claims carry a longer history: the FTC began paying more than $409,000 to 7,481 buyers of Roca Labs products in July 2025, under a 2018 judgment.
