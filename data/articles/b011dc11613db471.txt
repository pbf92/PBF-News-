TITLE: Ad buyers face longer supply chains under IAB Tech Lab's schain 1.1
OUTLET: PPC Land

IAB Tech Lab on November 18, 2026 published OpenRTB v2.6-202611, a dated release of the real-time bidding protocol that changes what the SupplyChain object is for. Version 1.1 of the object, which travels inside programmatic ad requests, now covers companies that hold or route a request without handling payment for the inventory, and a new value of the "complete" flag tells buyers whether those companies are all accounted for.
In Short
IAB Tech Lab updated the rulebook that ad auctions use so the record of who handled an ad request can include companies that never pay or get paid for the ad space. That matters to anyone who buys ads by looking at how many companies sit between them and the website, because the record will get longer once companies start using the new rule. The rules for checking those extra companies against the industry's lists of authorised sellers have not been written yet and are due early in 2027.
The record changes its purpose
The SupplyChain object has existed for seven years as a list of companies that touch the money. Each entry, or node, names an advertising system and the seller account it pays. A buyer reading the list could check each node against the publisher's ads.txt file and each exchange's sellers.json file, and so confirm that the inventory was being sold by companies the publisher had authorised.
Version 1.1 widens that list. "Supply Chain 1.1 is now live and discloses any entity that has custody of the bid request," Hillary Slattery, Sr. Director, Programmatic, Product Management at IAB Tech Lab, wrote in a post titled "Updating the Supply Chain Object. Finally." The post carries no publication date on the page; it links to the November 18 release. "Specifically entities that have the ability to initiate or route bid requests to another entity," she added.
The post states the test plainly. "The litmus test here is if you have the ability to overwrite something that was already there (whether or not you actually do it), you will become a node." Capacity, not behaviour, is what counts. A company that could alter a bid request but leaves it untouched still qualifies.
The previous rule was narrower. "Until today, only companies that either received or cut a check were required to list themselves. That changes with this update," according to the post. The specification text tells the same story. In the June 2026 release, the object list described SupplyChain as a "Chain of nodes from beginning to end representing the entities involved in the direct flow of payment for inventory." In the November release, the same line reads: "Chain of nodes from beginning to end representing the entities that took custody of a bid request."
What changed in the specification
The release notes summarise the change in two lines: "Updated Object: SupplyChain and Object: SupplyChainNode to schain v1.1" and "New complete values: 1 = payment complete, 2 = payment and non-payment complete". The full definitions sit in sections 3.2.25 and 3.2.26 of the OpenRTB 2.6 specification on GitHub. Two details in that table deserve attention. The first is the value 1. Under version 1.0, "complete": 1 simply meant the chain was complete, because only payment nodes existed. Under 1.1, the same value means something narrower: every payment node is present, but "some or all hp=0 nodes may be missing." A chain marked 1 is therefore no promise that every company with custody of the request has been listed. Only a 2 makes that claim.
The second is the hp flag. In version 1.0 it was a field with only one legal value. The specification said it "should always be 1", and the separate implementation document for the object, still published in the OpenRTB repository, explains that the field was required anyway because future versions were expected to introduce non-payment handling nodes. Version 1.1 is that future version. A node with hp set to 0, in the specification's words, "is not involved in the flow of payment for the inventory."
The node fields themselves did not move. The asi field still holds "The canonical domain name of the SSP, Exchange, Header Wrapper, etc system that bidders connect to," and sid still holds the seller or reseller account identifier, which "Should be limited to 64 characters in length." The name field is still defined as "The name of the company (the legal entity) that is paid for inventory transacted under the given seller_ID", payment language that sits unchanged next to the new non-payment nodes.
How the object is built under version 1.1
According to the specification, a SupplyChain object in an OpenRTB 2.6-202611 bid request is assembled as follows.
- The object sits at source.schain in the bid request, recorded in JSONPath as $.source.schain. Each node sits at $.source.schain.nodes[*].
- The ver field carries the string "1.1".
- Nodes are "appended in supply-chain order from seller to buyer." Each node identifies the advertising system (asi) and the seller account within it (sid), and may carry the request ID issued by that seller (rid), plus name and domain where these are not already in the system's sellers.json file.
- Each node sets hp. A value of 1 means "the advertising system in the asi field pays the seller in the sid field, who is responsible for paying the previous node in the chain." A value of 0 means the node is not in the payment flow.
- The complete flag records how much of the chain is present: 0 when the chain is not complete, 1 when it was created by the initial advertising system and holds all hp=1 nodes, and 2 when it holds all hp=1 and all hp=0 nodes.
The post adds the qualifying rule for step 3: an entity that can initiate or route a request, or that can overwrite something already in it, becomes a node. The specification text does not list which kinds of company that covers. Its asi definition mentions header wrappers alongside SSPs and exchanges, which hints at the range of systems involved.
Seven years in the making
None of this arrived suddenly. The object "was added to OpenRTB 2.5 in 2019 as an extension and codified official support in the full OpenRTB 2.6 specification in 2022," according to the post. Adoption among sellers followed quickly. Index Exchange adopted sellers.json and the SupplyChain object in August 2019, passing a schain object to DSPs in every bid request, and Centro said in August 2020 that it would require both standards from exchanges and ad networks, blocking intermediaries that did not comply. Anyone still using the old extension field gets a pointed note in the post: "if you're still sending the ext...it's time to upgrade my friend."
The idea of non-payment nodes was in the original design. "It was even called out when the spec was originally published that at some point non-payment handling entities would also be listed," the post says. "It took 7 years to come to pass, but today is the day."
The current effort began, by the post's count, "9 months and roughly eleventy billion meetings ago" and drew "about 50 participants from across the Programmatic Supply Chain community". The post names publishers, DSPs and verification vendors among them. IAB Tech Lab put SupplyChain v1.1 forward as a proposal on June 23, 2026, with public comment open until August 21, 2026, according to its announcement at the time. The response was heavy. "If the number of responses on the original Public Comment issue doesn't tell you how much this will impact the ecosystem, I'm not sure what will," Slattery wrote.
Version 1.1 shipped as part of a dated 2.6 release rather than as OpenRTB 2.7. Under the versioning policy in the repository's README, "As of OpenRTB 2.6-202211, OpenRTB's version number is only incremented on breaking changes," and the specification "is updated approximately once a month if there are non-breaking improvements". The previous tagged release was 2.6-202606.
Hop counting meets a longer chain
Why should a buyer care about entries that move no money? Because the object is used for more than fraud checks. The post is explicit about the gap between the intended use and the actual one. The purpose of ads.txt and sellers.json, it says, "is to fight misrepresentation of inventory in the bidstream." Then: "We acknowledge that in practice the Supply Chain Object is also used for Supply Path Optimization."
Supply path optimization is, in simple terms, the practice of choosing the cheapest or most direct supply path to the same impression. A common shortcut is to count the nodes. The post is sceptical of that shortcut. "What clients deem to be 'the most efficient' is up to them, but we know it will always be more nuanced than simple hop counting." And it spells out the consequence: "When fully rolled out, this update will result in more nodes in the supply chain. Buyers that are relying on blunt strategies will need to revisit that way of thinking to incorporate the additional transparency."
Hop counts already shape guidance and trading rules. IAB Tech Lab's own Programmatic Auction Definitions, finalised in June, describe paths with more than two or three hops as rare and generally suboptimal. PubMatic's supply policy effective October 7, 2026 allows two inbound schain hops for web and app and one for CTV, excluding the Google Open Bidding node from the count, and blocks resellers that send no schain at all. When The Trade Desk launched OpenAds in October 2025, it listed secret hops and multiple hops among the practices it was targeting. All of these rules were drafted when every node in a valid chain marked a payment step.
For a demand-side platform, the post anticipates a code change rather than a policy debate. "DSPs especially, may need to think in terms of IF statements if they're trying to preserve existing payment entity validation logic." Read against the specification, that means separating hp=1 nodes, which can be checked against sellers.json entries for paid sellers, from hp=0 nodes, for which no equivalent check yet exists.
The money involved is not trivial. IAB Spain's first SSP guide, published in April, put advertiser delivery at 70% to 80% through a direct SSP connection, against 40% to 50% for indirect multi-reseller paths. DataBeat found in September that resellers added 44,608 ads.txt lines across the top 50,000 US publishers, against 10,334 direct lines. Buyers have been acting on what the bidstream tells them: the Coalition for Innovative Media Measurement reported on November 12 that 62% of programmatic CTV buyers move spend to another path when the signals they need are missing.
Validation is the unfinished half
The OpenRTB change is only one of two parts. "This is just the first of at least two planned releases relating to the inclusion of non-payment handling entities in the supply chain," the post says. "We plan to release updates to sellers.json and (app)/ads.txt early next year. That release will include a metric ton of scenarios and additional rules around when and how to list yourself."
The order was deliberate. "The Commit Group made the decision to push just the OpenRTB update now so that implementers have plenty of time to talk about how and when to make these changes," according to the post.
Validation is where the hard questions sit. "A core piece of feedback from Public Comment was that the specification really needs to nail how to validate these new nodes," Slattery wrote. "There are multiple ways to go about said validation, and the group is working through the available options." The post says a Public Comment period on the sellers.json and ads.txt changes should be expected early next year, and points readers to IAB Tech Lab's GitHub, where "(app)/ads.txt and sellers.json now live."
