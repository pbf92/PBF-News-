TITLE: Claude 4 absorbed Claude 3's hallucinations, 15-model ancestry map records
OUTLET: PPC Land

Six researchers from the University of Washington, Carnegie Mellon University and Georgetown University have traced the ancestry of 15 foundation models, from OpenAI's GPT-4 and Anthropic's Claude 4 to AI2's OLMo 3 and Nvidia's Nemotron 3, and recorded 1,161 unique artifacts and 2,778 unique dependencies, some of them nine steps removed from the model at the end of the chain. Their preprint, "Model Maps: A Documentation Framework for Tracing Foundation Model Ancestry and Understanding AI Entanglement," is dated November 13, 2026, and was circulated on LinkedIn on November 18 by privacy and digital law specialist Rosalia Anna D'Agostino under the line "AI models are like layered cakes."
In Short
Researchers drew family trees for 15 well-known AI models and found that each one is built from hundreds of other models, datasets, benchmarks and websites, often from rival companies. That matters because advertising and marketing tools run on these models, and properties such as errors, bias or licence limits can travel down the chain without the company at the end seeing them. Nothing changes in law yet: the paper is a preprint arguing that transparency rules should require developers to name each upstream source, not just say that one exists.
What the paper sets out to do
The authors build on a term from earlier work by Hong and colleagues. "AI entanglement," according to the paper, describes how "properties of artifacts upstream can influence the outputs of a foundation model downstream." Their proposal for documenting it is called a model map: "a detailed knowledge graph that represents the relationships between artifacts that influence the resulting foundation model."
The problem they describe is practical. A black-box model, in their account, can pick up security flaws, hallucinations or biased predictions from somewhere upstream, and "developers are left with no recourse to identify or mitigate the root issue," the abstract states. Regulators, auditors and litigants face a parallel difficulty, according to the authors, in "determining the entities responsible for the harms enacted by a model."
Model maps build on three earlier documentation formats the paper cites by name: ecosystem graphs, datasheets and model cards. Where an ecosystem graph tries to chart the whole industry, a model map is drawn around a single model family. In graph terms, the paper notes, an ecosystem graph can end in many applications, while "a model map for M will only have sink nodes corresponding to M." The authors compare the idea to a software bill of materials, which lets dependencies be tracked "all the way up the chain in a modular, crowd-sourced manner without relying on a centralized repository."
The six authors are Rachel Hong, Madeleine Grunde-McLaughlin and Jevan Hutson of the University of Washington; Sarah Cen of Carnegie Mellon University; Jamie Morgenstern, listed with both the University of Washington and Amazon; and Tadayoshi Kohno of Georgetown University. The paper carries the arXiv identifier 2611.18998v1 in the Computers and Society category and runs to 47 pages.
Thirteen ways one artifact shapes another
At the core of the framework sits a taxonomy of 13 dependency mechanisms, grouped into four types. The authors say they started from categories set out in earlier work by Hong and colleagues and extended them to data-based dependencies. They deliberately left out relationships that "lack substantial evidence of impacting M's outputs," such as baseline comparisons, generated training code and reuse of a model's architecture.
Several of the mechanisms describe models used as tools in building other models. One model may filter another's training data, score its answers as a judge, or watch its outputs in production. The paper is blunt about the last of these. Output guardrails "may provide some sort of promise of safety," but earlier research "has revealed shortcomings in current personal information redaction, ineffective and biased toxicity classification, and insufficient copyright detection." A similar criticism applies to guardrails used on training data, which the paper says can "enable and obfuscate the incorporation of undesired data into the resulting dataset."

Each mechanism carries its own risk, according to the authors. Synthetic data generation has been linked to "model collapse" when models are successively trained on their own outputs. Automated labelling can create "feedback loops that shifts the distribution away from the real data and can exacerbate stereotypes." Distilled models "can inherit similar behaviors or generated preferences as the upstream model, through a process known as subliminal learning."
How a model map is built
According to Appendix A of the paper, building a model map for a model collection M follows five steps, which can be carried out by an outside party or by the developer itself.
- Workflow construction. The development pipeline for M is defined, with stages such as training data curation, model training and deployment, and evaluation benchmark selection, plus specific training steps such as pre-training, fine-tuning or reinforcement learning from human feedback.
- Artifact extraction. A node is created for every model, dataset, benchmark and API in each stage, recording its type, any documentation or usage licence, and any disclosed timestamps or modifications. Artifacts created by M's developers are "root" artifacts; pre-existing ones are "external."
- Relationship determination. Relationships between nodes are found, evidence for each is tracked, and the 13-category taxonomy is applied. Codebases, architecture, ablations, adversarial filtering and baseline comparisons are ignored.
- Artifact retrieval. For each external artifact, its research publication or source location (for example on Hugging Face) is located and cited, or recorded as missing. Steps 2 and 3 are then repeated for that artifact, omitting its own evaluation relationships, until a pre-defined stopping point is reached, "such as reaching X hops from M, or once a website, API, or another foundation model artifact is reached."
- Validation. Every relationship is checked again for evidence.
For the study itself, the authors stopped a path when it reached an unknown artifact, a website, an API or another foundation model. Websites, according to the paper, "possess challenging provenance gaps," while AI-based APIs "are often not transparent as to their individual components." Stopping at another foundation model has a second purpose: it lets one model's map plug into another's.
The work was done by hand. The first author followed the procedure using technical reports and the arXiv papers they cite as sources, and the maps were recorded in Obsidian, a linked note-taking application, with each note an artifact and each link a dependency carrying the quotation that proves it. A second author re-annotated a stratified random sample of 285 dependencies and reached what the paper calls "almost perfect agreement" (Fleiss kappa 0.884). The OLMo 3 map alone, according to the authors, "took several weeks to document and another several weeks to retrace and validate."
Fifteen models, measured
The sample covers language model families with public technical reports published between March 2023 (Alpaca) and December 2025 (Nemotron 3), chosen from 13 organisations or affiliated universities across "a wide range of reported transparency levels." Four are closed-weight: GPT-4, Apple's foundation model, Claude 4 and Gemini 2.5.

The last two columns are read from Figures 6 and 4: the share of all dependencies whose upstream artifact is unnamed, and the share of model-based dependencies whose source is a closed-weight model.
Many maps share artifacts, which is why the per-model counts add up to more than the unique totals. By PPC Land's calculation, the 15 maps contain 2,278 nodes and 4,017 edges before duplicates are removed, against the 1,161 unique artifacts and 2,778 unique dependencies the paper reports.
Open models built partly on closed ones
The most pointed finding concerns openness. "Across all model maps except for Mistral 7B, a proportion of model-based dependencies are based on closed models, hence demonstrating that even developers for open-weight models still rely on proprietary models," the paper states. Among the 11 open-weight models, the closed share ranges from 0.31 for Llama 3 to 0.69 for Microsoft's Phi 4.
OLMo 3 gets particular attention. AI2 describes it as "fully open," yet 0.41 of its model-based dependencies come from closed-weight models, according to the paper's Figure 4, and some of AI2's own intermediate models "are directly dependent on closed models as well." The four closed-weight models show shares from 0.47 for GPT-4 to 0.89 for Claude 4.
Size tells its own story. OLMo 3's map has 681 nodes, while the closed models' maps run from 38 to 115, and the authors attribute the gap to disclosure: closed models are "significantly smaller in size due to lack of disclosed dependencies." For the smaller maps, benchmark evaluation makes up "a disproportionate amount of dependencies," which the authors read as "supporting the observation that proprietary model organizations do not disclose details of training data curation."
Lineage that crosses company lines
OpenAI appears "predominantly across many dependencies," according to the paper, which counted the ten organisations most often found as sources. Developers lean on their own earlier work, and the paper names OpenAI for GPT-4, Google for Gemma 3 and Gemini 2.5, Anthropic for Claude 4, Apple for its foundation model, Alibaba for Qwen3 and Meta for Llama 3. Read from Figure 5, own-organisation artifacts account for 0.46 of edges in GPT-4's map, 0.38 in Claude 4's, 0.31 in Gemini 2.5's, 0.32 in Gemma 3's, 0.25 in Llama 3's, 0.24 in Qwen3's and 0.19 in Apple's. "However, various foundation models implicitly depend on artifacts from competitors," the authors write.
In the training data of the three largest maps (Nemotron 3, SmolLM3 and OLMo 3), the most common upstream models include GPT-4, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, DeepSeek-R1, GPT-3.5-turbo, two Llama instruction models, Qwen 2.5 32B Instruct and GPT-4.1. The ten most common upstream websites in those maps are Wikipedia, Common Crawl, GitHub, Codeforces, AoPS, Reddit, StackExchange, arXiv, CodeChef and AtCoder.
Ancestors nobody named
Every map except Alpaca's contains dependencies whose source is not named, "even for foundation model reports that claim to be completely open-source." The paper says closed-weight models have "relatively higher proportions" of such dependencies, at 0.12 for GPT-4, Apple's model and Claude 4. The single highest share in Figure 6, however, belongs to an open-weight model: 0.20 for Google's Gemma 3. Gemini 2.5, closed, sits at 0.06. For OLMo 3, the authors also recorded six nodes that are cited but whose documentation "could not be found or no longer exists," among them the retired Freebase website.
Loops
Some models consume their own output. The paper finds "concrete evidence that some foundation models are iteratively trained on their own generations," naming Llama 3.1 405B Instruct, DR Tulu SFT and Apple's FM-server. Others grade themselves. "Gemini 2.5, for instance, is evaluated on ZeroBench and an internal automated red-teaming benchmark, both of which use the same model as an LLM judge," the authors write, which "raises concerns of self-preference bias."
