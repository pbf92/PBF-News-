TITLE: Study confirms link between autism and GI symptoms in children
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Researchers at UTHealth Houston have confirmed that children with autism spectrum disorder who experience greater challenges with social awareness and communication may also be more likely to have severe gastrointestinal symptoms.
The findings were published in Nature and led by Marc Rhoads, MD, professor and chief of the Division of Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition in the Department of Pediatrics at McGovern Medical School at UTHealth Houston.
Gastrointestinal symptoms are common among children with autism, which currently affects 3% of the pediatric population in the U.S., and can affect behavior, sleep, and overall quality of life. In the study, nearly two-thirds of children with autism spectrum disorder experienced significant gastrointestinal symptoms, and those with more severe autism-related symptoms were more likely to experience digestive issues.
To better understand the connection between autism, digestive health, and the gut microbiome, researchers collected blood and stool samples from children ages 4 to 16 with a confirmed diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder. The team assessed autism-related behaviors and gastrointestinal symptoms through clinical evaluations and parent questionnaires, measured markers of inflammation and gut barrier function, and analyzed the children’s gut microbiota. Findings were compared with data from typically developing children and siblings of children with autism.
The study found that children with more severe autism-related symptoms were more likely to experience gastrointestinal symptoms, including abdominal pain, gas, foul-smelling stools, and nighttime awakening.
“Families may notice increased irritability, repetitive behaviors, or self-injurious behaviors when a child is experiencing gastrointestinal issues such as gas, constipation, or diarrhea,” Rhoads said. “For children who are nonverbal, these behaviors may be one of the few ways they can communicate discomfort.”
Among children with autism spectrum disorder, researchers found a significant association between gastrointestinal symptom severity and autism symptom severity. Children with autism also were found to have a lower microbial diversity and distinct differences in gut bacterial composition in their stool samples compared with typically developing children and siblings of children with autism.
The research identified increased levels of several bacterial groups in children with autism spectrum disorder and gastrointestinal symptoms that are associated with overall gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain, gas, foul-smelling stools, and nighttime awakening.
According to Rhoads, longstanding theories suggest gastrointestinal symptoms in autism may be related to inflammation or increased intestinal permeability, often called “leaky gut,” however, the study found no evidence to support those explanations. “Biomarkers of intestinal inflammation and gut permeability remained within normal ranges and were not associated with gastrointestinal symptom severity or autism symptom severity,” he said.
“This study highlights the importance of supporting a healthy gut microbiome in children with autism,” Rhoads said. “A varied diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, along with healthy toileting habits, may help improve both gastrointestinal health and behavior. Our team has also found promising results with probiotic treatment.”
Researchers hope the findings will advance understanding of the gut-brain connection and help inform future strategies to improve the health and well-being of children with autism.
