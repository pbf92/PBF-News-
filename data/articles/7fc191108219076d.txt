TITLE: GTA 6 Gave Me Guns, Cars, and Cash. All I Wanted Was to Take Pictures
OUTLET: PCMag

It’s safe to say that I was looking forward to the release of Grand Theft Auto VI. I’ve played most of the entries in the series starting with GTA III back in my college days (I somehow missed San Andreas), and I count Rockstar’s previous release, Red Dead Redemption 2, as an all-time favorite; it’s a game that I’ve played through multiple times. I wasn’t prepared for how much of a visual, visceral feast GTA 6 would be, however. I'm still hanging out in the Keys, but even so, the world gives me huge Michael Mann Miami Vice feels.
When I loaded it up on my Xbox last Thursday evening, my jaw dropped at the depth of the world, and I just knew I couldn’t wait to try out Photo Mode and start taking in-game photos. I know that’s not the typical first instinct. I think most gamers would want to check out the different firearms and vehicles, or run a few side missions to start building up character stats and bankroll. Not me; I’m a gamer, but I’m also a camera nerd, which puts me smack in the middle of the Venn diagram for video game photo modes.
And now that I’ve got a solid weekend of playtime in, I’m here to share some of my thoughts, tips, and show off some of my favorite snaps. Just note, there are some minor spoilers for Chapter 1 ahead (I’m taking it slow and haven’t progressed further), but I’ve tried to restrict them to random encounters and side missions.
How to Take Pictures in GTA 6
There are a few ways to snap images in GTA 6. You can use your in-game smartphone’s camera to take shots either with its rear camera or selfie camera; you can take screenshots on your console; or you can hit the pause button and select Photo Studio from the menu. All three methods are useful, though they give you different levels of creative control.
Take a Screenshot With Your Controller
I’ll start with the most basic: the screenshot. On the Xbox, you can press the Share button- that’s the one with the up arrow icon that’s just below the glowing X power button; for the PS5, press the Create button, the small buttons with three lines next to the D-pad. The screenshot does just what you expect it to: It saves a still image of what you’re seeing on your TV, subtitles, menus, and all, and is a good one to use for quick, in-the-moment grabs. GTA 6’s gameplay is already cinematic, and it’s a quick way to get a quick, in-the-moment grab for posterity.
Use Your In-Game Smartphone
Most of us use our smartphone cameras for IRL photos, and it’s a good tool for capturing action in GTA 6 too. To get started, bring up your smartphone (with the d-pad’s up direction) and select the camera app.
The phone camera app is straightforward enough; it shoots pictures in a square format to match those you see in the game’s in-universe Instagram clone, Snapmatic, and lets you switch between the front and rear cameras. The front (selfie) camera lets you make some poses, including hang ten and thumbs-up hand gestures, and, if you’re posing with a friend, adjust their pose too. You can also change your expression or toggle a fill light. The rear camera also supports a fill light, plus 5x digital zoom, composition grid lines, and 17 filter effects (including the unfiltered default look).
There are some limitations to consider when you’re using the phone camera. First off, your phone can only hold 32 photos. I guess Jason didn’t spring for extra iFruit cloud storage. Secondly, the only way to look at a photo after you’ve snapped it is in-game, on your phone’s screen. This is great for immersion, but if you want to share pics on real-life social media, you may want to take a screenshot; you don’t get as good a view of your snap if you’re viewing it on the in-game phone’s screen. And, sadly, you can’t become an influencer in GTA 6—Snapmatic is for viewing only.
Still, it’s worth it to browse for a chuckle, and some photos let you go to the location where they were taken (choose “Set Destination”), handy if you want to check out a landmark or if you see an opportunity to do some crimes.
One thing to understand is that using your in-game phone to take pictures can prompt reactions from various NPCs. That can be a lot of fun, or it can lead to violence and chaos. I was scolded by Jimmy, an old drunken sailor that I met outside a bar, when I used my phone to take a picture of him holding his spear gun. Thankfully, he didn’t fire off a spear in my direction; we had a good chat, I watched him urinate on a lamppost, and he drove off on his boat. That’s just how life goes in Leonida.
Get Creative in Photo Studio
Photo Studio is where you’ll go if you want to take creative control over a screenshot. It’s pretty similar to the photo mode in Rockstar’s previous effort, Red Dead Redemption 2, and other video games I’ve played, notably Cyberpunk 2077. Photo Studio includes a lengthy set of options, broken up into five tabs: Camera, Effects, Lighting, Vehicle, and Overlays.
Camera
Camera is where you’ll go to set up your shot. You’ll use the left and right sticks to change the camera position, the triggers to zoom in and out, and the context menu to change the movement speed, toggle a framing assist grid, roll the camera to get a Dutch angle, adjust the aspect ratio and angle of view, and change the aperture and focus point to control depth of field. There’s also a toggle switch that lets you choose whether or not your character appears.
I leaned heavily into the widescreen CinemaScope 2:35:1 ratio for my photos. It just felt right to me, but the game gives you the freedom to make your own choice. You can also use the full 16:9 screen, or opt for more moderate ratios like 1:85:1 and 2:1, also common choices for feature films. And if you plan on sharing your favorite shots on social media, there are a healthy number of square and tall ratios available, too.
I was a little surprised that the camera is rather limited in where and how far it can go from the starting point. You can’t just zoom in to a distant scene and take a picture. You need to get decently close to the action for a good photo. It’s not like Cyberpunk’s camera, which lets you move far away from the starting point, or zoom in for a close-up with few restrictions. If you want to get a photo of something far away in GTA 6, you’ll want to equip your binoculars and start Photo Studio (or take a screenshot) when peering through them.
I’ll get more into the restricted camera movement in the next section; on a technical level, it’s just something you’ll have to get used to. I’ll lend this piece of guidance: If you’re ever in a situation where you want to rotate the camera to an angle and find it won’t get there, try backing out of the menu, moving your character (or vehicle) a little bit, and try it again. Some in-game objects will block camera moves, and it’s just a matter of working around them. I’ve found a few steps in a different direction can make or break getting the shot you envision.
I do want to talk about depth of field and focus, though. Photo Studio defaults to a large aperture number; in camera terms, that means that most of the world is in focus. You can set it to a lower figure, which limits how much of a scene is in focus and draws the background with a soft effect (in the photo world, we call the background blur “bokeh").
You’ll want to use Aperture control in conjunction with the Focus Distance slider; change it to set the distance at which the lens is focusing. It’s also worthwhile to use the Focus Guide overlay; it’s a focus assist that separates your scene into dark (in focus) and light (out of focus) sections, with visible lines showing the limits of each. You can see it in action above. I’ve got it set so the rear and rear doors of the car are in focus, with the rest of the scene starting to blur. Now, if I wanted more of the car in focus, I could either raise the aperture number to something like f/8, move the focus distance further away from the camera, or a combination of both. If you’re not a seasoned photographer, it’s worth playing around with the different combinations to see how it works
Effects
The Effects panel includes a set of panels to adjust Exposure (the overall brightness of a photo), Contrast, Highlights, Shadows, Saturation, and Sharpness—the same options you’ll see in your real smartphone’s photo editing app. It also lets you add a film Grain effect, change the Temperature (making an image lean toward warmer or cooler tones), adjust the Tint (adding a green or purple look), adjust the intensity of Lens Flare, and add a light Bloom effect and Chromatic Aberration.
Most of these are straightforward, but a couple require explaining. Lens Flare is common enough, but understand that dialing in the slider only changes the look of an image if there’s already some flare; that is, if the sun is hitting the virtual camera lens at the right angle. Bloom is only really useful for nighttime scenes; ramping it up adds a halo effect around light sources and is great for neon-lit streets. Chromatic Aberration adds a red and purple false color effect around the edges of objects. It can look a little fake if you overdo it, but I ended up liking it for one nighttime aerial I shot from a helicopter. It really brings the neon lights of a hotel pool area near your house to life. (And, if you haven’t checked out the pool bar, make it a point to do so—it’s a wild time.)
Lighting
The Lighting screen is easily my favorite part of Photo Studio. It lets you create up to five virtual lights and move them around to highlight different parts of a scene. Each lighting slot has its own set of controls and can be positioned independently. If you’re ever in Photo Studio and wish that something in the scene was easier to see, or if something is too dark, Lighting is where you’ll go to fix it.
There are six types of lights to choose from: Stage Light, Reflector, Camera Flash, Ring Light, Softbox, and Bulb. Each has a configurable intensity (brightness) and a way to adjust the size and shape of its beam (Beam Angle). You can also change the color of any light and adjust its saturation. It comes in handy if you want to add some color to a dull scene.
Lights can be freely moved and rotated in space in any direction. They won’t bump into objects like the camera, though objects will block the light itself and cast shadows appropriately.
It’s a lot to digest, especially if you’re not an expert on lighting. For non-photographers, I’d recommend starting with the Stage Light and Softbox effects. Stage Light has loads of power and will create scenes with harsh, defined shadows, while Softbox isn’t as strong but spreads the light out over a larger area and draws less-defined shadows. Once you get comfortable with those, try out Camera Flash or Ringlight. Both give you direct light like the Stage Light but without as much overwhelming power. Bulb is fun for interiors. As you can gather from the name, it acts like a light bulb, and it has a few different shapes from which to choose. All of the virtual lights do a good job mimicking their real-world counterparts. They’re definitely worth using if you want to glam up your in-game pics.
Vehicle
The Vehicle tab lets you set how your car, plane, boat, or other method of transportation appears in the scene. You can turn headlights, turn signals, brake lights, and fog lights. And if you don't want your vehicle in the shot at all, you can toggle it off and make it invisible.
Overlays
The final tab is Overlays. This is where you’ll be able to toggle Gameplay Effects (if you want to take a picture when your character is under the influence or taking heavy damage, turn this off to get a clean view of the scene), apply a Filter effect, add a Vignette (darkened corners), or use Color Isolation for a selective color effect (only reds, only blues, or only greens).
