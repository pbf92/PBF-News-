Resistance Exercise Training Shows Rapid Antidepressant Effects: JAMA
A new study published in the Journal of American Medical Association showed that resistance exercise training (RET) was linked to fast and notable antidepressant effects, especially in participants with mental illness, showing larger effect sizes than other beneficial activities.
RET has well-documented antidepressant effects when compared with inactive control conditions; however, how it performs against active comparators is still unclear. This study quantified how RET's efficacy relates to both active and inactive comparisons for depressive symptoms and to examine variability across moderators.
Articles that had been published were located through Google Scholar, PubMed, MEDLINE, Web of Science, and PsycInfo, covering the period from each database's start to July 7, 2025. The review included randomized clinical trials (RCTs) that involved at least 3 weeks of RET or any comparison without RET, along with a validated measure of depressive symptoms taken at baseline and again at 3 weeks or later.
The active comparison groups consisted of aerobic exercise training, relaxation, flexibility, mind-body exercise, nutrition, education, advice or attention, mixed activities, and balance exercises. The inactive comparison groups consisted of no treatment, waiting lists, and continuation of usual care. Data were extracted independently by three reviewers. Meta-regression was applied to examine moderators, among them the primary moderators (sex, age, mental health status, RET duration, intensity, and concealed allocation).
Across 80 randomized controlled trials with 6123 participants (mean age 57 years; 67.1% female), resistance exercise training showed a significant antidepressant effect that varied between studies. The overall effect was small (Δ = 0.32), and it was larger when compared with inactive controls (Δ = 0.54) than with active comparators (Δ = 0.21). In comparison with active controls, the benefits were greater among participants who had a mental illness (Δ = 0.40).
When compared with inactive controls, the effects were larger in all-female cohorts (Δ = 0.87), when RET was performed at least 4 days per week (Δ = 0.46), and when sessions lasted 30 minutes or less (Δ = 1.75); concealed allocation, however, was linked to smaller effects (Δ = 0.32). Overall, resistance exercise training is endorsed as a feasible exercise-based treatment for depressive symptoms in both treatment guidelines and practice, particularly when behavioral treatment is preferred.
