TITLE: Exercise and brain training may preserve vulnerable brain regions
OUTLET: News-Medical

Combining aerobic exercise with computerized cognitive training may help preserve brain regions vulnerable to Alzheimer’s disease in older adults with memory impairment, but new research suggests the benefits are selective and far from definitive.
Study: Multicomponent Lifestyle Intervention and Brain Aging. A Secondary Analysis of a Randomized Clinical Trial. Image credit: Victor Moussa/Shutterstock.com
In a recent paper published in the journal JAMA Network Open, the authors report a secondary analysis of a randomized trial testing the effect of combined aerobic exercise with cognitive training on brain structure in older adults with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI). The authors report selective evidence of relative preservation of cortical thickness in brain regions vulnerable to Alzheimer’s disease.
Combining physical and cognitive training to address brain aging
Cortical thinning in brain regions vulnerable to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and white matter hyperintensities (WMH) are complementary imaging markers of brain aging, and both are common in aMCI. These structural abnormalities are associated with accelerated cognitive decline and progression to Alzheimer’s dementia. Aerobic exercise and cognitive training have long been recommended separately for cognitive health. They may act through partly distinct pathways, so combining them is plausible.
However, there is limited and inconsistent evidence for structural benefit from such combined interventions. The US POINTER trial found a modest cognitive benefit, but its imaging ancillary study showed no overall effect on structural biomarkers.
Combined programs also demand more time and effort. The current analysis sought to answer some of these questions.
Testing four lifestyle interventions in older adults with aMCI
The ACT Trial was a phase 2, single-blind, multisite randomized trial conducted at the University of Minnesota, the University of Rochester, and Arizona State University. Of 911 people screened, 146 adults with aMCI were randomized to four arms of 37, 36, 36, and 37 participants.
The mean participant age was 73.8 years, with about 92% being White and 51.4% male. Participants had impaired memory with preserved daily function, no dementia, and no major psychiatric, cardiac, or neurologic conditions, depression, or contraindications to exercise or MRI. They underwent brain imaging through magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at baseline, after the intervention, and at 12 and 18 months, with blinded assessment.
Participants were scheduled to complete 72 supervised sessions over six months in one of four intervention groups:
- Combined aerobic exercise and cognitive training (ACT): cycling plus speed-of-processing (SOP) computer training, 80 minutes per session.
- Cycling alone at moderate-to-vigorous intensity, 30–50 minutes per session.
- SOP training alone using computerized attention and processing-speed games, 30–50 minutes per session.
- Mental leisure activities plus stretching (MS), as an active comparison group, 80 minutes per session.
The authors assessed two main imaging outcomes: Alzheimer disease signature cortical thickness (ADSCT), calculated as the mean thickness of the bilateral entorhinal, fusiform, middle temporal, and inferior temporal cortices, and natural log–transformed white matter hyperintensity volume (ln-WMH).
The primary analysis used outcome-specific modified intention-to-treat populations, comprising 144 participants with baseline ADSCT measurements and 141 with baseline WMH measurements. It used 100 forward-only multiple imputations for missing follow-up scans and linear mixed-effects models adjusted for age, sex, and site.
Sensitivity analyses included only visit-specific complete cases. Exploratory analyses examined attendance, with "high" attendance defined in advance as at least 47 of 72 sessions (65.3%). Whole-brain volume was a post hoc outcome.
Combined training shows selective but uncertain structural brain benefits
In the primary imputed analyses, ACT participants showed greater preservation of cortical thickness than the SOP and MS groups at certain follow-up assessments. At 18 months, cortical thinning was greater in the SOP group than in the ACT group, with a between-group difference in change of −0.060 mm. Similarly, the MS group showed greater thinning than the ACT group at 12 months (−0.066 mm). However, the cycling-alone group did not differ significantly from ACT at any assessment.
Within groups, ADSCT remained relatively stable with ACT but decreased in the SOP group at 12 and 18 months and in the MS group at 12 months.
However, the cortical thickness findings were sensitive to how missing data were handled. Neither of the statistically significant differences observed in the primary analyses was reproduced when only participants with complete imaging data at the relevant follow-up were included.
The evidence for white matter benefits was less convincing, with no statistically significant between-group differences in ln-WMH progression in the primary analyses. Although the complete-case analysis showed greater progression in the cycling-alone group than in the ACT group at 18 months (0.452 log units), this difference was smaller and not statistically significant in the imputed model.
Together, these findings suggest a potential but selective benefit of combined training for cortical thickness, although inconsistencies across analytical approaches warrant cautious interpretation.
Greater attendance linked to cortical preservation, but uncertainties remain
Attendance did not differ significantly across the intervention groups. Of the 138 participants who attended at least one session, 100 met the high-attendance threshold. Among these participants, the SOP group showed less favorable ADSCT changes than the ACT group at 12 and 18 months, while cycling alone was associated with greater ln-WMH progression at 18 months.
Within the ACT group, each additional 10 sessions was associated with a more favorable post-intervention cortical thickness change of 0.022 mm, although no similar association was observed for ln-WMH. Because attendance was measured after randomization, the authors caution that factors such as motivation, health, or tolerance may explain this association.
Despite the apparent cortical thickness benefits, there was no evidence that combining aerobic exercise and cognitive training produced effects beyond those expected from their individual contributions. However, the study was not adequately powered to assess synergistic effects. Whole-brain volume changes also did not differ significantly between groups in the primary imputed analysis, suggesting that the apparent cortical preservation was not accompanied by a consistent global volume effect.
White matter changes were similarly inconsistent over time. Within-group analyses showed increases in ln-WMH most clearly in the cycling-alone group, both after the intervention and at 18 months, and in the ACT group after the intervention and at 12 months. However, these descriptive findings did not establish differences between interventions, and WMH trajectories did not follow a consistent upward or downward pattern.
Interpretation was further complicated by declining imaging completion, with complete-case samples ranging from 73 to 101 participants depending on the assessment. By 18 months, only about half of the original randomized participants had the imaging measurements required for these analyses.
Imaging limitations and missing data warrant cautious interpretation
Although the study benefited from randomized intervention allocation, standardized multisite imaging, and follow-up beyond the supervised intervention, several limitations warrant consideration.
The relatively small outcome-specific sample sizes and declining imaging completion rates over the follow-up period limited the robustness of the findings, while assumptions about missing data could not be verified. Furthermore, because all four groups received active interventions, the study could not establish whether combined training offers structural brain benefits compared with usual care or no structured intervention.
Additional uncertainty arises from processing each MRI scan independently rather than using a longitudinal template, which may increase measurement variability. Participants were also diagnosed on clinical grounds without confirmation from amyloid or tau biomarkers, meaning the findings cannot be attributed specifically to AD pathology.
The predominantly White, non-Hispanic, and highly educated study population further limits the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, cortical thickness and WMH are indirect markers of brain health.
Given these limitations and the inconsistent white matter findings, the authors caution against interpreting the results as evidence that cycling increases WMH or that ACT prevents their progression.
Combined training shows preliminary promise for cortical preservation
This analysis provides preliminary evidence that combined aerobic exercise and cognitive training may help preserve cortical thickness in brain regions vulnerable to Alzheimer’s disease in older adults with aMCI. This was in comparison with computer-based cognitive training alone at 18 months and with stretching plus mental activities at 12 months.
However, the signal was selective and appeared only in some comparisons. It was not consistently reproduced in complete-case analyses. No white matter hyperintensity advantage could be established, and combined training did not demonstrate a statistically significant cortical thickness advantage over cycling alone.
The attendance findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating and do not establish a causal dose-response relationship. The authors call for larger confirmatory trials to determine whether combined interventions truly preserve vulnerable cortex, and if so, whether such structural preservation translates into cognitive or functional benefit.
