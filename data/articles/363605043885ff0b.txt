TITLE: Texas no longer asks court to make Character.ai name its $10m buyer's backer
OUTLET: PPC Land

Texas today rewrote its objection to the $10 million sale of Character.ai's app, models and user data to Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, taking out its request that the court order Character Technologies, Inc. to disclose the buyer's parent company or sponsor and signing on to the objection filed on November 20 by a coalition of 42 states and the District of Columbia. The 18-page amended objection, Docket No. 224 in case 26-11603 (BLS) in the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, was filed at 1:28 p.m. Eastern according to the court's docket, the same afternoon as a scheduled hearing on the buyer's designation and the day before bids are due. The eight conditions Texas wants attached to any transfer of user data are unchanged, starting with a bar on moving the personal information of known minors without a verified parent's consent.
In Short
Texas has narrowed its own objection to Character.ai's planned $10 million sale to Smirks and Pangs: it no longer asks the judge to make the company identify who stands behind the buyer, and it has joined the larger objection from 42 states and DC. Its own filing now targets only the sale and the order that would approve it, not the designation of Smirks and Pangs as the opening bidder, although the coalition objection it joined still asks the court to refuse that designation on the current record. What has not changed is the part that matters for users and advertisers: if the judge accepts Texas's terms, no known Texas minor's personal information moves without a verified parent's consent, chats cannot train models without consent, and the buyer must honor earlier opt-outs from data sales and targeted advertising.
What Texas changed
The new document calls itself "The State of Texas's Amended Objection" and adds a second half to its title: a "Joinder to the Coalition of States Objection to the Debtor's Additional Stalking Horse Sale." It is filed by the Bankruptcy and Collections Division of the Office of the Attorney General of Texas, under Attorney General Ken Paxton, and signed by Assistant Attorney General Stephanie Eberhardt, with Sahrish K. Soleja and Layla D. Milligan also listed, the same team that filed the original on November 20. That original, Docket No. 216, was covered when PPC Land reported that Texas said Character.ai's $10m sale can't move minors' data without parents.
A line-by-line comparison of the two filings shows that most of the text carries over word for word under new paragraph numbers. The changes are concentrated in four places: the list of grounds, a deleted section on disclosure, the scope of the orders Texas wants to shape, and the relief.
The filing gives no reason for the amendment.
The disclosure demand that moved
In the November 20 version, the buyer's undisclosed backer was a standalone ground. Texas argued that the designation notice, filed on November 14, left out what paragraph 4 of the court's bidding procedures order required for a newly formed bidder, and that without those details neither Texas nor the court could judge whether the buyer could comply with the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act, the Securing Children Online Through Parental Empowerment Act and other law. It asked the court to direct the company to disclose the parent company or sponsor before the sale hearing. That section, the ground that introduced it and the matching item in the request for relief are all gone.
The point has not disappeared entirely. It now lives only in the argument against a good-faith finding under section 363(m) of the Bankruptcy Code, the finding that would protect the sale from being undone on appeal. "The Supplement does not identify the Purchaser's parent company or sponsor, as paragraph 4 of the Bidding Procedures Order requires," the amended objection says. "The APA itself shows that an unnamed 'affiliate of Purchaser' signed the confidentiality agreement on November 4, 2026, ten days before the Purchaser was designated on the designation deadline."
Texas still asks the court to "decline to make any section 363(m) finding unless the Debtor and the Purchaser file sworn evidence on these subjects before the Sale Hearing." If those schedules or statements are filed under seal, Texas asks to receive them. In the alternative, it wants any finding limited to the transfer of title, with the no-successor, no-liability and injunction provisions, and the governmental-unit reservation, treated as outside the consideration for the sale and open to review. The missing sponsor, in other words, has become an evidence problem for the sale hearing rather than a defect in the designation that Texas asks the court to cure first.
The narrower framing also shows in two smaller edits. Where the original asked for Texas-law language in any order on the designation notice as well as in any sale order, the amended text says "any order entered on any sale." And where Texas originally wanted its protections for personal information written into any order on the designation notice, including one approving the bid protections, it now refers only to "any order entered on the sale." The bid protections are a $350,000 termination payment, 3.5% of the price, that the estate would owe Smirks and Pangs if another bidder wins.
Joining the 42-state coalition
The second change is a single sentence. "The State of Texas also joins Coalition of States' Objection," paragraph 4 now reads, replacing a reservation of the right to join other objections.
That coalition filed Docket No. 219 late on November 20. It asks the court to keep users' chats, and any models trained on them, out of the sale unless users consent after direct notice, to exclude six further categories of data, to refuse a good-faith finding for user data and to impose nine operating conditions on any buyer, as PPC Land reported when 42 states and DC asked the court to keep chats out of Character.ai's $10m sale. By PPC Land's count, Texas's joinder puts 43 states and the District of Columbia behind that objection. Seven states are not on it: Alabama, Arizona, Iowa, Montana, Nebraska, New Mexico and Wyoming.
The coalition had only partly joined Texas's original filing. It adopted everything except the parts objecting to the designation or the break-up fee, on the ground that the deadline for those objections had passed. The passages Texas has now deleted are the ones that dealt with the designation notice and the order on the bid protections, the same territory the coalition carved out.
One loose end remains. The coalition's own request for relief asks the court to decline the designation of Smirks and Pangs and its termination payment on the current record. Texas's joinder carries no stated exception, and the amended objection does not say whether Texas adopts that request while withdrawing its own challenge to the designation.
What the company said this morning
The amendment followed a reply Character Technologies filed earlier in the day, Docket No. 220, asking the court to overrule objections to the designation from the US Trustee and from Texas. According to that reply, Texas's objection to the designation notice came after the extended deadline for objections, and the company was unsure whether Texas meant to oppose the designation itself or only the substance of the sale. The reply also said counsel for Texas would receive the sponsor's identity on a confidential basis, to the extent Texas had not already been given it. The company had asked on November 19 to keep that identity out of the public record, as covered when Character.ai asked the court to keep its $10m buyer's backer secret; Texas was not on its original list of recipients.
The amended objection does not mention the reply. It keeps its own account of the timetable: the bidding procedures order set the objection deadline at 9 p.m. Eastern on November 18, "but following an agreement with counsel, the Debtor extended that deadline for the State of Texas to 11:59 p.m. (ET) on November 20, 2026." It does not say whether Texas now has the sponsor's name. The US Trustee's objection over the unnamed backer, reported when Character.ai's $10m bidder faced objection over its unnamed backer, is not affected by the Texas amendment.
What Texas still asks for
The substance of the original objection survives intact. Texas opens, as before, with the scope of the deal: "The Debtor seeks Court approval of a sale of the Character.AI consumer platform itself, together with the account data, chat content, and parental-consent and age-assurance records of its users, many of them minors."
Texas has two open civil investigative demands to the company, issued by its attorney general on December 31, 2024, and August 19, 2025, concerning possible violations of the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act and the SCOPE Act relating to minors' use of Character.AI, and of the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act relating to "false assertions of medical licensure, academic credentials, patient confidentiality, and other privacy assertions by the Debtor and its chatbots." The purchase agreement lists those demands as a "Regulatory Matter." The state announced its inquiries in December 2024 and August 2025, as covered when Texas launched investigations into Character.AI and Meta for children's privacy.
The core request is unchanged: "The Purchaser should be obligated to provide notice and an opportunity to opt out of any transfer of data for all Texas users and also obtain the opt-in consent from the parents of Texas users who are minors." Should the court approve any transfer, Texas wants the sale order to bind both the company and Smirks and Pangs to eight conditions, enforceable by Texas.
Condition (f) connects to age-assurance commitments already in the contract: the purchase agreement's interim covenant bars the company from materially reducing "age-assurance, minor-protection, content-moderation, or crisis-response controls pending Closing," according to the objection. Under section 959(b) of title 28, Texas argues, the company may not use the sale order to move Texans' data in a way Texas law would forbid an owner outside bankruptcy, and may not "relax the guardrails" while it runs the platform before closing or provides transition services through February 18, 2027.
Federal law is cited in support. The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, or COPPA, authorizes a state attorney general to sue as parens patriae to enforce its regulations, and the Texas Legislature directed that the Deceptive Trade Practices Act be read in light of Section 5 of the FTC Act. "The FTC and States have long treated the sale of consumer data in breach of the promises under which it was collected as a deceptive practice," the objection says.
On the sale order itself, Texas repeats its challenge to terms the purchase agreement requires: a finding that the buyer is not a successor, a bar on liability "relating to any act or omission of Seller or any user interactions with Seller's products or services," an injunction against "all Persons" and "any other provision reasonably requested by Purchaser." Del. 2017) for the point that the duty to obey state law is not an "interest" a free-and-clear sale can strip, and Harrington v.
