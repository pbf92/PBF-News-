TITLE: Exercise may be good for your social life, too
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

WASHINGTON, D.C. – If you’ve been thinking about joining one of the social running, walking or hiking clubs popping up on your social media feeds, new research from The Ohio State University offers another reason to give it a try: Working out with other people might serve as a social lubricant.
The fitness platform Strava reported in 2025 that new clubs on the app nearly quadrupled to 1 million total clubs. Regular physical activity is known to have myriad health benefits, bolstering everything from cardiovascular fitness to mental well-being. Social support similarly has been linked to better health outcomes. In 2023, U.S. Surgeon General Vivek H. Murthy warned in an advisory titled “Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation” that declining rates of meaningful social connection represent “an urgent public health concern.”
Now, researchers have uncovered evidence that exercise may influence not only physical and mental health but also how we interact with one another. In a study of mice, animal pairs that regularly exercised together on running wheels were less likely to fight over resources such as food and warm resting spots and showed increased activity in brain regions associated with social behavior.
The research poster was presented today (Nov. 18, 2026) at Neuroscience 2026, the annual meeting of the Society for Neuroscience.
“Mice are known to be naturally social. They like hanging out with other mice, just like humans like hanging out with other humans. They also exercise naturally. If you give them a running wheel, they run about six miles a night, voluntarily,” said Liz Kirby, an associate professor of behavioral neuroscience in the Department of Psychology at Ohio State and the principal investigator of the study.
The research team housed same-sex male and female pairs of mice together for six weeks. Some pairs had 24/7 access to functioning running wheels, while others had wheels that couldn’t turn. The researchers then tested how the mice behaved when competing for desirable resources, including a tasty treat and a warm resting spot that only one mouse could use at a time. They measured and compared behaviors associated with competitive aggression, like trying to steal food or push a cage mate off the warm spot.
In a follow-up experiment, the researchers limited some mice to scheduled “gym” sessions, providing access to running wheels for six hours a day, five days a week. After six weeks, they measured how the mice behaved when competing for food and for a coveted warm resting spot.
“What we found is that when the mice were competing to access something like a really delicious food pellet, the mice who exercised were better at sharing the food pellet with each other,” Kirby said, “whereas those who didn’t weren’t as good at sharing it. They would rip it from each other’s paws and run away with it in a cage and chase each other down. Generally very rude behavior when it comes to mealtime.”
This was also true for the mice with time-limited access to running wheels. “Living with a wheel and visiting the gym both seem to reduce this competitive fighting behavior over limited resources,” Kirby added.
Will Butke, a doctoral student in Kirby’s lab and the lead author of the study, said existing research into what’s happening in the brain when people exercise socially is limited.
“People think about the physiological benefits: How does exercise improve your heart health, your lung health, your quality of life? But it’s also important to intersect exercise with its social aspect. People like to run in groups or exercise together — not only because you get to have a person you can rely upon, but also it gets you into a social context where you can de-stress,” he said. “That’s what we want to look at specifically.”
The team used a technique that highlights recently active brain cells to identify which regions of the brain became more active after exercise. They found that the running mice showed increased activity in specific brain regions linked to social behavior, while other regions remained unchanged.
“This points toward the idea that this isn’t just a brain-wide increase in activation, but instead that the activation is specifically in certain subregions of the brain,” he said.
While it’s difficult to draw direct comparisons between mice and people, “the work suggests that exercise does have a direct social benefit in that it reduces competitive fighting behavior,” Kirby said. “This adds to the list of potential benefits you could be getting by exercising. It’s not just going to make your heart healthier, and it’s not just going to reduce your risk for cancer, obesity and stroke, but it also might help strengthen your social ties.”
In addition to Butke and Kirby, the research team included Bryon Smith of Ohio State.
