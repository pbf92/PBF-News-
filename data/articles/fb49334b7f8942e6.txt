TITLE: Narrative Review Outlines Framework Aligning Dysmenorrhea Management With Individual Fertility Goals
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A narrative review published in Frontiers in Medicine outlines a structured clinical framework designed to coordinate menstrual pain management, secondary etiologic assessment, and individualized reproductive planning among women of reproductive age. Authored by researchers from Changchun University of Chinese Medicine, the synthesis examines how treatment strategies for dysmenorrhea can achieve effective symptom relief while systematically evaluating underlying pelvic pathologies and addressing reproductive priorities. Menstrual pain frequently affects women across their reproductive years, occurring either as primary dysmenorrhea without identifiable organic lesions or secondary to structural gynecologic conditions such as endometriosis, uterine adenomyosis, uterine fibroids, and pelvic inflammatory disease. The authors emphasize that routine reliance on continuous empirical pain relief without structured reassessment risks delaying the clinical recognition of progressive secondary disorders that can impair future fertility.
To build this clinical framework, the investigators conducted comprehensive literature searches across PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar between July 2026 and September 28, 2026, without lower publication-date limits. The search strategy incorporated diverse clinical and historical terms encompassing primary and secondary dysmenorrhea, specific pelvic structural conditions, polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, ovulatory dysfunction, ovarian reserve markers, and assisted reproduction. From these searches, the authors synthesized findings from 149 cited publications, encompassing clinical guidelines, consensus statements, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and observational studies. The authors explicitly note that their methodology followed a narrative rather than a systematic review protocol, with no quantitative pooling of data, formal risk of bias scoring, or prospective clinical validation. Consequently, the proposed framework serves as a conceptual model to assist clinical decision-making rather than establishing validated investigation thresholds or graded clinical practice recommendations.
The review delineates the pathophysiological distinction between primary dysmenorrhea and secondary causes while addressing the clinical overlap that complicates early diagnosis. Primary dysmenorrhea stems from elevated prostaglandin production leading to excessive uterine contractions, local tissue ischemia, and inflammatory signaling. While initial empirical treatment with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or hormonal therapies is standard for typical presentations after obvious pathology is excluded, early-stage secondary conditions often present with similar symptom intensity. The authors observe that pain severity does not reliably differentiate primary from secondary disease, nor does it correlate directly with the anatomic extent of conditions such as endometriosis. Instead, secondary etiologies are frequently flagged by evolving clinical features, including abnormal uterine bleeding, nonmenstrual or acyclic pelvic pain, deep dyspareunia, dyschezia, dysuria, and progressively worsening pain that demonstrates an inadequate response to established medical therapies.
Endometriosis represents a prominent secondary condition where symptom burden and reproductive implications frequently diverge from pain severity. The disorder can impair fertility through widespread pelvic inflammation, structural pelvic adhesions, anatomical alterations of the fallopian tubes and ovaries, and compromised endometrial receptivity and ovarian function. In reviewing surgical management, the authors note that operative laparoscopy may improve spontaneous conception rates in select women with minimal or mild disease, though evidence does not support routine immediate surgery for all patients experiencing pain and subfertility. The review highlights that conservative ovarian cystectomy for endometriomas may decrease recurrent pain and lesion recurrence compared to drainage or ablation, but randomized Cochrane evidence indicates low certainty regarding spontaneous pregnancy rates within the first postoperative year. Furthermore, ovarian surgery carries documented risks of reducing ovarian reserve markers, such as serum anti-Müllerian hormone, especially in the context of bilateral or recurrent endometriomas.
The synthesis also evaluates the complex clinical management of uterine adenomyosis, which commonly manifests with dysmenorrhea, abnormal bleeding, and compromised reproductive outcomes. Reviewing evidence from a prospective observational study of 228 women undergoing their first donor oocyte single embryo transfer, the authors highlight that while implantation, clinical pregnancy, and live birth rates did not differ significantly between groups, women with adenomyosis experienced a significantly higher rate of miscarriage. This miscarriage risk was particularly pronounced among patients with junctional zone involvement, diffuse lesions, and greater overall disease severity. Conservative medical treatments, such as levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine systems, combined oral contraceptives, and progestins, offer effective symptom control for women deferring conception. However, uterus-sparing surgical interventions remain supported only by heterogeneous observational data from 32 studies comprising 2,501 women, with no randomized controlled trials establishing clear fertility benefits over expectant management.
Uterine fibroids and the chronic sequelae of pelvic inflammatory disease are analyzed as distinct structural causes whose fertility effects depend heavily on anatomic distribution. For uterine fibroids, submucosal and cavity-distorting intramural lesions pose the primary obstacle to embryo implantation and maintenance of pregnancy. Drawing on American Society for Reproductive Medicine guidance, the review notes that hysteroscopic removal of submucosal fibroids may improve clinical pregnancy rates, although evidence regarding live birth improvements remains limited. Conversely, routine myomectomy for non-cavity-distorting intramural or subserosal fibroids solely to improve fertility is generally discouraged, as Cochrane review findings derived from four trials with 442 women showed very low-quality evidence and demonstrated no clear reproductive advantage between surgical modalities while carrying risks of postoperative pelvic or intrauterine adhesions.
Reproductive endocrine disorders, particularly polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome—formerly designated as polycystic ovary syndrome—are examined as conditions that frequently coexist with dysmenorrhea but require separate diagnostic handling. Characterized by ovulatory dysfunction, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology, this endocrine disorder contributes substantially to anovulatory subfertility. The authors highlight that while polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome is not recognized as a direct structural cause of dysmenorrhea, chronic pelvic pain and menstrual pain frequently occur in these patients. In a retrospective electronic health record analysis from the TriNetX network, dysmenorrhea and pelvic pain were documented in women with this endocrine disorder even after excluding recorded diagnoses of endometriosis or fibroids, although observational diagnostic codes cannot exclude unrecognized coexisting disease. The authors emphasize that clinicians must not automatically attribute severe menstrual pain to ovulatory dysfunction, nor should ovulatory treatments substitute for targeted investigations of secondary structural pain etiologies.
Pain management during the preconception period requires balancing therapeutic efficacy with the biological requirements of natural conception. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs remain the primary pharmacological option for acute symptom relief due to their inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis, with agents such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac exhibiting comparable general efficacy. While short-term, low-dose administration during active menstruation has not been shown to reduce natural conception rates, the authors caution that high-dose or repeated administration extending into the periovulatory window may inhibit or delay ovulation. Hormonal therapies, while highly effective for symptom suppression, induce contraception or ovulatory suppression and are incompatible with active conception attempts. Guidelines from the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology explicitly advise against using hormonal ovarian suppression solely as a strategy to enhance future fertility in women with endometriosis-associated subfertility.
Clinical evaluations of transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, acupuncture, and acupressure have also demonstrated reductions in menstrual discomfort and improvements in quality of life scores, although study protocols show considerable heterogeneity in blinding methods, control designs, and treatment parameters. Nutritional supplements and herbal preparations, including ginger, cinnamon, fennel, omega-3 fatty acids, and vitamin D, have shown variable pain reductions in clinical trials. However, the authors emphasize that evidence is strictly limited to subjective pain outcomes, with no verified data indicating improvements in ovulation timing, conception rates, or pregnancy viability. Consequently, these therapies should serve strictly as supportive pain relief rather than presumed fertility-enhancing interventions.
The review details the sequential role of noninvasive and invasive diagnostic modalities, starting with pelvic ultrasonography. When conducted according to structured consensus guidelines, such as the International Deep Endometriosis Analysis framework, ultrasound can identify obliterated pouch of Douglas anatomy and deep infiltrative lesions through specific dynamic markers such as the sliding sign. However, prospective diagnostic data indicate that ultrasound sensitivity for superficial peritoneal endometriosis remains low, ranging between 4.0% and 43.5%. Therefore, a normal ultrasound examination cannot definitively rule out superficial disease in patients with persistent, debilitating symptoms.
Magnetic resonance imaging provides targeted secondary diagnostic utility, particularly when ultrasound findings are inconclusive or when extensive preoperative mapping of deep infiltrating lesions involving the bowel, bladder, or rectovaginal septum is required. In a comparative evaluation of 178 women with surgically verified deep endometriosis, magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated higher overall sensitivity for identifying deep lesions than physical examination or transvaginal ultrasound alone. Similarly, for adenomyosis, dedicated re-evaluation of magnetic resonance imaging scans improved overall diagnostic accuracy from 65.6% to 82.4% compared with standard routine clinical interpretation. Regarding ovarian reserve biomarkers, including anti-Müllerian hormone and antral follicle counts, the authors stress that these tests serve to predict response to controlled ovarian stimulation rather than natural fecundability. Low ovarian reserve markers should not be interpreted as an inability to conceive naturally, nor do they identify the underlying etiology of dysmenorrhea.
Ultimately, the authors organize their findings into a three-tiered clinical management model structured around the patient’s immediate reproductive timeline. For women with no near-term pregnancy plans, therapeutic priorities focus on sustained symptom control, functional preservation, and structured periodic reassessment to detect emerging signs of secondary pathology. For women preparing for pregnancy in the medium term, clinicians should coordinate nonhormonal pain relief while selectively initiating diagnostic investigations based on risk history, such as previous pelvic infections or ovarian surgeries.
