TITLE: OpenAI’s MedChemBench tests GPT models on drug research decisions | ETIH EdTech News
OUTLET: EdTech Innovation Hub

GPT-6.2 Astra led the 99-problem benchmark with 84.5% of the available credit, while results for GPT-6 Astra showed where AI still struggles with decisions in drug research
OpenAI’s new medicinal chemistry benchmark asks AI models to make a decision drug researchers face repeatedly. Given what a lab already knows about a group of compounds, which one is worth testing next? In OpenAI’s evaluation, GPT-6 Astra earned 95.4% of the available credit for reconstructing molecular structures. It earned about half the credit on tasks that asked it to select compounds or predict numerical properties.
OpenAI introduced MedChemBench on November 20. It built the test from 117 published papers and patents, drawing on 761 distinct molecular structures. The problems trace decisions from past research projects, giving models some of the evidence chemists had at the time and asking them to work through what follows.
When the strongest compound has a drawback
A molecule can be good at blocking a protein and still be difficult to work with. It might have trouble crossing a cell barrier or break down too quickly. Chemists weighing up their next experiment have to consider those properties together.
One MedChemBench problem draws on studies of PRMT5, a protein investigated in cancer drug discovery. Models were given structures for three related compounds and findings from earlier experiments. They had to predict how strongly each compound would block PRMT5 in cells, how readily it would cross a cell barrier and how quickly liver enzymes would break it down in a lab test.
The later results, which OpenAI withheld from the models, pulled in different directions. Compound 23 blocked the protein at a lower concentration. Compound 24 crossed the barrier more readily and broke down more slowly. The value of either result depends on which problem the researchers are trying to solve next.
OpenAI awarded partial credit based on how close a prediction came to the measured value. Models also had to reproduce any measurements they had already been given, so the exercise tested whether they could keep track of the evidence as well as make a prediction from it.
An 18-atom ring shows what a misread drawing can change
A chemical drawing tells a chemist which atoms are connected and, where specified, how parts of the molecule are arranged in three dimensions. In one task, OpenAI asked models to assemble a compound from a central structure and two attached groups while preserving an 18-atom ring.
GPT-6 Astra got the structure right in all 10 responses at the shared xhigh reasoning setting. GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.6 Luna failed in all 10. In the examples OpenAI published, Sol produced a 15-atom ring and Luna split the molecule into two disconnected fragments. In both cases, the model had drawn a different molecule from the one it was supposed to assess.
Accurate reconstruction was one of GPT-6 Astra’s strengths. OpenAI also found it performed well at explaining experimental evidence. In examples from the research paper, it identified changes to a compound’s structure and explained how those changes might affect properties such as absorption or breakdown. Turning that explanation into a numerical prediction, or using it to choose which compound to test, proved harder.
What the overall scores tell researchers
OpenAI evaluated 11 GPT models. At xhigh, the highest reasoning setting shared by all 11, GPT-6.2 Astra led with 84.5% of the available credit. GPT-6 Astra followed at 83.3%, with GPT-6.1 Sol close behind at 82.0%. GPT-6.1 Terra earned 75.1% and GPT-5.6 Sol earned 58.2%. At its separate maximum reasoning setting, GPT-6 Astra rose to 83.8%.
Those overall figures combine several kinds of work, from reading a chemical drawing to explaining evidence, ranking compounds and predicting measurements. The component scores show how much the overall result can obscure. GPT-6 Astra’s score for reconstructing molecules was far higher than its scores in the areas closest to choosing the next experiment.
Each response was produced in a single attempt, without tools, feedback or fresh experiments. OpenAI also says the models may have encountered some of the published material before. The question left for laboratory research is whether an AI model can help a chemist decide what to test when the answer has yet to be published.
