TITLE: Grand Theft Auto VI Review, Gameplay Impressions, Videos and Top Features
OUTLET: Bleacher Report

Grand Theft Auto VI from Rockstar Games overdelivers.
It's a stunning statement, really. Each GTA tends to hang around in the top half of greatest games of all time discussions. When Rockstar isn't making them, it's busy making all-time classics like Red Dead Redemption 2 or quieter generational hits like Bully, Max Payne and L.A. Noire.
Thirteen years since the last entry in the series, GTA 6 arrives and almost laughs off all-time expectations with a best-ever open world, a level of immersion that's hard to explain and a game so gigantic and filled with depth that it's no wonder the rest of the video games released this year fled the November calendar like a sinking ship. They might want to start thinking about 2027 as a whole, too.

Graphics and Gameplay
Returning to Vice City, expanded and modernized, has gone from the imagination to the screen in ways that might leave the imagination wanting.
GTA 6 is breathtaking in its depth. Yes, all the bells and whistles are here in a way that modern hardware will spend years attempting to fully realize. It's a stunner of a looker, but the real treat comes in the near-endless details.
Density of both crowds and traffic is a feat for the gaming space. Same story, especially, for nighttime in GTA 6. It sounds a little strange rolling off the tongue, but this feels like the first game to let nighttime actually be dark, letting the stunning lighting systems take over in ways we've never really seen before.
Those light systems can be manmade, like headlights or the afterglow of a city's neons in the clouds, or natural, like lightning storms from a dynamic weather system.
While the odder-by-the-day Internet wages frames-per-second debates, the rest of the world will enjoy the way GTA masters the little things with alarming frequency. Just little appreciable things like dents in a punching bag, the random arrays of personal items scattered in each NPC car to give it a unique feel, the overwhelming number of enterable buildings, it just goes on and on.
In many ways, GTA is just bragging its game design superiority. Characters will organically squeeze through tighter spaces. They'll dual-wield or single-wield weapons or do everyday things naturally with their off-hands.
And on the sound design front, yes, there is no equal in the voice-acting department when weighing the protagonists right on down to the lowest of random NPCs out in the world. The budget demands it and delivers.
But it goes far beyond that. Yes, the mind-boggling number of stations, songs, and sheer material on the radio is borderline overwhelming. But everything, from the phone to the minigames and beyond, makes even a stroll down a street an engrossing thing.
The world simply feels like it would exist and move on without the player, which is pretty much a first in gaming. And the more measured pace of the experience almost encourages players to act more lawful and sane instead of a sociopath, fitting in to the point of maybe even obeying red lights. It's fun in a way that is honestly pretty hard to explain, but makes the action-packed missions and the actual time for combat all the more impactful.
Generally speaking, combat is a weighty, thrilling experience that layers atop the tried-and-true GTA experience. Take gunplay. Bigger weapons straight-up blast off limbs now.
And when the fight is done? Money to be had, sure, but if a player likes a victim's sunglasses, well, they can pluck those off the body and keep moving, too.
Same story for simple brawls with melee weapons. It's a little more measured than just button mashing, but that works too. Grisly violence and damage, this game has.
How much has GTA evolved? Bank heists were story missions with specific beats and scripted moments. But banks outside of the story could hardly ever even be entered. Now? Once equipped with the proper gear, players can rob whatever bank they choose in whatever manner they see fit.
There are consequences, of course. Namely, the Wanted system. It's evolved in the best way possible here: Now, police can only act on the information they have. Players don't have immersion-breaking map feeds to watch while they flee anymore. But they do have eyeballs and ears to listen to the radio callouts. And they have creativity: Dipping into a new car, a new outfit, hiding, blending in, there are just so many ways to experiment with and enjoy the Wanted system now that it's an exclamation point for the entire game (and will make going back to the older Wanted systems in the series very rocky).
Those wondering how Rockstar's Red Dead Redemption 2 systems would translate to modern GTA gameplay can rest easy:
It's amazing.
In loose terms, a player's vehicle functions as the horse might have for players in RDR2. It houses a stash of goodies, ranging from stolen items to outfit changes (for bank robberies, of course) to bigger weapons like rocket launchers. That goofy rocket-launcher-from-out-of-the-jeans-pockets stuff is a thing of the past and GTA is much better for it.
Now, there's more weight to decisions players must make on the fly. That doesn't mean pausing time to work a scroll wheel. It means decisions matter and everything is more lifelike.
Speaking of lifelike, this hits life simulation better than something like the Sims at points. Players can very much wake up and brew coffee for buffs, hit a couple missions, then go bar-hopping while playing pool or whatever, all while keeping an eye on things like hunger, how much fuel is in the gas tank and calorie counts that might encourage a little exercise. There are buses, trains and rideshare companies to use to get around the map while flicking through a phone that features a fully functioning internet.
All that aside, GTA 6 almost encourages save-scumming a bit. They know it's going to happen anyway, so why not? A separate save file might be a good idea before doing some wacky stuff because, after all, one of the best stories in gaming history boasts multiple endings where a player's actions can very much dictate how things land after the 60-plus hours of long journey.
Story and More
Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos are the most authentic GTA protagonists to hit the series.
Jason is an army veteran with shades of mystery and Arthur Morgan, in a good way. Lucia, though, is the real triumph of GTA 6. She's a nuanced, confident character with a huge range of emotions and by far the most likable protagonist not just in the series, but arguably in gaming outright.
Both are less exaggerated caricatures like past protagonists and something more, well, normal. And normal means relatable. There are degrees of relatability, especially when going out for some fun crimes, but these two are a masterclass of relatable characters that make players care, and quickly.
Attempting to explain the scope of the story is a task. There's less "let's go get rich for reasons" and more everyday struggle as the duo bumbles their way into mistakes while trying to improve their situation. It's more Bonnie and Clyde on the run than anything, and all the unforgettable set pieces and side characters are there.
And that's just talking about the main story. This isn't a world of un-interactable backdrops or window dressing. There's a gaming console in the player's house with full games. The classic GTA minigames with surprising depth are all over the map. All that while digesting the thousands of hours of in-game media content on television, radio or otherwise.
This is, by far, the most immersive open world in the history of video games.
There are side areas and niches that could function as their own video games on their lonesome. The car culture throughout the map is a good example. In some ways, this might as well be Forza through some of the ways players can tweak engines and performance. Even the quality of the paint jobs varies from the local car joint on the block or the professional dealerships elsewhere.
Some might bemoan some of the more mundane missions. But ironically, it's the playerbase besieged by scrolling TikTok that has lost the plot here. GTA missions have always smartly broken up the pace with goofy (or not) bits that develop characters and create moments. Think, hitting some yoga with Michael De Santa, to stay very brief with the examples.
Those who would declare that there isn't as much satirization of the real world simply aren't paying enough attention, either. It's not as blunt and in a player's face this time. Instead, it's smoothly woven into handcrafted NPCs out in the world and the many layers of media, radio or otherwise, that don't go overboard while developing a superb (and sometimes uncomfortable) message.
We could spend eons writing about all of the side activities or the little encounters that create huge impressions. But it would feel, well, criminal, to spoil some of these things. Or any of it, really.
Yes, on the technical front, GTA 6 doesn't always run the best and those accustomed to higher frames will notice the 30 FPS or even below moments. But in the grand scheme of things, GTA 6 is such a compelling, unique experience that the little hiccups almost have their own charm and aren't immersion-breaking.
Conclusion
Grand Theft Auto VI is the peak of video games. This is it, and will be for a long time.
Who had GTA writing the most compelling protagonists and overall romance in the medium? Who could have confidently predicted that the broad themes of satirizing inequality, unfairness of the systems and life in today's America would be so easily accomplished in these absurd times?
Few. And that's to say nothing of the more measured, paced gameplay that borders on simulation. It's a standout in all facets that masters its small bits in ways games dedicated to those niches don't match.
Detractors will nitpick, but GTA 6 was never promised to implement all of the GTA Online craziness into its core experience. It's unabashedly GTA and better for it. GTA Online will arrive later.
Most of all, GTA 6 remained true to itself. It's so far beyond that we're past comparing it to other games and the conversation should shift to how it compares to all-time classics in other mediums. Only its sequel, perhaps in a decade or more, is the real threat to it at this point.
