Nasdaq 100 Forecast for 27 November 2026: After Touching Bear-Market Territory, Will the Rebound Hold?
On Wednesday 25 November 2026 the Nasdaq 100 fell to an intraday low of 24,970.66. That was 20.03% below the record close of 31,224.47 set on 6 October. The index then recovered most of the loss and closed at 26,046.90, down 0.64% on the day (FinancialMarkets.com, Yahoo Finance).
The key point for Friday is simple. The -20% line was crossed intraday. It was not crossed at the close. By the usual closing-level rule, the Nasdaq 100 is in a correction. It is not in a bear market.

25 November: From -20% to a Near-Full Recovery
The Nasdaq 100 lost as much as 4.7% from Tuesday's close in the morning session. The S&P 500 fell about 3.2% at its low (FinancialMarkets.com). From the low to the close, the Nasdaq 100 then rose about 4.3%. That morning, Benzinga said the index was on course for its worst two-month fall since 2008. At that point it was down roughly 17% since the end of September (Benzinga).
Why Did the Nasdaq 100 Fall on 25 November?
- Core PCE inflation for October came in at 3.3% year on year (BEA), above the 3.2% forecast cited by TMoney Daily (TMoney Daily).
- The US Trade Representative proposed new tariffs in 14 Section 301 investigations, which are not yet in force (USTR).
- Micron fell 10.4% in the morning (Benzinga).
- Nvidia and AMD each lost about 8% in early trading (Benzinga).
Credit markets had flashed a warning first. Five-year credit default swap spreads on several AI-linked borrowers had reached record levels (FinancialMarkets.com). Nvidia also had its AA rating placed on CreditWatch negative by S&P Global Ratings, according to Bloomberg (StockAnalysis). Our earlier pieces on the Micron downgrade and on AI bubble fears give more background.
Why Did the Rebound Happen?
CNBC linked the recovery partly to thin pre-holiday trading and a post by investor Michael Burry (TMoney Daily, citing CNBC).
Bear Market or Not? What the 20% Rule Actually Says
A bear market is commonly defined as a fall of at least 20% from the peak, measured on closing levels. A fall of 10% to 20% is usually called a correction.
