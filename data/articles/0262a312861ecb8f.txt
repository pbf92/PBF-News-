TITLE: Deprescribing Assessment Tools Show Significant Gaps Across Clinical Professions and Validation
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A comprehensive scoping review evaluating instruments designed to measure healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding deprescribing has revealed significant structural limitations across published research tools. Deprescribing is broadly conceptualized as the planned process of withdrawing an inappropriate medication under the supervision of a healthcare professional, aiming to optimize medication regimens, manage polypharmacy, and improve patient safety, potentially incorporating dose reductions or tapering depending on the specific clinical scenario. Although international research into medication discontinuation has expanded rapidly over recent years, the measurement instruments deployed to gauge the clinical competencies, beliefs, and behaviors of practitioners remain constrained in their scope and often lack essential psychometric validation.
The investigation, conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodological guidance for scoping reviews and reported following the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews, analyzed published primary studies involving nurses, pharmacists, and physicians. Eligible investigations applied research instruments assessing knowledge, attitudes, or practices related to deprescribing either individually or in combination, without restrictions on clinical setting or geographic location. Nine electronic databases—encompassing PubMed, Virtual Health Library, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and CINAHL, alongside grey literature searches in Google Scholar and ProQuest—were comprehensively searched from inception through September 2024. The underlying protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework platform and published in a peer-reviewed journal to ensure methodological transparency.
From an initial retrieval of 7,655 records across all searched sources, the research team removed 3,834 duplicates using reference management software, leaving 3,821 unique citations for title and abstract screening. Full-text assessment was performed independently by three pairs of calibrated reviewers on 105 articles retrieved from electronic databases alongside three records located through supplementary manual searches of reference lists. Following full-text evaluation, 59 publications were excluded, predominantly due to non-compliance with the deprescribing concept criteria, ineligible methodological designs, or status solely as conference abstracts, while seven multi-language duplicate publications were also removed. Ultimately, 49 primary studies satisfied all inclusion parameters and formed the quantitative and narrative synthesis.
Temporal analysis of the 49 included studies, which originally spanned from 1996 through 2024, demonstrated that research interest in professional deprescribing behavior is overwhelmingly recent. More than two-thirds of the included investigations, representing 67.3 percent (33 studies), were published from 2020 onward. This pronounced concentration coincides with wider demographic shifts, including population ageing and an increasing prevalence of multimorbidity, which have substantially heightened the complexity of pharmacotherapy management. In this environment, structured tools that investigate provider perceptions have become increasingly popular as researchers seek to uncover the professional determinants influencing medication discontinuation in routine clinical encounters.
Geographically and economically, the research landscape displays a marked concentration in affluent regions. Among the 46 studies that could be classified by economic status—excluding three multi-country studies that were not categorized by income level—78.0 percent (36 studies) were conducted within high-income nations. Looking at regional distributions across the 22 represented countries, the Europe and Central Asia region accounted for 41.0 percent (19 studies), followed by North America with 22.0 percent (10 studies). Individual country contributions were led by the United States with seven investigations (14.0 percent) and India with four studies (8.2 percent), raising important questions regarding whether instruments created in well-resourced environments can be transferred to healthcare systems facing differing organizational, regulatory, and infrastructural realities.
Regarding practice settings, primary care emerged as the predominant clinical arena, representing 37.0 percent (18 studies) of the synthesized evidence base. Hospitals accounted for 12.0 percent (6 studies), while community pharmacies served as the investigative location in 6.0 percent (3 studies), and 14.0 percent (7 studies) failed to clearly report the setting of data collection. In terms of research methodology, cross-sectional designs dominated the literature, utilized in 90.0 percent (44 studies) of included papers. Methodological studies focused on instrument validation represented 6.1 percent (3 studies), and mixed-methods investigations accounted for 4.1 percent (2 studies). Across the evaluated studies, sample sizes of eligible healthcare professionals ranged widely from 30 to 1,415 participants, yielding a mean sample size of 297 individuals with a standard deviation of 326.
Professional coverage within the identified instruments was heavily skewed toward medical doctors. Nearly two-thirds of the included literature, encompassing 65.0 percent (32 studies), focused exclusively on physicians. Conversely, studies dedicated exclusively to pharmacists comprised only 6.1 percent (3 studies), and investigations that simultaneously evaluated the tripartite team of physicians, pharmacists, and nurses were documented in only 16.0 percent (8 studies). This physician-centric focus contrasts with clinical deprescribing frameworks that emphasize collaborative interprofessional care, wherein pharmacists frequently perform detailed pharmacotherapy reviews and nurses actively monitor clinical responses and adverse events following medication withdrawal.
Among the 40 studies that provided sufficient questionnaire detail to evaluate individual survey items, structural implementation of the Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices framework was frequently fragmented. Only 30.0 percent (12 studies) simultaneously evaluated all three interrelated domains. When domains were examined in isolation, attitudes were assessed alone in 25.0 percent (10 studies) and practices in 7.5 percent (3 studies). Notably, knowledge was never assessed as an isolated standalone domain in any of the detailed instruments. Because the theoretical model posits that knowledge informs attitudes which subsequently shape clinical practices, evaluating these dimensions separately risks compromising the explanatory depth required to understand behavioral drivers in healthcare delivery.
Psychometric evaluation among the included instruments proved exceptionally sparse. Only 26.5 percent of the included studies (13 investigations) reported any empirical evidence of validity assessment for their measurement tools. Among these 13 validated instruments, internal consistency and content validity were the most frequently evaluated properties, each reported in 10 instruments. When reported in combination, internal consistency and content validity appeared together in 31.0 percent of the validated subset (4 studies). In contrast, broader test-retest reliability was evaluated in only two instruments, and criterion validity was examined in just a single study. Only one instrument achieved the most comprehensive psychometric assessment profile by testing internal consistency, content validity, construct validity, and criterion validity simultaneously.
The review authors noted that the predominance of early-stage properties, specifically content validity and internal consistency, aligns with the exploratory cross-sectional designs commonly applied in survey development. However, relying on internal consistency alone remains insufficient to demonstrate instrument quality, as high internal consistency reflects item correlation rather than true representation of the underlying clinical construct. With primary evidence heavily weighted toward physicians in high-income primary care settings, the synthesis underscores an urgent need for the development and psychometric refinement of theoretically grounded, multiprofessional assessment tools that reflect the shared interprofessional realities of modern medication discontinuation.
To categorize the depth of psychometric evidence systematically, the researchers applied a three-level validation classification framework adapted from principles outlined by the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments. Under this operational scheme, Level 1 comprised instruments evaluating only a single psychometric property, whereas Level 2 designated tools with two or more assessed properties excluding construct validity. Level 3 represented instruments reporting two or more measurement properties that specifically included construct validity. The authors emphasized that this descriptive framework served to benchmark available psychometric depth across the literature rather than to render definitive qualitative judgments regarding instrument adequacy.
Applying this evaluative framework revealed that higher-tier validation profiles remain remarkably scarce in deprescribing research. Only four instruments reached the criteria for Level 3 categorization. Among these top-tier measures, three instruments identified in publications by Agosti and colleagues, Shresta and colleagues, and Buzancic and colleagues each evaluated three separate properties, yet none examined criterion validity. The single instrument achieving a comprehensive four-property evaluation profile was documented by Niznik and colleagues, which uniquely assessed criterion validity alongside internal consistency, content validity, and construct validity. Overall, among the thirteen studies reporting psychometric measures, only one evaluated criterion validity and merely two assessed reliability, demonstrating notable omissions in testing stability and external comparison across clinical contexts.
The study selection and charting processes incorporated standardized calibrations to maintain evaluative rigor across the multi-investigator team. Prior to data extraction, three pairs of independent reviewers conducted calibration trials involving five articles per reviewer to align interpretations of study characteristics, population metrics, and questionnaire item classifications. Individual survey items were systematically categorized as knowledge, attitudes, or practices using an established structural framework, while items outside these dimensions were documented as other. Any discrepancies during screening or formal extraction were resolved through consensus or arbitrated by an experienced third reviewer, and statistical summaries were executed in R software.
Reflecting on broader limitations, the authors highlighted that their synthesis depended strictly on information explicitly disclosed within published texts, noting that some psychometric evaluations may have been performed without being documented. Considerable heterogeneity across survey structures, response scales, evaluated domains, and target professional groups also precluded meta-analytic pooling. Furthermore, the reliance on high-income cohorts and medical doctors restricts the immediate transferability of these instruments to developing health systems, where differences in regulatory authority, infrastructure, and electronic record access shape distinct clinical workflows. Future instrument design must prioritize interprofessional applicability and rigorous theoretical grounding.
