TITLE: About Six Percent of Youth in Obesity Care Have Binge Eating Disorder
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

Approximately six percent of children and adolescents living with obesity, most of whom were attending specialized obesity clinics, have coexisting binge eating disorder, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis published in the Journal of Eating Disorders.
The authors observed that although children and adolescents with obesity are recognized to be at an increased risk of developing eating disorders, scientific knowledge regarding the actual prevalence of these conditions in this demographic has remained scarce. To address this critical knowledge deficit, the research team carried out an extensive librarian-assisted literature search across five major biomedical and psychological databases, encompassing Medline via Ovid, Embase via Ovid, PsychInfo, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. The search captured published studies up to February 4, 2026.
From an initial total of 7,635 scientific records screened during the review process, only nine studies met all specified criteria for inclusion in the final synthesis. All nine selected studies investigated the prevalence of binge eating disorder among participating children and adolescents. By comparison, only three of the included studies gathered empirical data on bulimia nervosa and anorexia nervosa, demonstrating a notable shortage of broader clinical assessments.
Crucially, none of the included studies explored other specified feeding or eating disorder, a diagnostic category that encompasses atypical anorexia nervosa. Due to limitations in data availability across the identified literature, a formal quantitative meta-analysis could only be performed on the reported binge eating disorder data, precluding statistical pooling for other diagnostic classifications.
The quantitative meta-analysis yielded a pooled proportion of six percent for binge eating disorder among youth living with obesity, predominantly representing clinical populations attending specialized obesity clinics. Across the three studies that reported on other disorders, the average reported prevalence was 1.15 percent for bulimia nervosa, whereas zero cases of anorexia nervosa were identified across the examined pediatric cohorts.
These collective findings led the researchers to conclude that clinicians providing pediatric obesity care should actively evaluate young patients for binge eating disorder alongside standard obesity care protocols. Because six percent of youth with obesity may carry a concurrent binge eating disorder, addressing these conditions in parallel is critical during clinical consultations.
The investigation also brought to light the remarkably low number of empirical studies examining eating disorders in their entirety within this pediatric population. With few research teams investigating disorders beyond binge eating disorder, the authors noted that further systematic research is urgently required to clarify the true prevalence of all eating disorder types among children and adolescents living with obesity.
In addition, the authors underscored the need to develop age-appropriate diagnostic interviews specifically tailored for young people living with obesity, alongside reaching a formal medical consensus on which interview instrument should be standardly used for diagnosis. Finally, they called for increased awareness among clinicians regarding the double burden of eating disorders and obesity, emphasizing that both conditions require simultaneous screening and coordinated treatment.
