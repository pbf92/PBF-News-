TITLE: Palbociclib in Low- and Middle-Income Countries: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Generic Bioequivalence and Real-World Clinical Outcomes
OUTLET: CancerNetwork

Palbociclib in Low- and Middle-Income Countries: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Generic Bioequivalence and Real-World Clinical Outcomes
Generic palbociclib met regulatory bioequivalence criteria, and real-world first-line palbociclib-based therapy in low- and middle-income countries was associated with a pooled median progression-free survival of 19.9 months.
Introduction
Hormone receptor–positive, HER2-negative breast cancer accounts for approximately 70% of all breast cancers.1 Disease progression is driven by dysregulation of CDK4/6, leading to uncontrolled tumor proliferation.2,3 Historically, endocrine therapy (ET) has been the standard treatment. Many patients on ET develop intrinsic or acquired endocrine resistance, resulting in disease progression and poor survival outcomes.1
The introduction of CDK4/6 inhibitors, including palbociclib, has changed treatment outcomes in hormone receptor–positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer.2,4 Clinical trials showed that adding palbociclib to ET significantly improved progression-free survival (PFS). This treatment is still underused in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) because of high drug costs, limited insurance coverage, weak health care infrastructure, and the heavy financial burden on patients and health systems.4,5
Patients in LMICs often have more advanced disease than those enrolled in the major Western trials, with greater visceral involvement and more prior chemotherapy. This has been seen in India3,5 and China,2 and later-stage diagnosis has been reported in Brazil, particularly among publicly insured patients.6 To help close this access gap, generic palbociclib is now available in some LMICs.5 It costs much less and may allow more patients to receive CDK4/6 inhibitors.
Significant concerns persist regarding pharmacokinetic bioequivalence (PKB) of generic palbociclib under varying gastric pH conditions, a relevant issue because palbociclib absorption is pH dependent and acid-reducing agents such as proton pump inhibitors are widely used.7 Critical unanswered questions remain: (1) Do generic and innovator palbociclib formulations demonstrate pharmacokinetic equivalence in controlled trials? (2) What are the real-world clinical outcomes of palbociclib therapy in LMIC populations stratified by treatment line? (3) Do regulatory bioequivalence parameters translate to equivalent clinical outcomes in actual patient populations receiving generic formulations?
This systematic review and meta-analysis has 3 aims, as follows:
Aim 1: PKB. Evaluate bioequivalence of generic vs innovator palbociclib in randomized crossover bioequivalence trials in healthy volunteers, with 90% CIs of geometric mean ratios (GMRs) for maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) and area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) within the regulatory range (80%-125%).
Aim 2: Real-world clinical efficacy. Assess efficacy of palbociclib-based therapy in patients with hormone receptor–positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer in LMIC settings through median PFS and HR, stratified by treatment line.
Aim 3: PKB-to-clinical translation and generic equivalence in patient populations. Examine whether PKB translates to equivalent PFS and whether regulatory PKB (80% to 125% GMR) predicts equivalent outcomes (noninferiority defined as an upper 95% confidence limit of the HR for generic vs innovator palbociclib of ≤ 1.20).
Secondary aims include: (1) determining objective response rates (ORRs) and clinical benefit rates (CBRs) across treatment lines, (2) quantifying grade 3 and 4 adverse events with focus on neutropenia incidence, (3) assessing the impact of dose reductions on PFS outcomes, and (4) examining clinical outcomes across treatment line, metastatic site, and endocrine sensitivity subgroups.
Exploratory comparative effectiveness of palbociclib vs other CDK4/6 inhibitors in available LMIC cohorts is presented for contextual information but does not form part of the primary research objectives. Where the available studies could not answer an aim (the HR comparison in Aim 2 and the clinical comparison in Aim 3), the review reports this as an evidence gap.
Methods
Study Registration and Reporting Standards
This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines8 and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261392204).
Study Eligibility and PICOTS Framework
Population
Evidence Stream 1 for the PKB analysis consisted of healthy volunteers (≥ 18 years) in randomized crossover bioequivalence trials. Evidence Stream 2 for the clinical efficacy analysis consisted of adults (≥ 18 years) with confirmed hormone receptor–positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer in LMIC settings.
Intervention and Comparators
Primary Comparisons: (Aim 1) generic vs innovator palbociclib in healthy volunteers; (Aim 2) palbociclib plus ET vs ET alone in patient populations; (Aim 3) generic-recipient patients vs innovator-recipient patients (stratified from real-world studies)
Secondary Comparisons: Palbociclib vs other CDK4/6 inhibitors (exploratory only); first-line vs later-line palbociclib use (prespecified subgroup)
Outcomes
Primary Outcomes: Aim 1 (PKB): Bioequivalence formally demonstrated if 90% CI of GMR for Cmax and AUC fall within 80% to 125% regulatory acceptance range (US Food and Drug Administration [FDA]/European Medicines Agency [EMA] standards)
Aim 2 (Clinical Efficacy): Median PFS in patients treated with first-line palbociclib plus ET, stratified by treatment line, and HRs with 95% CI for palbociclib plus ET vs ET alone
Aim 3 (PKB-to-Clinical Translation): PFS in generic-recipient vs innovator-recipient patients, expressed as the HR for generic vs innovator palbociclib, with noninferiority defined as an upper 95% confidence limit of the HR of ≤ 1.20
Secondary Outcomes: ORR; CBR; grade 3 to 4 neutropenia incidence; impact of dose reductions on PFS outcomes; treatment line–specific outcomes; outcome stratification by metastatic site
Study Types and Geographic Scope
Evidence Stream 1: Randomized, open-label crossover bioequivalence trials in healthy volunteers
Evidence Stream 2: Prospective and retrospective observational cohorts in LMIC patient populations
Geographic Scope: Studies conducted in LMIC settings as defined by World Bank classification (2025),9 excluding high-income countries
Timeline: Published studies through April 28, 2026
Language: English-language publications only
Formulation Specification
Preference was given to studies explicitly documenting palbociclib formulation type (generic or innovator). Studies without explicit formulation documentation were included for Aim 2 (clinical efficacy patterns) but excluded from Aim 3 analysis (generic vs innovator comparison).
Exclusion Criteria
Studies were excluded if they: (1) had exclusively hormone receptor–negative/HER2-positive populations without hormone receptor–positive/HER2-negative stratification; (2) were case reports or case series with fewer than 10 patients, editorials, or narrative reviews; (3) were conducted only in high-income countries; (4) had insufficient clinical or pharmacokinetic outcome data for extraction (for Evidence Stream 2, at least median PFS, a PFS rate at a fixed time point, or graded adverse events for patients with hormone receptor–positive/HER2-negative disease treated with palbociclib were required); or (5) were duplicate publications.
Information Sources and Search Strategy
PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane Library were systematically searched through April 28, 2026, using search terms “palbociclib,” “bioequivalence,” “generic,” “real-world,” “metastatic breast cancer,” and “low- and middle-income countries.” The complete search strategy and database-specific strings are provided in Supplementary File. Reference lists were manually reviewed, and corresponding authors were contacted for missing or unpublished data.
Study Selection and Data Management
Two independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts, and full texts using predefined eligibility criteria. References were deduplicated using Excel and managed with study management software. Conflicts in study selection were resolved through team discussion or senior reviewer consultation.
Data Extraction
A standardized, piloted extraction form collected the following: study characteristics (design, country, publication year, funding source), population demographics (age, menopausal status, prior therapy), intervention details (palbociclib formulation type, dosing, coadministered endocrine therapy), clinical outcomes (PFS, ORR, CBR, duration of follow-up), pharmacokinetic parameters (Cmax, AUC, GMR, 90% CI), safety data (grade 3-4 adverse events, discontinuation rates), and subgroup data (treatment line, metastatic site, endocrine sensitivity). Median PFS was extracted with its 95% CI where reported, overall and by treatment line; for cohorts that included other CDK4/6 inhibitors, palbociclib-specific data were extracted when reported.
Risk of Bias and Evidence Quality Assessment
Bias Assessment by Study Type
Randomized crossover bioequivalence trials (PKB studies) were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool.10
Observational cohorts (clinical efficacy) were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS),11 with predefined thresholds for quality scoring (good, ≥ 7 stars; fair, 5 or 6 stars; poor, ≤ 4 stars). Because all cohorts were single arm for palbociclib, the NOS item for selection of the nonexposed cohort was not applicable and was scored 0 for every cohort, so the maximum attainable score was 8 stars; the 2 comparability stars were awarded when the cohort reported a multivariable (Cox) analysis adjusting for key prognostic factors. The prespecified thresholds were applied unchanged. Ratings for each cohort are given in Supplementary Table S1.
Certainty of Evidence
Overall certainty of evidence for each primary outcome was graded using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology,12 incorporating risk of bias, inconsistency (heterogeneity), indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Because the clinical efficacy outcomes are single-group estimates (median PFS and event rates) rather than comparative effects, they were rated with the GRADE approach for prognostic and event-rate estimates, in which evidence from observational cohorts starts at high certainty before downgrading.13 Summary of findings tables are provided in the Supplementary File. Two independent reviewers assessed all bias domains, with disagreements resolved through consensus.
Statistical Analysis
Aim 1: Pharmacokinetic Bioequivalence
Bioequivalence was defined as 90% CI of GMR for Cmax and AUC within 80% to 125%, consistent with FDA and EMA regulatory standards. Descriptive tabulation of PKB results from both crossover bioequivalence trials is presented; formal meta-analysis was not performed due to only 2 PKB studies. Data were qualitatively reviewed for potential sources of variability stratified by fasting vs fed conditions, and normal vs modified gastric pH (relevant to acid-reducing agent use).
Aim 2: Real-World Clinical Efficacy
Meta-analysis approach: Random-effects inverse-variance models accounted for heterogeneity. Median PFS was pooled on the log scale for time-to-event data.14 When 95% confidence limits (L, U) were reported, the SE of ln(median PFS) was calculated as [ln(U) − ln(L)]/(2 × 1.96). If the CI was incomplete or excluded the median, SE was approximated as 1/√(n/2). This asymptotic SE for a log median under an exponential survival model assumes half of patients progressed (d ≈ n/2 events); it can underestimate SE with short follow-up or heavy censoring, so sensitivity analyses tested effect (see Sensitivity and Publication Bias Analyses).
