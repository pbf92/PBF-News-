TITLE: Elevated blood sugar levels over decades linked to worse midlife memory, cognitive function
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Research Highlights:
- A study of young adults without Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes with increasing fasting blood sugar levels over 3 decades found that they were more likely to have poor cognitive functioning by midlife.
- Participants who developed Type 2 diabetes over the three decades also had signs of structural brain changes by midlife.
- The findings suggest early monitoring and management of blood sugar levels in young adulthood (18 to 30 years old) might be a strategy to preserve brain health, researchers said.
Embargoed until 4 a.m. CT/5 a.m. ET Weds., Nov. 25, 2026
DALLAS, Nov. 25, 2026 — Young adults with a progressive rise in fasting blood sugar levels over multiple decades were more likely to have worse cognitive health by midlife. Participants who developed Type 2 diabetes during the study also had signs of structural brain changes, according to new, independent research published today in the Journal of the American Heart Association, an open-access, peer-reviewed journal of the American Heart Association.
“Diabetes is known to affect cognitive function, but cognitive impairment due to diabetes was once believed to happen mostly during childhood among people with Type 1 diabetes or after age 65 in people with Type 2 diabetes,” said lead study author Regina Paradela, Ph.D., a post-doctoral researcher at the University of São Paulo Medical School in São Paulo, Brazil, and a neuroepidemiologist at the Global Brain Health Institute at the University of California, San Francisco. “However, prediabetes and sustained elevated fasting blood sugar levels in adults without Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes may also have an increased risk of late-life cognitive decline and dementia.”
This study is among the first to investigate and compare the association between changes in fasting blood sugar levels from early adulthood to changes in cognition and brain structure in middle age, over approximately 30 years. Normal fasting blood glucose is generally below 100 mg/dL; 100–125 mg/dL indicates prediabetes; and ≥126 mg/dL is consistent with Type 2 diabetes.
“The fact that increasing glucose levels were associated with poorer cognitive performance, even among people who had not developed Type 2 diabetes during the 30-year study, is very important,” Paradela said. “This suggests that changes in glucose metabolism may be relevant to brain health earlier in adulthood than we typically think about.”
Researchers analyzed health data for nearly 3,000 participants in the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study, which enrolled Black and white adults ages 18 to 30 in four U.S. cities from 1985 to 1986. Participants had three different fasting blood sugar (glucose) patterns of change over the next 30 years and at least one cognitive test at the 30-year mark that examined memory, processing speed, executive function, verbal fluency and overall cognition. A subset of participants also received MRI brain scans to measure gray matter volume and abnormal white matter tissue.
What are the study’s main findings?
- Researchers identified three fasting blood sugar trajectories from young adulthood to midlife: lower-stable glucose (blood sugar), moderate-stable glucose and increasing glucose.
- After adjusting for demographic and lifestyle factors in adults without diabetes, researchers found that those with increasing glucose levels and those who developed Type 2 diabetes scored worse on tests of memory, processing speed, executive function and a combined cognitive measure, compared with people with lower-stable glucose levels.
- MRI data found that compared to the lower-stable glucose group, those who had Type 2 diabetes had less total gray matter and more abnormal white matter tissue in their brain, which is associated with lower cognitive function in other studies.
- Additionally, the participants who developed Type 2 diabetes had lower cognitive test results, and the MRI images showed signs of structural changes in their brains.
Elisabeth Marsh, M.D., FAHA, chair of the writing group for the American Heart Association’s 2026 scientific statement, Brain Health Across the Life Span: A Framework for Future Studies, said, “The number of older adults is increasing around the world, so it’s important to understand what helps keep our brains healthy as we age. This study examined how glucose levels and Type 2 diabetes can affect brain health throughout our lives. The American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 offers steps to improve heart and brain health and support cognitive well-being at every stage of life.” Marsh, who was not involved in this study, is a professor of neurology and associate director of the neurology residency program at The Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine and director of the Stroke Center at Johns Hopkins Bayview Medical Center in Baltimore.
“Future research should evaluate whether monitoring and managing increasing glucose levels earlier in adulthood can prevent or delay cognitive decline and structural brain changes,” said study senior author, Kristine Yaffe, M.D., professor of psychiatry, neurology and epidemiology, and director of the Center for Population Brain Health at the University of California, San Francisco.

Study details, background, design and limitations:
- The analysis included 2,808 participants in the CARDIA study (56% women; 44% Black adults), whose average age at the 30-year visit was 55 years. MRI data was available for 605 participants.
- Among 2,463 participants who did not develop Type 2 diabetes, 56% had lower-stable glucose, defined as ~ 80-85 mg/dL; 38% had moderate-stable glucose, defined as ~ 90–100 mg/dL; and 5.6% had glucose that increased over time (starting at 85 mg/dL and increasing up to 110–115 mg/dL).
- Another 345 participants developed Type 2 diabetes during the 30-year follow-up.
- Blood sugar was measured at baseline and at years 7, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30.
- Study limitations include that cognitive function was assessed only at year 30, so cognitive decline over time could not be measured. Also, MRI data were available only for a small subset of participants (605), which limited the statistical power for the association between rising glucose and structural brain changes within the small increasing-glucose group.
