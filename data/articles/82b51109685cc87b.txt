TITLE: Ondato's selfie age check wins certification for New York's kids law
OUTLET: PPC Land

Ondato, a London-based identity verification company, said on November 24, 2026, that its facial age estimation method has been certified as meeting the accuracy requirements of New York's Stop Addictive Feeds Exploitation (SAFE) for Kids Act, 62 days before the rules under that law take effect on January 25, 2027. The certificate was awarded by the Age Check Certification Scheme (ACCS), a UK body that audits age-check technology. According to Ondato, ACCS named it among the first eight providers whose methods meet the Act's requirements.
In Short
Ondato says its selfie-based age check has been independently certified as accurate enough for New York's new rules on children's social media feeds. From January 25, 2027, large social media platforms must establish that a New York user is an adult before showing a personalised feed or sending overnight alerts about it, unless a parent consents. At least two vendors, Ondato and Veratad, now say their methods have passed, but neither has published the error rates behind the certificate, and no public list of certified methods has appeared.
What was certified
The certificate covers one product: Ondato's facial age estimation, which guesses a person's age from a photo of their face. According to Ondato, ACCS "audited and certified" the method "as aligned with the Act's requirements." The release is datelined London, November 24, 2026.
The Act requires platforms to offer at least one way to check age that does not depend on a government-issued ID. A selfie-based estimate is one such option, and Ondato positions the certification as giving social media platforms "a verified option" for that requirement. Ondato also sells document-based age verification, which it says can be set up as a fallback. The release does not say that the document method was part of this certification.
The New York certificate is not Ondato's first in the field. According to the company, it already holds independent age assurance certifications under ISO/IEC 27566-1:2025 and IEEE 2089.1-2024, the two main technical standards for age checks. Its age estimation algorithms have also taken part in testing by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), under the agency's Face Analysis Technology Evaluation (FATE) programme. The release gives no NIST results.
Dmitrij Olifer, Chief Information Security and Product Compliance Officer at Ondato, framed the New York rules as a turning point. "For years, the debate around age checks has been about whether they work at all. New York's SAFE for Kids Act has changed that by setting clear requirements and asking providers to prove that their methods meet them," he said. "That is good for children, and it's good for platforms, which now have an objective benchmark for one of their most important compliance decisions. Independent testing and certification against standards should be the baseline for this industry, and we expect other states and regulators to follow New York's lead."
Ondato was founded in 2018, has its headquarters in London and runs research and development in Vilnius, Lithuania. It says it has onboarded more than 70 million users and served clients in more than 200 jurisdictions. Its main customers are regulated businesses such as neobanks, payment providers, social media and marketplace platforms. The company said North America is set to become a strategic priority in 2027.
What New York's law restricts
The SAFE for Kids Act does not ban children from social media. It targets two features. Covered platforms may not show users under 18 an algorithmically personalised feed - the stream of posts a platform picks for each person based on their behaviour - unless a parent has given verifiable consent. And without that consent, they may not send those users notifications about such feeds between midnight and 6 a.m. Eastern Time.
To apply either rule, a platform first has to know who is a minor. That is where age checks come in. According to the Act's implementing rules, a platform must complete an age check showing that a user is not a minor before it switches on those features, unless it has parental consent.
New York Attorney General Letitia James and Governor Kathy Hochul released the final rules on July 28, 2026. They were published in the State Register on July 29, and Section 700.11 says they take effect "on the 180th day after publication in the State Register" - January 25, 2027. PPC Land noted that date when it covered Hochul's separate law on AI chatbots and minors, signed on October 28, 2026, whose age obligations start 24 days earlier.
Not every platform is covered. The rules apply to an "addictive online platform," meaning one where personalised feeds are a significant part of the service: at least 20% of monthly active users' time spent on those feeds, measured over a six-month period in the prior year. Platforms with "fewer than 5 million monthly active users or fewer than 20,000 monthly active users who are covered minors" are exempt, unless their main user base is minors. The attorney general can seek civil penalties of up to $5,000 per violation.
The error limits
What sets New York apart, according to Ondato, is that the rules put numbers on how often an age check may get it wrong. The key error is a "false positive": a child judged to be an adult, and so given the adult version of the platform. The permitted rate shrinks sharply for younger children.
Source: New York SAFE for Kids Act rules, Section 700.1(b). The 98% figure is a detection rate, not an error rate.
In plain terms, a method may treat no more than one in 1,000 children aged seven or under as adults, and no more than about one in seven 17-year-olds. The rules leave out cases where a user refuses to take part or the result is inconclusive. A second measure, the "Total Accuracy Minimum" in Section 700.1(gg), applies the same age limits but counts inconclusive results too.
The cheating rule reads: "a rate of detecting method circumvention for an age assurance method that meets or exceeds 98%." The rules define method circumvention as "submission of false data or interference with an age assurance method" - for example, holding up a printed photo or a video of an older person.
Other rules on age checks
The accuracy limits sit inside a wider set of requirements. According to the final rules and Ondato's summary:
- No-ID option. Platforms must "provide at least one age assurance method that does not require the furnishing of government-provided identification." Two exceptions apply: where the platform already has to collect ID under other laws, or where it offers a zero-knowledge proof method, which confirms an age without revealing the underlying data.
- Yearly certificate. "A covered operator must obtain a certification annually for each age assurance method it offers," according to Section 700.5(a). The certifier must be an accredited third party recognised by the American National Standards Institute "or equivalent accreditation body."
- Test reports. Each certification needs a written report with the test protocols and all results, including false positives, inconclusive outcomes, false negatives (adults wrongly flagged as minors) for ages 18 to 30, cheating detection, data handling and encryption. The method "must not have been trained or tuned on the testing dataset or any substantially overlapping dataset." Test records must be kept for at least 10 years.
- Appeals. Users classified as minors can appeal, with at least one option that does not require government ID. Platforms must respond or ask for more information within 10 business days and give a written summary of the decision.
- Data limits. Age-check data must be "the minimum necessary," used only for compliance and deleted once no longer needed. Certain records, such as the age status reached and monthly counts, must be kept for at least five years. An estimated age may be kept only with the user's consent.
How ACCS certifies a method
ACCS set out its approach in notice CPN-2026-01, "Implementation of the New York SAFE for Kids Act - Certification of Age Assurance Methods," issued on July 21, 2026, a week before the final rules were released. According to the notice, certification checks the following, as applicable:
- Conformity with ISO/IEC 27566:2025, IEEE 2089.1-2024 or an equivalent industry standard.
- That the method meets the statutory accuracy limits.
- That testing meets Section 700.5(b) of the rules.
- A review of supporting technical evidence.
- An evaluation of governance, quality management and change control arrangements.
- Confirmation that independent testing has been carried out.
- Verification that the certified scope "accurately reflects the deployed age assurance method."
At the time, ACCS said detailed guidance, assessment criteria and testing requirements would follow formal publication of the final regulations, and that "certification cannot be guaranteed."
Ondato describes ACCS as "an independent conformity assessment body for age assurance." ACCS's own website describes it as a not-for-profit company limited by guarantee, registered in England and Wales, and as accredited by UKAS, the UK's national accreditation body. In its New York notice, ACCS calls itself the only accredited body currently offering certification against ISO/IEC 27566 and IEEE 2089.1.
How Ondato's age check works
According to Ondato, its method runs in five stages:
- The user takes a selfie.
- The system checks image quality and runs liveness detection and anti-spoofing checks, which test whether a live person is in front of the camera rather than a photo, mask or replayed video.
- The system returns either an estimated age or a yes-or-no answer against an age threshold, with a confidence level, depending on how the platform has set up the integration. Ondato says this typically takes seconds and does not ask the user for other personal details.
- Where the result is "insufficient or inconclusive," a document-based check can be configured as a fallback.
- Age-check data is collected only as far as necessary, used for the compliance purpose and deleted when no longer required, subject to legal retention duties. Ondato says the design supports data minimisation.
Face-based estimates are less precise near the adult line than a document check. On one benchmark dataset, the best algorithms tested by NIST in 2024 were still off by about three years on average, which is why many deployments send anyone who appears to be under 25 to a further check. New York's rules reflect that by allowing more error at 16 and 17 than at younger ages. Ondato has not said what error rates its method achieved in the certification tests, or which age thresholds were tested.
The "first eight" claim
Ondato's statement that ACCS named it "among the first eight providers" cannot be checked against a public ACCS list. ACCS's public registry, reviewed on November 24, has categories for age estimation, age verification and other services, but no separate New York category, and the ACCS pages reviewed give no count of methods certified against the SAFE for Kids Act.
Ondato is not alone in making the claim. On November 16, 2026, identity verification company Veratad said that ACCS had certified two methods on its VX platform against New York's requirements: facial age estimation and a check that matches a government-issued ID to a selfie. Veratad also described itself as among the first providers. Neither company named the other, and neither published accuracy figures.
That leaves platforms with vendor announcements rather than a public register. Under the rules, the duty to hold a yearly certificate for each method sits with the platform, not the vendor. The release does not say how a platform would rely on Ondato's certificate for its own deployment.
