TITLE: Experts Urge Implementation of Known Interventions to Reduce Global Pre-Eclampsia Deaths
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

In an expert Perspective published in PLOS Medicine, maternal health researchers argue that stagnating global progress against maternal mortality demands a decisive pivot from discovery-driven research toward implementation science, particularly for pre-eclampsia. Led by Dr. Alice Beardmore-Gray and colleagues from King’s College London and Stellenbosch University, the authors emphasize that pre-eclampsia remains a leading global cause of death among pregnant women and neonates. Although laboratory investigations continue to seek foundational biological insights and novel therapeutics, the authors point out that proven, cost-effective clinical measures already exist. These existing interventions, most notably routine antenatal surveillance and planned early birth, could avert tens of thousands of deaths annually if health systems successfully delivered them to the populations carrying the highest disease burden.
The scale of the maternal health crisis remains severe, with preventable pregnancy complications claiming approximately 800 women’s lives every day according to 2023 figures. Strikingly, 92 percent of these maternal deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, highlighting stark geographic and socio-economic inequities in health outcomes. Despite this concentrated toll, funding allocations fail to reflect the burden: data from the Global Observatory on Health Research and Development revealed that in 2023, low- and middle-income countries received a mere 0.2 percent of awarded research grants. Furthermore, all maternal health conditions combined accounted for only 0.32 percent of global annual research grant allocations, leaving critical areas of obstetric care chronically under-resourced on the international stage.
However, the allocation of these financial resources reveals a pronounced imbalance between laboratory discovery and frontline implementation.
This misallocation occurs against an estimated annual toll of 47,000 maternal deaths and 500,000 perinatal deaths directly attributable to pre-eclampsia. The authors highlight that historic progress in high-income nations demonstrates that reducing this toll does not hinge on awaiting novel pharmaceutical discoveries. In the United Kingdom, maternal mortality from pre-eclampsia plummeted to one in a million women by 2016, achieved not through a singular cure, but through the nationwide institutionalization of universal antenatal care, systematic clinical protocols including blood pressure control, fluid management, seizure prophylaxis using magnesium sulfate, and the strategic timing of planned early birth.
In sharp contrast, healthcare settings in regions facing the highest global burden—most notably sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia—continue to lack reliable access to basic obstetric tools and fundamental therapies. Although the World Health Organization added magnesium sulfate to its Model List of Essential Medicines in 1996 for the prevention and management of eclamptic seizures, the drug remains underutilized and inconsistently stocked across resource-constrained clinics. Essential diagnostic supplies, such as functional blood pressure monitors, urine protein dipsticks, and basic laboratory reagents, are frequently absent, causing women to present to health facilities late in the progression of severe disease when opportunities for early risk modification have passed.
Reviewing prevention and risk-reduction strategies, the authors evaluate recent systematic evidence regarding common pharmacological interventions. An updated Cochrane review demonstrated that calcium supplementation offers little to no meaningful difference in reducing the incidence of pre-eclampsia, composite maternal mortality, severe morbidity, or stillbirth. A 2019 Cochrane systematic review of low-dose aspirin established a small-to-moderate preventative effect, documenting an 18 percent reduction in the overall relative risk of pre-eclampsia and five fewer fetal or neonatal deaths per 1,000 treated women. However, the clinical utility of aspirin is largely confined to preventing preterm pre-eclampsia, whereas the vast majority of morbidity and mortality worldwide occurs in women who reach late preterm or full term gestations.
Similarly, standard acute therapeutics such as magnesium sulfate and anti-hypertensive drugs effectively control severe blood pressure and reduce seizures, yet evidence shows that these pharmacotherapies alone do not reverse disease pathophysiology or lower overall maternal mortality without timely delivery. While promising biological candidates exist—such as extended-release metformin, which prolonged gestation in initial preterm trials, and preclinical messenger RNA platforms targeting soluble Flt-1—these experimental approaches remain in early developmental stages. The authors caution that complex, high-technology biological therapies are unlikely to achieve widespread, equitable availability in fragile healthcare settings in the near future.
Consequently, planned early birth remains the only established, definitive cure that reliably halts the progression of pre-eclampsia and prevents fatal maternal complications. An updated 2026 Cochrane systematic review assessing planned early birth from 34 weeks’ gestation onwards across both high-income countries and low- and middle-income countries produced high-certainty evidence of its effectiveness. Initiating birth via labor induction or indicated cesarean delivery nearly halved serious maternal complications without increasing overall cesarean section rates, healthcare resource use, or total treatment costs. Furthermore, planned delivery reduced stillbirth rates by approximately 75 percent and reduced the proportion of infants born small for gestational age, without causing a statistically significant rise in neonatal intensive care admissions.
Because clinical risks do not resolve immediately upon delivery, comprehensive management requires structured postnatal surveillance to detect late-onset maternal and infant complications. The authors advocate for integrating planned birth into a standardized, multicomponent pre-eclampsia care pathway, drawing parallels to successful trials in other critical areas of maternal survival. Notably, the E-MOTIVE trial for postpartum hemorrhage and the APT-Sepsis trial for maternal sepsis demonstrated that packaged, multicomponent clinical care bundles achieve profound reductions in maternal mortality by streamlining identification and clinical response, even when the individual therapeutic components are already established and widely recognized.
The perspective concludes that successfully deploying a pre-eclampsia care pathway requires national and global commitments to health system strengthening rather than incremental tweaks to existing drug regimens. Sustained investment is needed to ensure reliable supplies of diagnostic tools, skilled obstetric staff, and dependable emergency referral networks, paired with community education and maternal decision-support resources. Re-focusing funding toward the rigorous evaluation, adaptation, and universal delivery of established clinical interventions represents the most reliable path to ending preventable pre-eclampsia deaths across the globe.
Crucial to refining clinical decision-making is the development of practical diagnostic instruments that can reliably pinpoint which pregnant women require escalated surveillance and early delivery. Point-of-care biomarker tests are currently undergoing clinical evaluation in resource-constrained settings to address this diagnostic hurdle. Should these point-of-care evaluations demonstrate measurable improvements in maternal and perinatal outcomes, future research must examine how to equitably incorporate the technologies into routine healthcare pathways, with careful attention paid to cost-effectiveness and operational sustainability. The authors specifically emphasize the value of targeted programs, welcoming initiatives such as the FIGO pregnancy passport to improve maternal postnatal follow-up and the PremPrep-5 initiative to mitigate preterm neonatal mortality.
Beyond expanding diagnostic precision, the authors call for a fundamental reassessment of research priorities to minimize research waste. Rather than dedicating scarce funding to marginal adjustments of established interventions, such as trials evaluating subtle variations in aspirin dosage, the exact timing of aspirin cessation, or alternative administration routes for magnesium sulfate, investigators should focus on implementation and scale-up. The authors point out that such minor clinical adjustments offer minimal therapeutic gains compared to the massive reductions in mortality achievable by ensuring universal frontline adoption of proven treatments. To translate this principle into practice, national governments, charitable bodies, and global health agencies must commit logistical and financial support toward establishing a coordinated operational framework. This strategic approach mirrors the comprehensive roadmap frameworks previously organized by international health authorities to tackle other primary contributors to maternal death, including postpartum hemorrhage.
This call to elevate delivery above laboratory discovery reflects a long-standing yet unfulfilled scientific consensus. Two decades ago, in 2006, a landmark analysis by Campbell and colleagues demonstrated that nearly two-thirds of the global disease burden affecting mothers and neonates could be averted through the effective adaptation and widespread implementation of existing medical findings. At the time, researchers urged the global health community to focus on implementing what is already proven to work. Twenty years later, with worldwide reductions in maternal mortality having stalled over the past five years, the authors argue that failing to deliver proven interventions remains an ongoing implementation failure.
Ultimately, while laboratory research into novel therapeutics and biological pathways must continue, the authors contend that an elusive panacea is unnecessary to achieve immediate reductions in preventable loss of life. Each component of the proposed pre-eclampsia care pathway is inherently simple and low-cost, yet realizing its life-saving potential requires a concerted national commitment to health systems strengthening, especially across fragile states. By equipping frontline health workers with functional tools, establishing reliable clinical referral systems, and providing educational decision-support aids to pregnant women and their families, global healthcare systems can finally deliver planned early birth to the right patients at the optimal time.
