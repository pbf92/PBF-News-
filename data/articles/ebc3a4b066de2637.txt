TITLE: Fewer Young Women Are Using The Pill As Hormonal Hesitancy Rises: Lancet
OUTLET: Times Now

The contraceptive pill has long been a widely used method of birth control, but a growing number of young women in Great Britain are moving away from hormonal contraception. A new study published in The Lancet has highlighted this shift, raising questions about the influence of hormone-related fears, online misinformation and access to reproductive healthcare.
The findings come from the fourth National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal-4), which examined reproductive health trends between 2010 and 2024. The survey included almost 7,000 people aged 16–59 across England, Scotland and Wales. Researchers found that the proportion of women aged 16–24 using hormonal contraception fell from 64.6% to 50.3% over the past decade. The decline was also observed among women under 35, suggesting that younger generations are increasingly reconsidering their contraceptive choices.
The findings come amid growing concerns about what researchers and healthcare professionals describe as “hormonal hesitancy,” a reluctance to use hormonal contraceptives, including the pill, implants and injections.
What Is Driving The Shift?
Concerns about potential side effects, including mood changes, weight gain and effects on future fertility, have become increasingly prominent in online discussions about contraception. Healthcare professionals have also raised concerns about social media content promoting natural or hormone-free methods while portraying synthetic hormones as harmful. Some online claims suggest that hormonal contraception can cause lasting damage to fertility, despite evidence that fertility generally returns after stopping most hormonal methods.

Fertility-tracking apps and other natural approaches are also being promoted as alternatives. However, these methods can be less reliable than many established contraceptive options when used to prevent pregnancy. The survey itself does not establish why women are changing their contraceptive choices. Experts have suggested that limited access to sexual health services and gaps in sex education may leave women more vulnerable to misleading online information.
Why The Findings Matter
The study found that nearly 1 in 5 women in Great Britain at risk of unintended pregnancy were not using effective contraception. However, the decline in hormonal contraception does not mean women are abandoning birth control altogether. Use of the hormonal coil increased from 4% to 10% across all age groups, showing that contraceptive choices are also changing. Researchers have highlighted the need for accessible reproductive healthcare and reliable information to help women make informed decisions.
The findings also underline the importance of recognising that contraceptive needs and experiences differ. Hormonal contraception can cause side effects for some women, while others use it without significant problems. Rather than dismissing concerns or encouraging women to avoid hormonal methods, healthcare professionals say women should have access to balanced advice and a range of contraceptive options that suit their individual needs. The central concern is not that women are choosing differently, but whether they have the information and support needed to make those choices safely.
