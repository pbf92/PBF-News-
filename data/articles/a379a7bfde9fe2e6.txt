The US space company SpaceX has achieved a milestone by safely catching the upper stage of its Starship spacecraft as it returned to Earth following a 22-hour orbit. As it descended, the rocket was gently plucked from the air by two ‘chopstick’ arms of its giant launch tower. The move is an important milestone on the path to someday making Starship reusable. The lower stage faced a more prosaic end: its journey was aborted and it blew itself up above the Gulf of Mexico.

Researchers have used an artificial-intelligence model called GPT-6 Astra to read and tinker with the inner workings of another AI network. The team found that Astra can interpret the raw internal values of some other models well enough to predict what different parts of the systems do — and make tweaks to change their abilities. The results don’t mean that Astra can explain or understand how a given AI network works, but suggest that general-purpose models might be able to modify a network in useful ways.

There is growing evidence that menopause is linked to the risk of Alzheimer’s disease in women, who make up two-thirds of those diagnosed with the disease. What’s less clear is whether hormone therapy helps to reduce the risk. Research presented at the Society for Neuroscience’s annual meeting suggests that hormone treatments can make a difference, but how much depends on genetic factors and the specific formulations used. “It seems that midlife is this critical window,” says neuroscientist Stephanie Perez, who presented separate work in rats showing how hormones affect brain aging.

A sharp drop in the number of international students arriving in the United States at the start of the academic year is squeezing already strained university budgets, and researchers worry about the impact on US science. Higher-education specialists link measures introduced by the administration of President Donald Trump — including restrictions on travel and visas, and a potential levy on post-graduate training — to the large decrease in newly arrived overseas students, who, they say, can no longer count a US education as a stable investment. (Nature | 5 min read)
The drop is partly driven by a decrease in the number of students moving to the US from China, who are increasingly choosing to pursue postgraduate opportunities within Asia instead. (Nature | 7 min read)

In The Last Animal, historian Kyle Harper offers a continuous account of biodiversity from prehistoric times to present day to ask: how have humans affected Earth’s animal communities? Harper argues that only by zooming out can we truly appreciate how we’ve reached the current biodiversity crisis — and how we might turn things around. “The book is a triumphant piece of scholarship,” write ecologist Elizabeth Blackmore and computational biologist C. Brandon Ogbunugafor in their review. “For scientists, it offers an opportunity for reflection.”
Is agriculture the key to the development of the state? Quite the contrary, argues Mongolian anthropologist Lhamsuren Munkh-Erdene. “Building a state does not require grain, but does demand extrahuman transportation,” he writes: particularly, on land, horses. “Extrahuman transportation made the state and civilisation possible; this places pastoralism at the origin of the state and civilisation, and the Eurasian steppe at the centre of human history.”
In the first of a three-part Nature Podcast series, host Nick Petrić Howe seizes the nettle of climate ‘doom and gloom’. Overwhelmed by the grim narrative, he set out on a mission to find out if there are any reasons left for optimism. In the first episode, he talks to experts around the world about what the data really show and finds some surprising beacons of hope.
