TITLE: YouTube's video tests will give creators seven days to pick a winning cut
OUTLET: PPC Land

YouTube today published a written account of how its planned video A/B testing tool will work: a test runs, YouTube reports results, and the creator gets seven days to decide which version of the video to keep. The post, on the YouTube Blog, recaps a conversation between the creator strategist Paddy Galloway and Amjad Hanif, YouTube's vice president of creator product, hosted by YouTube's creator liaison Rene Ritchie, in which Hanif said the company built the tests around retention curves rather than click-through rate because "that is the true insight from the A/B testing."
In Short
YouTube has explained in more detail how creators will be able to test different versions of the same video, starting with Shorts, and then choose within seven days which one to keep, guided by how well each held viewers. This matters to creators, and to the brands that sponsor them, because the opening seconds of a video, where sponsor mentions often sit, are the part creators are expected to test most. The same conversation also described tools that show different thumbnails to different viewers, refresh old videos and draft brand pitches with AI, but YouTube still has not said when most of them will arrive.

Seven days and a predicted winner
When video testing was first shown, its demo did not say how a test ends or what the creator does with the result. The September 27 report noted that the sources disagreed on scope, with the narration covering Shorts or long-form videos and the description pointing to Shorts alone.
The conversation settles both points, at least for the first release. Video A/B testing is introduced in the video as "Starting off with shorts, but this could let you test hooks. It could let you test story structures. It could let you test call to actions." Shorts are therefore where the feature begins, with no date given for longer videos.
The end of a test is described in one sentence: "the test is going to run. It'll give you a predicted winner and then you have seven days to to make your choice." Today's blog post puts the same rule in YouTube's own written voice: "Video A/B testing will run a test, provide results, and give the creator seven days to choose which edit to go with."
That is a different model from YouTube's existing title and thumbnail tests. When YouTube added titles to those tests in December 2025, the variant with the highest watch time per impression was applied automatically once the test ended, and if no winner emerged the first combination uploaded became the default. With video testing, YouTube names a predicted winner, but the creator makes the call.
How many versions can compete? The conversation refers to "the retention for the two the two or three different videos that you're testing", consistent with the limit of three cuts PPC Land reported in September. Each version gets its own curve. "You're going to get both retention curves," the video notes, and the participants suggested the comparison might not produce a clean winner at all. "And the real winner might be this hybrid and that's good," one of them said. "You know there might be two great moments from the two different variants and that's gives you feedback for the next video that you produce."
Retention, not clicks
Why not simply count clicks? Ritchie put the question in terms of thumbnail testing. "When you did thumbnail AB testing, you didn't center on CTR because it could encourage click baiting and then the retention would drop," he said, describing video testing as "giving creators like a fuller picture with with the analysis."
Hanif acknowledged that creators still want the number. "I know creators still ask for CTR on the thumbnail testing," he said. In the blog post's version of his answer: "When you have a sea of metrics, it's easy to get distracted by something that may not actually be in your interest as a creator. That's why we really focused on the retention curves, because that is the true insight from the A/B testing."
The point was taken further in the discussion with an observation about how click-through rate behaves as a video spreads. "Like a CTR is high because no one's watching like as virality increases CTR tends to go down," the video states. A test that shows which version people preferred, by contrast, "is like very actionable information very objective and it's hard to argue with um especially at the scale of YouTube and the viewers that are seeing those different variants so it's more than like a a test with 10 people."
YouTube has made the same choice before. The December 2025 title tests were also set up to optimise for watch time rather than click-through rate, as PPC Land reported at the time. Video testing applies that logic to the content itself.

The first 30 seconds
Asked how creators will use the tool, Hanif pointed to the opening. "I think a lot of that energy is going to be on that intro," he said. "I think creators probably that would be the number one thing that I've heard at least from creators is really testing different maybe the first 30 seconds." Different storylines and "maybe a different way of ending" were also on his list, "but I think for the most part it's going to be focused on that intro."
Galloway, who advises large channels, explained why the intro had been out of reach until now. He said he puts "so much emphasis on thumbnails, you know, with our clients because we can retroactively change it." With the video itself, "it's like we've uploaded it, there's very little we can actually do to test." The intro, in his ranking of what moves a video's performance, "is right up there."
His rule for intros is simple. "Your title thumbnail makes a promise. Your intro should deliver on it," he said. Clients often overload the opening, in his account, with "here's everything that's happening. We're going to do this. We're going to do that." He expected some creators to be daunted by the workload at first, and pointed to smaller variations. "I think maybe at first people would be a little bit intimidated with like whoa, I have to like make multiple videos now," he said. "But I think this is where it's important to think about the the subtleties of like testing different intros, testing different CTAs."
Podcasts came up as an obvious case. Galloway said podcasters often wonder: "should we do a a quick compilation of clips at the start or should we just start with the first question? Now we can actually test that." Hanif said a podcaster had told him earlier that they wanted to test "the trailer versus the non-trail intro."
Hanif expected the tool to puncture creator folklore, citing beliefs such as "oh, I think an intro has to be 23 seconds.5." He framed the benefit in time saved: "so much time gets invested in all these myths or things that you thought were made sense and this will help you just really focus on what actually moves the needle."
On-camera presence is one of those assumptions. "We also learned a lot from the early thumbnail testing is sometimes being on camera is really great," Hanif said, "but sometimes really just focusing on what the story is about and not about you, but about what you're talking about also hooks folks, particularly folks that aren't your core audience."
Galloway called himself someone who has "probably studied 10,000 plus retention curves like second by second", looking for patterns such as whether a brighter background holds viewers better than a darker one. "It's beautiful that now we can just do that with certainty," he said.
Three thumbnails, one viewer at a time
The second feature discussed in depth was dynamic thumbnails. Hanif described it plainly: "for dynamic thumbnails, you can upload three three thumbnails and based on what we know about the viewers, we will serve the best thumbnail for that particular viewer." The process is automatic. "You just have to give us three thumbnails."
The blog post puts it as going "beyond regular 'A/B' testing: you provide three thumbnails and they will be served to different audience segments automatically based on which one performs best for that group."
Ritchie offered his own reading. "One for me is segmenting by like new versus regular versus casual audience," he said. "Like maybe for my regular viewers, I'm going to have my face in it. Maybe for new viewers, I'm going to have like the gadget or the device in it because they don't really know or care who I am." Those three groups match the categories YouTube introduced to Studio analytics in July 2025, when it split returning viewers into casual viewers, who watch at least once a month for one to five months of the past year, and regular viewers, who do so for more than six.
Ritchie also pointed to "pocket audiences", niches that respond to one angle of a video, which creators have discovered through A/B tests. Galloway said this explained a pattern he had seen with clients. A thumbnail test would produce a result, and "a week later I'm like I really think the one that narrowly lost is actually might be a bit better with the video that or the audience that's being surfaced this video right now." A rerun could flip the outcome. In the blog's wording of his conclusion, "different audiences sometimes do require a different thumbnail."
Until now, the workaround was guesswork. "Generally speaking, in the first day, we might get a few more core viewers. So, I'm going to try this thumbnail for the first day," Galloway said. "But we all know you're still getting new viewers on your first day." His verdict: "It's always a good sign when you're adding things that like people like me have been trying to manufacture and do behind the scenes for years." Galloway made a similar point on the Made on YouTube post show, which PPC Land covered on October 11.
