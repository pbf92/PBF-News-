TITLE: The relationship between uric acid levels and fatigue: a systematic review and meta-analysis
OUTLET: Nature

Abstract
Uric acid (UA) plays a dual biological role as both a neuroprotective antioxidant and a pro-inflammatory pro-oxidant. Its relationship with fatigue across various clinical populations remains fragmented and controversial. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively synthesize clinical evidence regarding the association between serum uric acid (SUA) levels and fatigue. We searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane, PsycInfo, CNKI, and SinoMed up to August 29th, 2026 for observational studies on SUA and fatigue. Two reviewers screened, extracted data, and assessed quality (NOS). Pooled correlation coefficients (r) and odds ratios (ORs) were estimated using random-effects models with REML and Hartung-Knapp adjustment. Eighteen studies (16 cross-sectional, 2 cohort; n = 3,225) were included. In Parkinson’s disease (3 studies, n = 248), SUA was consistently negatively associated with the NMSS sleep/fatigue domain (r = − 0.36, 95% CI: −0.47 to − 0.25; I² = 0%). In five cross-sectional studies on post-stroke fatigue, the pooled OR was 1.52 (95% CI: 0.34–6.80; I² = 92.1%) but did not reach statistical significance. The association between SUA and fatigue appears to be population-specific. In Parkinson’s disease, higher SUA levels were consistently associated with less severe sleep/fatigue symptoms, whereas the association in post-stroke fatigue remained heterogeneous and inconclusive.
Background
Uric acid is the terminal product of purine metabolism, and its serum levels in humans have long been a focus of clinical attention1. Traditionally, hyperuricemia has been regarded as a direct risk factor for gout and nephrolithiasis2,3, and is closely associated with components of metabolic syndrome such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease4. However, fatigue—a multidimensional and debilitating symptom—is prevalent across a wide range of chronic conditions and in the general population5. It is not only the most common non-motor symptom in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, but also a prominent clinical complaint among patients with gout, post-stroke conditions, and chronic fatigue syndrome6. Despite its high prevalence, specific biomarkers for risk assessment and intervention monitoring in clinical practice remain lacking.
Notably, emerging evidence from diverse disease contexts has suggested a potential link between uric acid levels and fatigue. The pathogenesis of fatigue involves central neuroinflammation, neurotransmitter dysregulation, metabolic abnormalities, and psychosocial factors7. Uric acid, which has a dual role in neurological disorders—hypouricemia may compromise antioxidant defense capacity, while hyperuricemia may reduce the risk of Parkinson’s disease but increase cerebrovascular risk—has been proposed as a key molecule linking metabolic disorders and neurological diseases8,9. A prospective study of patients with ischemic stroke reported that a high serum uric acid level at admission was a strong independent risk factor for chronic fatigue one year after stroke10. Similarly, multiple studies on Parkinson’s disease have consistently indicated an association between serum uric acid levels and fatigue, spanning both early and advanced stages of the disease11,12,13.
Although the evidence outlined above spans multiple disciplines—including neurology, rheumatology, and rehabilitation medicine—it remains fragmented. Considerable heterogeneity exists in study design, population characteristics (e.g., stroke, Parkinson’s disease, gout, general population), exposure definitions (serum uric acid, urate, or hyperuricemia), and outcome measurement tools (e.g., Fatigue Severity Scale, Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory, single-item reports). To date, no study has systematically synthesized evidence from these diverse clinical contexts to address a more fundamental question with translational implications: In human populations, is there a consistent and independent association between uric acid levels and fatigue? If so, does the direction of this association vary according to the metabolic or neuropathological background of the population? Furthermore, whether the underlying dominant mechanism is oxidative stress injury, pro-inflammatory effects, or the antioxidant protective role of uric acid remains inconclusive.
Conducting this systematic review is justified on multiple grounds: fatigue is a core symptom that diminishes the quality of life in patients with chronic diseases, making the identification of modifiable biomarkers clinically valuable. Uric acid, as a routinely measured clinical parameter, could serve as a convenient tool for screening high-risk populations if its association with fatigue is confirmed. Furthermore, existing evidence remains fragmented across different research contexts, underscoring the urgent need for methodological synthesis to clarify the consistency and moderators of this association.
Therefore, this study aims to systematically synthesize evidence from observational studies, comprehensively evaluate the relationship between serum uric acid levels and fatigue symptoms, and explore the consistency of effect directions and sources of heterogeneity across different populations, thereby providing an evidence base for future mechanistic research and clinical interventions.
Methods
Design
The results of the systematic review are presented following the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement14. The study protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO on 2 March 2026 (CRD420261305883). Available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420261305883. No deviations from the registered protocol occurred.
Search methods
Two independent researchers (HTF and CSY) systematically searched the following electronic databases from inception to August 29th, 2026: PubMed, Web of Science (including the Web of Science Core Collection, KCI-Korean Journal Database, MEDLINE, Preprint Citation Index, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Citation Index, Research Commons, and SciELO Citation Index), Embase, the Cochrane Library, APA PsycInfo, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and the China Biomedical Database (SinoMed). The search aimed to identify studies examining the association between fatigue and uric acid in human populations. The search strategy involved a combination of relevant MeSH terms and other key terms using Boolean operators (AND, OR, and NOT) such as ‘Hyperuricemia’, ‘uric acid’ ‘Gout’, ‘urate’, ‘fatigue’, ‘fatigability’, ‘exertional’, ‘exhaustion’, ‘exhausted’, ‘depletion’, and ‘tiredness’. To ensure that no articles were overlooked, the reference lists of the retrieved studies were thoroughly searched. The complete search strategy is presented in Table 1.
Eligibility criteria
The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) observational studies including cross‑sectional, case‑control, and cohort designs; (2) conducted in any human population regardless of age or clinical status; (3) reporting serum uric acid levels; (4) assessing fatigue using any validated self‑reported instrument (e.g., questionnaires, scales) or clinically defined fatigue outcome (e.g., fatigue‑related diagnosis, fatigue as a primary complaint); (5)Investigating the association (relationship) between uric acid levels and fatigue; and (6) no restrictions on publication language. Studies were excluded if they: (1) were animal or in vitro studies; (2) had an interventional design (e.g., randomized controlled trials), were reviews, meta‑analyses, case reports, or conference abstracts with insufficient data; (3) lacked full‑text access or essential data for quantitative synthesis; or (4) were duplicate publications—only the most complete or recent report was included.
Study selection and data extraction
EndNote 2025 was used to manage the included studies and exclude duplicate records. Two researchers (HTF and CSY) independently performed a two-step screening process: first, titles and abstracts were reviewed to exclude obviously irrelevant studies; subsequently, the full texts of potentially eligible articles were assessed against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by arbitration from a third researcher (TTH).
Two reviewers (HTF and CSY) independently performed data extraction using a piloted, standardized form. The extraction instrument was refined iteratively to enhance clarity and consistency before being applied to all eligible studies. Extracted information comprised: first author, publication year, country, study design, population, sample size, participant characteristics (including age and gender), uric acid concentration, fatigue assessment instrument, reported findings on the association between uric acid and fatigue, and both adjusted and unadjusted effect estimates. Adjusted OR were preferentially extracted when available. For the OR analysis, the direction of fatigue outcome coding was consistent across all included studies. For the correlation analysis, fatigue scales were scored in different directions; correlations were therefore harmonized a priori so that positive values indicated higher uric acid levels associated with greater fatigue burden. All extracted data were compiled and cross-verified into a structured summary table.
Quality appraisal
Two reviewers (HTF and CSY) independently evaluated the methodological quality of the included studies. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for quality assessment, with different versions applied according to study design15. For cohort studies, the original NOS was applied across three domains: Selection, Comparability, and Outcome, with a maximum possible score of 9 points. Studies achieving a score of ≥ 7 were classified as high quality. For cross-sectional studies, the adapted NOS for observational studies was utilized, with scores ranging from 0 to 10. Studies were categorized as high quality (7–10 points), moderate quality (4–6 points), or low quality (0–3 points) based on their overall score16. Any disagreements during the evaluation were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (TTH).
Data analysis
All meta-analysis were conducted using R software (version 5.4.2). The combined results are expressed as odds ratios (OR), Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r), and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Prior to pooling, correlation coefficients underwent Fisher’s z‑transformation to reduce bias17. Given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity among observational studies, a random-effects model was used as the primary analysis for all meta-analysis, irrespective of statistical heterogeneity indices18. The restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) estimator was used for τ², and the Hartung-Knapp adjustment was applied to provide more robust confidence intervals, particularly given the small number of studies in each synthesis19,20. A common-effect model was used only as a sensitivity analysis. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and the Q test. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially omitting each study and by examining the result of study design and effect direction where feasible. For the OR analysis, all estimates were rescaled to represent the effect per 100 µmol/L increase in serum uric acid to improve clinical interpretability21.All tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
