TITLE: Inserting genetic material from humans with a rare disease recapitulates the disease in mice to speed treatment design
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

Through “genome writing,” inserting a large swath of human genetic material linked to a rare disease called X-linked dystonia-parkinsonism (XDP), a research team created living mice with an accurate but accelerated version of the genetic condition, a new study shows.
Led by NYU Langone Health researchers, the study featured mice with a humanized version of XDP, which enabled the team to confirm cellular mechanisms behind the disease and partially reverse them. To do so, the research team had to rewrite a portion of the mouse gene TAF1 such that it had genetic material corresponding with human XDP, an adult-onset neurodegenerative disease in which a person develops movement disorders that worsen with age.
Published online in the journal Cell, the study is based on how human genes are known to be influenced by DNA snippets called transposons that weave their genes into the genome, the complete set of genetic material in each cell. Thousands of transposons, or jumping genes, reside in the mouse and human genomes, where they duplicate and randomly insert themselves into new locations in the DNA code.
While most transposons are silenced by our cells’ defense mechanisms, a few have come to play roles in genetic diseases when they insert themselves in the wrong places. The pathogenic insertions causing XPD are known to be SINE-VNTR-Alu (SVA) transposons, a class unique to humans and absent in mice, which insert themselves into the TAF1 gene.
TAF1 encodes part of the machinery that copies DNA into messenger RNA, which builds key proteins to carry out vital cellular functions. The inserted SVA elements reduce TAF1 gene expression by forming roadblocks to the transcription of DNA into RNA, leading to a short supply of the TAF1 protein and cell death, as shown by the team.
To confirm the mechanism, the researchers rewrote a portion of the mouse gene TAF1 such that it had genetic material corresponding exactly to human XDP. The resulting mouse embryonic stem cells and the mice that developed from them copied the molecular and symptomatic hallmarks of human XDP, including reduced TAF1 protein, cell death and holes in the brain. Importantly, these mice showed XDP symptoms up to 300 times faster than human patients (in 1.5 months versus 30 years), enabling high-speed tests of potential treatments.
The reason it takes more than 30 years for the disease to develop in humans, said senior study author Jef D. Boeke, PhD, the Sol and Judith Bergstein Director of the Institute for Systems Genetics at NYU Langone, may be that humans have evolved genetic mechanisms to defend cells against SVAs. Mice lack these because they have never encountered SVAs, until now. Using a molecular tool called dCas9 to boost TAF expression in stem cells, the researchers showed that the effect of the SVA on TAF1 levels could be reversed. Thus, the researchers say, boosting TAF1 levels may counter XDP progression.
“Our experiments establish a rationale for the future development of new therapeutic approaches for XDP patients,” said Dr. Boeke. “Beyond XDP, the work presents a generalizable strategy for building mouse models of human rare diseases at speed, not only to model disease biology but also to serve as a therapeutic testing ground.”
Past studies showed that an SVA transposition at one point occurred randomly in one person living on Panay Island in the Philippines. The TAF1 gene with the SVA insertion and resulting XDP was then inherited by that person’s descendants.
Genome Writing
The study result was made possible because, for the last decade, Dr. Boeke has led an international consortium that created a set of genome-writing techniques to swap large portions of genetic material with engineered replacements. The techniques can even build synthetic versions of entire chromosomes—the super structures that house and regulate the activity of DNA chains.
The process begins with the team designing very specific changes to a targeted chromosome and assembling synthetic DNA sequences. These sequences are then introduced into cells, where cellular machinery finishes the genome writing. In the current case, 78,000 human DNA code “letters” (78 kilobases), including the SVAs, were inserted into the mouse genome.
A second paper, led by Shane A. Liddelow, PhD, an associate professor in the Institute for Translational Neuroscience and the Departments of Neuroscience and Ophthalmology at NYU Langone, and published in the same edition of Cell, showed that the cells affected by XDP in the brains of mice were oligodendrocytes rather than neurons as many had predicted. Oligodendrocytes produce the myelin “insulation” around the axons that interconnect neurons, enabling them to pass on messages.
Using the same humanized XDP mouse model, Dr. Liddelow’s team found that the resulting male mice had the same features seen in human XDP patients, including severe movement disorders. Remarkably, the researchers showed that the loss of myelin in the mouse model occurred in banked human XDP brain tissue, a defect that had been missed by decades of earlier studies on human XDP brains.
“NYU Langone researchers working together across departments produced a novel model to study XDP, which led to the groundbreaking discovery that this disease may not be due to the loss of neurons, but more importantly due to the loss of their supporting cells,” said Dr. Liddelow
