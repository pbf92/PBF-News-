TITLE: Amazon adjusts bids to chase the spending ad buyers promised publishers
OUTLET: PPC Land

Amazon Ads has set out, in two Support Center pages last updated on November 2, 2026, how advertisers and agencies record the money they have promised to spend with individual publishers, and what its buying system does when that spending falls behind. Titled "Create and manage commitments" and "Track and analyze commitments," the pages sit directly beneath the "Create a DVA+ campaign" guide in the Support Center, a guide Amazon updated again on November 24, 2026, and which carries a commitments step of its own in two of its three campaign-building methods.
The rules are specific. A commitment can last from 7 to 366 days, with one quarter recommended. It counts only what the publisher receives for its ad space, not the fees charged on the buying side. Its recommended setting tells Amazon's system to adjust bids until the promised amount is spent, except on one type of deal. And publishers can share commitments directly with a buyer's account, where the buyer cannot change the dates or the deals.
In Short
Amazon has documented a tool that tracks how much money brands and agencies have promised to spend with each publisher, such as Disney for Hulu, and over what period. It matters to anyone buying streaming TV, video or audio through Amazon on a contract with a publisher, because the recommended setting changes bids automatically to hit the promised figure, and because a commitment shared by the publisher locks its dates and deals. The promised amount covers only the media itself, so campaign budgets have to be larger than the commitment to pay for fees on top.
What a commitment is
Television advertising has long run on promises. In the annual upfront market, brands and agencies agree months in advance to spend a set sum with a broadcaster or streaming service, and spend the rest of the year making sure the money goes out the door. Programmatic buying, where ads are bought by software one impression at a time, has carried that habit over. A buyer may agree to spend a fixed amount with a publisher through a set of private arrangements, each identified by a deal ID, the code that tells an auction which buyer and which terms apply.
Amazon's pages describe the tool in one sentence: "Commitments help you manage advertisement spending agreements between ad buyers (advertising agencies and brands) and sellers (publishers) in Display, Video, and Audio campaign inventory." The next sentence names the three things each record holds: "Each commitment tracks your agreed spending amount, time period for spending, and associated deal IDs."
A diagram on the "Create and manage commitments" page explains how spending is counted. Under the heading "Impressions," two overlapping circles are labeled "Advertisers & Campaigns" and "Deal IDs." The overlap carries the words "Commitment Spend Accrual." In plain terms, money counts toward a commitment only when it is spent by the advertisers and campaigns linked to that commitment, through the deals linked to it. Spending by the same advertiser outside those deals does not count; nor does spending on those deals by campaigns left out of the commitment.
Amazon lists four benefits. The tool is meant to "Monitor spending across all your commitments without manual calculations," to view "spending breakdowns across campaigns and deal IDs," to show the current state of each commitment, and, the one with consequences for bidding, it "Helps meet commitment goals by optimizing bidding on your commitment's deals when it falls behind pace." Falling behind pace means spending more slowly than needed to reach the promised total by the end date.
The settings, in Amazon's words
The rules sit in the "Create commitments manually" tab of the first page. Most of them are short. Set out together, they read as follows.
Committed spend counts the media, not the fees
The definition of the target matters more than its length suggests. Committed spend is measured in supply cost, the price paid to the publisher for the ad space. According to Amazon, committed spend is "The total amount you've agreed to spend with a publisher during a specific time period. It includes only the Supply cost (excludes buy-side fees) and doesn't take into account your planned advertising cost outside of Amazon Ads Agent."
Two things follow from that wording. The first is that a campaign budget has to pay for more than the target. Fees are layered on top of the media cost, and Amazon's own streaming TV guide names five of them: a platform fee, Amazon audience fees, third-party audience fees, third-party pre-bid fees and agency fees. A campaign budget equal to the commitment would therefore leave the publisher short. That guide, updated on November 17, addressed exactly this with a Budget Calculator that sizes an ad group's budget as committed spend plus up to five estimated fees. The second is that the tool only knows about money spent through Amazon. A buyer who promised a publisher a sum to be spent partly through Amazon and partly elsewhere would see only the Amazon share.
The phrase "outside of Amazon Ads Agent" also shows how far a renaming has travelled. Amazon Ads Agent is the name now printed across the top of the advertising console, and it is the name the documentation uses for the platform as a whole, although by late October the conversational assistant the console is named after was still in closed beta.
The bidding switch
Spend priority is the setting that changes what the system does, and it treats one deal type, programmatic guaranteed, differently from the rest. The first option, "Prioritize fulfilling the committed spend," is marked as recommended. Its full description reads: "Display, Video, and Audio campaigns automatically optimizes bidding to reach your committed spend by analyzing spend trajectory and adjusting bids based on pace and performance. Campaign optimization settings remain unchanged. (This doesn't apply to Programmatic Guaranteed (PG) deals)."
The second option, "Prioritize campaign performance," does the opposite: "Display, Video, and Audio campaigns disables automatic bid adjustments related to commitments. Campaigns and ad groups operate independently."
The exception in brackets is not arbitrary. Programmatic guaranteed deals are bought at a fixed price for a reserved number of impressions, so there is no auction bid for the system to raise or lower. The pages do not say how a commitment made up only of guaranteed deals is kept on pace; they say only that the bid adjustments do not apply to them.
What the recommended option means in practice is a trade. A buyer who chooses it asks Amazon to treat the promised sum as a goal in its own right, alongside whatever the campaign was built to achieve, whether that is reach, return on ad spend or something else. The page says campaign optimization settings "remain unchanged," and that bids move "based on pace and performance," but it gives no limit on how far bids can move, and does not say which goal wins when spending the money and performing well point in different directions. For the publisher at the other end of the deal, the arrangement has an obvious appeal: the money it was promised is more likely to arrive.
Two ways in: build it or import it
Commitments can be created by hand, or imported from the publisher. The import route depends on an account number. According to Amazon, "To import a commitment, share your Deal Creation Seat ID with your seller typically via email. The seller uses this ID to permission commitments to your account."
A seat ID, in this sense, is the identifier a publisher uses to address deals and commitments to a particular buyer account. The page gives the path to find it in three steps, and adds two exceptions: "Seat IDs can also be found at the advertiser level in advertiser settings. If you manage commitments at the group level, contact your account representative."
Imported commitments come with conditions. "Seller commitments have restricted edit permissions because they stay linked to the seller's data," according to the page, which lists three settings the buyer cannot edit: start and end dates, deals, and "Seller information and commitment source."
The link to the publisher's record runs both ways, and fast. "When your seller revises the commitment, all linked details update automatically within 1 hour," the page states. The same passage describes what happens when a publisher withdraws one: "If a seller deletes a commitment, your commitment still exists but all associated deals are removed. The commitment becomes inactive because it has no deals. You can still view it in the Commitments tab."
That makes the publisher's copy the master record for the terms that matter most. A buyer can still see a deleted commitment, but without deals no spending can count toward it, and the dates cannot be moved to start over. Which party holds the authoritative version of an agreement is a question agencies and publishers usually settle in contracts; for the fields it locks, the software settles it.
There is a small tension in the page's own wording. Its note says "Any commitment setting can be edited before, during, and after the commitment flight," while the seller section lists three settings that cannot be edited at all. The second rule applies only to commitments imported from a seller, which the note does not say.
How the setup works, step by step
According to Amazon, the commitment builder is reached through the campaign side of the console, not a separate tool. The documented path and settings are as follows.
- Finding the seat ID (for imports). In the left menu, Administration; then the manager account settings; then the Deal Creation Seat ID in the account details. The ID is shared with the seller, "typically via email."
- Opening the commitment builder. In the navigation side menu, Campaigns; at the top of the Campaigns page, the Display, Video, and Audio tab; expanding the page side menu; Inventory in that menu; then the Commitments tab at the top of the Inventory page, and Create commitment.
- Choosing a route. Two tabs: "Create commitments manually" and "Import commitments." The pages describe the manual tab in full.
- Commitment settings. Commitment Name; Dates, including time zone, within 7 to 366 days; Committed spend, in supply cost; Spend priority, one of two options.
- Associated parties and orders. Advertisers (all or selected); Campaigns (all current and future campaigns using the linked deal IDs, or selected campaigns); Seller, at the level of the parent publisher organization.
- Deals. Deal IDs linked to the commitment. Without a deal ID, the commitment can be saved as a draft and the deals added later through its settings.
- Launch. Create commitment at the top of the page. Afterwards the commitment can be tracked in the Commitments Hub and edited from the Inventory page, up to 14 days after its end date.
The campaign setting has a practical effect on maintenance. "All current and future campaigns" "Automatically includes every campaign (existing and new) from the selected advertisers that uses the linked deal IDs. No manual updates needed," according to Amazon. "Selected campaigns" counts only campaigns chosen explicitly, "giving you tighter control." The first option means a new campaign can start counting toward a commitment the moment it targets one of the linked deals, whether or not its builder intended it to.
