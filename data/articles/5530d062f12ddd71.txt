TITLE: The typical Google Ads sale costs European shops 14% more, Channable finds
OUTLET: PPC Land

The median cost of winning a sale through Google Ads in Europe rose 14% in a year, from €14.41 in October 2025 to €16.47 in October 2026, according to benchmark data Channable released today. The Dutch e-commerce software company said the price of a click barely moved over the same period. Channable puts the increase down to shoppers who clicked on more ads while fewer of those clicks ended in a purchase, and its chief executive expects the weekend starting with Black Friday on November 27 to be the most expensive per sale in its benchmark.
In Short
Channable, which manages product listings for online retailers, compared Google Ads results from more than 10,000 European online shops in October 2025 and October 2026 and found that the typical cost of getting one sale rose 14%, to €16.47. Clicks did not get more expensive; people clicked on more ads and fewer of those clicks ended in a purchase, so retailers paid for more browsing per order, with German shops on one Google campaign type seeing a 25% rise. The figures come from a company that sells tools for lowering Google Shopping costs, and the release leaves out most of the numbers behind its category and country claims, so the 14% and Germany's 25% are the only year-on-year figures it gives.
What the October numbers show
Channable's release, issued today from Utrecht, compares two Octobers across five measures for online retailers advertising on Google: the price of a click, the share of people who click an ad after seeing it, the share of clicks that end in a purchase, the cost of each sale, and the revenue each euro of advertising brings back. The data is split between Google Shopping campaigns, the product ads with a picture and a price, and Performance Max, Google's automated campaign type that places ads across Search, Shopping, YouTube and other Google properties.
Only one of the five measures comes with numbers. The median cost of a sale, the amount an advertiser spends on ads for each order, went from €14.41 to €16.47. That is a rise of 14.3%, a PPC Land calculation from the two figures, which Channable rounds to 14%. The median is the middle value when all accounts are lined up from lowest to highest, so half of the advertisers paid more than €16.47 for a sale and half paid less.
The other measures are described in words. Click prices were "broadly flat" year on year, according to Channable. The click-through rate, the share of people who saw an ad and clicked it, rose in every product category the company tracks. The conversion rate, the share of clicks that ended in a purchase, fell in most of them. Germany recorded the steepest rise in the cost of a sale: 25% on Performance Max, according to the release.
The release does not say whether the 14% covers Shopping and Performance Max together or one of them, and it gives no figures for any other country.
How a flat click price turns into a dearer sale
The arithmetic behind the headline is simple. For a single advertiser, the cost of a sale equals the price of a click divided by the share of clicks that turn into orders. If a click costs €0.50 and one in 30 clicks ends in a purchase, each sale costs €15 in advertising. If the click price stays at €0.50 but only one in 34 clicks leads to an order, the cost of a sale climbs to €17, even though nothing changed in the auction.
Applied to Channable's figures, a flat click price and a 14.3% rise in the cost of a sale would mean about 12% fewer clicks ending in a purchase, or roughly one sale lost for every eight that the same clicks would have produced a year earlier. That is a PPC Land calculation and only an approximation: medians taken across thousands of accounts do not divide into each other as neatly as the numbers of one account do, and Channable published no conversion rate figures to check it against.
Channable reads the combination of more clicks and fewer purchases as a change in how people shop: according to the company, shoppers are clicking on more ads for each purchase and making up their minds later. For retailers, the release argues, the same budget now buys fewer sales than a year ago, and every discounted order carries a higher advertising cost on top of the price cut. Channable also argues that a budget planned on last year's numbers will run out before the end of the Black Friday weekend, and that spending more on clicks will not solve it, since the higher cost does not come from click prices.
How Channable's benchmark works
The figures in the release come from a free online report that Channable calls the eCommerce Google Ads Benchmark. Its introductory page and the release describe the setup in the following way.
- Data pool. Channable says it aggregates and anonymises data "from over 10,000 Google Ads accounts across the EU and EEA." The release's methodology note describes the same pool as more than €1.38 billion in verified Google Ads spend from more than 10,000 European e-commerce advertisers.
- Format. The report runs on Google's Looker Studio, the company's free dashboard tool. Its page list includes "How To", "What is this report?", three pages comparing a merchant's own performance with the market, "Market Trends", "Industry Year Median" and "Country Year Median." A glossary panel lists the abbreviations CPC, CTR, CVR, CPA, ROAS and PMax.
- Comparison with a merchant's own account. "Your account data shown in this report is loaded directly from your Google Ads account through Google's official connector. We do not store or process data that flows through this connection," the report's introduction states.
- Currency. "For consistency, the current version uses the currency EUR in all benchmark data," according to the same page.
- Measures. The release names median cost per click, click-through rate, conversion rate, cost per acquisition and return on ad spend, split by Shopping and Performance Max campaigns. Channable's public benchmark page also lists Search campaigns, says the figures are medians and says the data is refreshed daily.
- Segment rules. When PPC Land covered Channable's previous release in July, the benchmark required at least 10 merchants in each segment and no single business above 25% of a segment, with only verified EU and EEA advertisers included. The new release does not restate those rules.
The report presents itself as a service for Channable's clients in Google Shopping. "As your Google CSS partner, we help thousands of merchants get more out of every Shopping click, lower CPCs, stronger bids, better visibility," its opening panel reads, before describing the benchmark as the company's way of sharing what it sees across its network. A comparison shopping service is the intermediary that European merchants use to place Google Shopping ads, and Channable runs one. The company therefore sells a product whose stated benefit includes lower click prices, while publishing data on what those clicks cost.
What changed since July
Channable's previous release, in July, pointed in a different direction. Covering June 2025 to June 2026, Channable's data showed the price of a click up 15% year on year across Shopping and Performance Max, with return on ad spend down 46% on Performance Max and 43% on Shopping. The cost of a sale rose €1.44 on Performance Max and €1.94 on Shopping in that period. Channable warned at the time that budgets set for the competition levels of the first quarter would not last through the fourth.
Four months later, the price of a click is described as flat. The release links the two snapshots directly: in July the pressure showed up in click prices, and in October it showed up in the cost of each sale. The two comparisons cover different months, however, June against June and October against October, so they do not show when click prices stopped rising or whether they fell back between the two readings. The €1.38 billion and 10,000-advertiser figures in the November methodology note are the same ones Channable gave in July, when the data ran from June 2024 to June 2026.
Google's own numbers offer a wider frame. Alphabet reported paid clicks up 8% and cost per click up 4% in the third quarter of 2026, worldwide, for ads on Google Search and its other owned properties. In business-to-business search, an agency study of 14 advertisers found that the same non-brand keywords cost about 21% more between April and September 2026 than a year earlier. Channable's October figures suggest that for European online shops, at least, the click price was no longer where the year-on-year increase sat.
Browsing in October, buying on the weekend
Channable does not present the data as a sign of weak demand. Rob van Nuenen, the company's chief executive and co-founder, framed October as a trial run for the weekend ahead.
"If October was the rehearsal, this will be the most expensive Black Friday per sale we've measured in our benchmark, and retailers should plan for spending over 20% more per order than last year," he said. "The browsing was already up in October; the buying wasn't. We expect conversion to concentrate into the Black Friday weekend itself: weeks of window-shopping, then a few days that decide how successful the quarter has been and at what cost."
The 20% figure is his forecast. The measured October increase is 14%, and the release does not explain how the higher number was reached.
To support the view that shoppers are waiting rather than walking away, Channable cites a KPMG survey. KPMG UK, which polled 3,000 people online between October 2 and October 7, 2026, found that 49% of those planning to shop the Black Friday period, or who might be tempted to, had postponed buying items in the hope of a discount. A separate dataset points the same way for last year: Billy Grace found that median cost per order fell about 18% in Black Friday week 2025 compared with the weeks before it, even as ad prices rose.
The release also leans on Adobe's forecast for the US season. Adobe expects US online holiday spending of $275.1 billion, up 6.7%, between November 1 and December 31, 2026, with Black Friday itself at $12.9 billion, up 9.2%, and traffic from AI services to retail sites up 130%. Those are American figures; Channable's benchmark covers only European advertisers.
AI assistants and out-of-date product data
The second half of van Nuenen's statement concerns software that shops on a person's behalf. Channable's own business is managing product feed data, the files that carry a retailer's prices, stock levels and descriptions to Google Shopping, Amazon and, according to the company, more than 3,000 other channels.
"We also know it won't just be people window shopping this Black Friday. AI assistants are comparing products and prices on shoppers' behalf, and they can only recommend what they can read," he said. "A price that hasn't updated or a stock flag that's out of date doesn't look like a small error to an agent - it looks like a reason to buy from someone else. The retailers who hold their margins this weekend must ensure their product data is as ready as their discounts."
Channable's release refers to a BBC test of four AI chatbots on Black Friday television deals the previous week, which, according to the company, turned up dead links, sold-out products and last year's prices presented as current offers. Channable attributes that pattern to product feeds updating on different schedules across different channels, so that an AI tool reads whichever copy it can reach. The release does not name the chatbots or link to the BBC report, and it gives no data of its own on AI shopping traffic in Europe.
Who is behind the numbers
Channable was founded in 2014 in Utrecht, the Netherlands. Its software manages product data for online sellers across Google Shopping, Performance Max, Amazon and other marketplaces, and includes tools for ad bidding, AI-written product descriptions, automated ad images and comparison shopping service placements.
