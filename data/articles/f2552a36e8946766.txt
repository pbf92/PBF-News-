TITLE: Study finds 1 in 6 U.S. teens report moderate to severe anxiety symptoms
OUTLET: ABC30 Fresno

FRESNO, Calif. (KFSN) -- A recent study found that one in six U.S. teenagers report moderate to severe anxiety symptoms, and many are not getting help.
Researchers at the University of Connecticut analyzed a subset of data from the 2024 National Survey on Drug Use and Health, including self-reported anxiety symptoms from more than 15,000 teenagers ages 12 to 20.
The study, published in JAMA Pediatrics, found that nearly two-thirds of youths who reported severe anxiety symptoms also met criteria for severe depression. Nearly half reported suicidal thoughts, and nearly one-third reported substance abuse.
Teenagers often face a variety of stressors, including exams, social pressures, social media, adapting to new environments and forming friendships.
According to the Child Mind Institute, anxiety can appear differently from one teenager to another. Common symptoms include irritability, difficulty concentrating, recurring fears and worries about everyday life, extreme self-consciousness, sensitivity to criticism, withdrawal from daily activities, declining grades and school refusal.
The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests that anxiety becomes a concern when thoughts or symptoms interfere with daily functioning.
Researchers found that nearly half of teenagers with severe anxiety symptoms said they had not sought care for their symptoms during the previous year.
The study also found that girls were nearly twice as likely as boys to report severe anxiety symptoms.
According to the study, male teenagers, Black teenagers and uninsured teenagers were the most likely groups to report not seeking care for anxiety symptoms.
Dr. Zinah Qader, a family medicine physician with Adventist Health, said parents and caregivers can help by creating a safe environment where teenagers feel comfortable discussing their emotions.
"I would be more listening, and I would be, in a way, like a soothing way, telling them, like, 'I'm noticing you've been a little bit stressed these days. Do you want to talk about it?' And in a way that you want to listen," Dr. Qader said. "I really recommend that parents bring the teenager in and do a visit with their primary care. That way, we can discuss these symptoms together with the family and the teenager. And usually in clinic, we have some screening tests- a screening questionnaire about anxiety and we can go more into, you know, what level they are at with their anxiety. And usually we discuss the modalities of treatment, with medication, with a therapist. We have specialized people to treat anxiety, and then we'll refer them to a behavioral specialist as needed. I highly encourage parents to look into it, and then never hesitate to discuss it with your primary care."
Doctors say open communication and early conversations with health care providers can help connect teens with appropriate support and treatment options.
