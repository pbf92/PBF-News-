TITLE: Channel 4 backs 2034 Freeview switch-off, citing a fourfold cost per home
OUTLET: PPC Land

Channel 4 wants the UK's terrestrial television network switched off at the end of 2034 rather than kept running for another decade, arguing that a later date would leave broadcasters paying more to reach fewer homes. The publicly owned broadcaster sets out the case in a four-page statement, "Securing a Sustainable Future for TV Distribution", which summarises its response to the government's Media Green Paper. The document carries no publication date.
Today, Ian Whittaker, the media analyst whose report on the economics of a 2034 switch-off was commissioned by transmission operator Arqiva, published a point-by-point critique of the statement on LinkedIn. He accepts parts of Channel 4's argument and disputes four others, chief among them that the broadcaster's case says nothing about advertising revenue.
In Short
Channel 4 has published a short statement asking the UK government to switch off digital terrestrial television, the signal behind Freeview, at the end of 2034 and move TV distribution to the internet, instead of extending the network to 2044. The date matters to anyone who buys or sells UK TV advertising, because analyst Ian Whittaker, in a report Arqiva commissioned, projects that a 2034 date would leave TV ad revenue 16% lower in 2034 than a 2044 date would, and Channel 4's statement does not address that question. The two sides also disagree on how many homes would be left without internet TV in 2034: 0.67 million in the forecast Channel 4 cites, 1.51 million in the government's own Green Paper. The government's consultation closed on August 31, 2026, and no decision has been announced, so broadcasters, advertisers and viewers still have no fixed date to plan around.
What Channel 4 is asking for
The statement opens with a description of the broadcaster itself. "Channel 4 is the UK's only publicly owned and entirely commercially funded broadcaster," it reads. For a broadcaster that lives on advertising, every pound spent on transmission is a pound not spent on programmes, and the statement makes that link directly when it says rising DTT costs would divert funding from content.
The core request is set out in the summary. According to Channel 4, it "is united with the other UK public service media (PSM) providers in calling for a managed switchover from digital terrestrial television (DTT) to internet protocol television (IPTV)-only distribution in 2034, supported by a clear transition plan that preserves universality and ensures TV and PSM remain free and easy to access." A footnote names those providers as the BBC, ITV, Channel 4, Channel 5, STV and S4C.
The statement responds to "Watch this space: a new strategic direction for UK media", the Green Paper published by the Department for Culture, Media and Sport on June 23, 2026. According to the government's consultation page, the consultation ran from June 23 to 11:59pm on August 31, 2026, and the department is now analysing feedback. The Green Paper consults on two dates for a managed withdrawal of DTT: the expiry of current licences on December 31, 2034, or a time-limited extension to December 31, 2044. According to the Green Paper, the government sees a compelling case for moving from DTT to IPTV by 2034, but it also commits to keeping DTT running until at least December 31, 2034, and says no decision has yet been made on what follows.
Channel 4's statement leans on the "compelling case" phrase. Its first section heading reads: "The Government recognises a 'compelling case' for a DTT transition in 2034."
The two options on the table
The cost argument: fourfold and £1.5bn
Channel 4's economic case rests on fixed costs. According to the statement, "the costs of maintaining the network are largely fixed, meaning that as viewing declines and commercial broadcasters leave the platform, a greater share of those costs falls on the remaining PSM providers who retain obligations to maintain universal availability via DTT."
The broadcaster puts a number on its own exposure. "Channel 4 estimates that its cost of delivering DTT on a per-household basis will increase fourfold between 2024 and 2034, diverting funding away from British content, digital services and innovation." The statement does not give the underlying pound figure, the number of households used in the calculation, or the cost in 2024.
The second figure is the price of keeping DTT alive. "Current proposals would require a substantial rebuild of the DTT network at an estimated cost to broadcasters of £1.5 billion, in order to extend the platform's life by a further ten years," the statement says. The source cited is written evidence from the Future TV Taskforce to the Culture, Media and Sport Committee's BBC Royal Charter Review inquiry, dated June 2026.
Channel 4 also rejects the idea that 2044 would preserve today's service. "Crucially, the choice is not between a managed transition in 2034 and maintaining today's DTT service unchanged until 2044," according to the statement. As audiences and revenues fall, commercial channels are likely to leave the platform, it argues, so a delay "risks a disorderly, market-led, and costly transition rather than preserving a stable DTT service for another decade."
The summary is blunter about the consequence for viewers who stay on the aerial. If the switch waits until 2044, "those audiences who remain reliant on DTT will be left with a dwindling range of channels and content as the costs of remaining on the network rise for TV service providers."
Who would be left behind
The number of homes without internet television in 2034 is where the two sides' figures part most sharply.
According to the Green Paper, there were 4.01 million broadcast-only households in 2024, alongside 16.61 million hybrid homes that use both broadcast and internet TV. It projects that the number of households without access to IPTV will fall to 1.51 million in 2034.
Channel 4 uses a lower figure. According to the statement, "the most recent 3Reasons forecasts suggest a very small proportion of households will be without access to IPTV in 2034 (0.67m / c.2%)." The footnote points to a critique of the Green Paper's cost-benefit analysis by Robert Kenny of Communications Chambers, dated August 2026; a later footnote gives the source as "3 Reasons estimates, spring 2026." According to that critique, which says it was funded by Everyone TV, the free TV platform operator, the 0.67 million is split between 0.19 million households choosing not to connect and 0.48 million with unsuitable broadband. The same critique says the government's 1.51 million includes 0.88 million homes without suitable broadband.
The government's figure is more than twice the one Channel 4 uses. The broadcaster concludes that "the remaining challenge can be addressed through targeted support for those most at risk of exclusion" and that the gap can be closed "at reasonable cost and funded through the millions that a full transition would unlock, including proceeds from a future auction of DTT spectrum." No figure is given for that cost or for the auction.
The case beyond television
The statement widens the argument from broadcasting to digital inclusion. It cites three figures, each from a third party:
"Access to broadband is increasingly essential for accessing public services, managing money, finding work and participating in education and community life," the statement says. The GVA figure comes from research commissioned by the BBC, one of the six broadcasters backing the same date.
On resilience, Channel 4 turns a common argument for keeping the aerial on its head. "Vigilance will of course be needed to protect broadband networks against security threats," according to the statement. "However, with its reliance on a small number of major infrastructure sites, DTT has greater vulnerability to certain forms of security threat (such as physical sabotage) than broadband services operating via a distributed network." Fixed wireless and satellite are offered as alternatives in harder-to-reach areas.
What the statement asks the government to do
According to Channel 4, a 2034 switchover depends on seven elements, set out across the final page of the statement:
- Confirm the date. Channel 4 wants the government to "move from recognising the case for transition to setting out the clear timetable and coordinated framework needed to deliver it successfully in 2034."
- Preserve universality. "Any replacement for DTT must deliver comparable standards of reliability and resilience, alongside affordable and easy access to PSM services."
- Rely on the broadband rollout. The statement points to the Project Gigabit target of 99% of premises by 2032, with fixed wireless and satellite in harder-to-reach areas.
- Fund targeted support. Support for vulnerable and unconnected households is to be paid for from the money a full transition would release, including a future auction of DTT spectrum.
- Use broadcasters to explain the switch. Public service broadcasters would use their reach "to communicate the transition and direct viewers towards support."
- Point viewers to Freely. The statement says services such as Freely "are already helping audiences access IPTV through a familiar free-to-air experience."
- Share the job of connecting homes. Getting unconnected households online "will require coordinated action from Government and the wider industries that stand to benefit from increased connectivity."
A further condition runs alongside the timetable: prominence and payment on the platforms that would carry public service TV after the switch.
Gatekeepers and terms
The last paragraph of the statement is the one most directly about money. "As viewing moves online, PSM providers are increasingly reliant on global technology platforms that act as gatekeepers between PSM services and audiences, with significant influence over how content is discovered, accessed and monetised," according to Channel 4. "PSM services must therefore remain prominent and widely available, with fair commercial terms that enable continued investment in distinctive British content and services."
The statement does not say what fair terms would look like, which platforms it means, or whether it wants new rules before 2034.
That gap is the centre of the critique.
Whittaker's four objections
Ian Whittaker, managing partner of Liberty Sky Advisors, opens his post with a disclosure: "my report on the economics of a 2034 switch-off, A Risk Too Far, was commissioned by Arqiva, which runs the terrestrial network." Arqiva has proposed continuing DTT to 2044 on a lower-cost basis, and the report describes a platform of three multiplexes instead of six. He describes the Channel 4 document as "the public service broadcasters' joint case for 2034."
He agrees with two points. "The point that, if switch-off slips to 2044, commercial channels will leave Freeview as audiences fall does raise questions over a conditions-based delay," he writes. "It is also true global platforms now sit between broadcasters and their audiences, with real influence over how content is found and paid for." Then: "However, there are several problems with the submission's arguments."
Advertising revenue
The first objection is that the statement "treats the move from DTT to IPTV as merely a change of distribution mechanism. There is no mention of the impact on advertising revenues, yet for Channel 4 that is the number that matters most."
According to Whittaker, "A Risk Too Far argued UK television advertising revenues would be 16% lower under a 2034 switch-off than under 2044 in 2034, because a date changes how advertisers behave long before switch-off." He adds that "the Government's own appraisal says the effect of either date on advertising revenue is undetermined, not that it is inconsequential." The Kenny critique that Channel 4 cites for its household figure also lists the advertising revenue effect of faster IPTV adoption under an undetermined heading.
