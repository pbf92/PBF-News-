TITLE: Behind the numbers: what is driving the latest fall in net migration?
OUTLET: Office for National Statistics

Net migration has fallen to levels not typically seen since the 1990s. In this blog Sarah Crofts reviews how the picture of migration has changed since then, including updates to how we measure it and its impact on the UK population.
Excluding the early stages of the pandemic when travel restrictions were in place, UK long-term international net migration – the number of people coming to the UK for 12 months or more minus the number of people leaving – has fallen to its lowest level since 1997. Our provisional estimates show that in the year ending (YE) June 2026, net migration had fallen to 77,000. This is less than half the level of the previous year, and continues the steep decline from the peak three years ago.
Back to the 90s
In 1997, when net migration was recorded at 48,000, we collected data from the International Passenger Survey (IPS), which involved surveying people arriving at UK seaports, airports and the Channel Tunnel. Since the pandemic we have switched to using administrative records, such as visas and borders data from the Home Office, to estimate those moving into and out of the country more comprehensively. As such, we should be careful about diving too deeply into comparisons between 1997 and 2026, but we can look at the broad trends.
This shows that in 1997 movement from inside and outside the EU made a positive contribution to net migration, partially offset by more British people leaving than arriving. In 2026, non-EU citizens are the only group driving positive net migration. EU Nationals are now leaving in greater numbers than are arriving, as is the case with British Nationals. There are also larger flows of people in 2026, with more than double the number of people leaving and arriving compared with 1997. In that year, there were 327,000 arriving compared with 738,000 immigrants in YE June 2026, and 279,000 emigrating in 1997 versus 661,000 emigrants now. This reflects greater global mobility in 2026 than there was 30 years ago.
Upcoming updates
While the trends in migration are clear, the latest provisional figures do come with more uncertainty than normal, especially estimates of British nationals. Last year, we began using the Registration and Population Interaction Database (RAPID), maintained by the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP), to study their movement, but the latest RAPID release has been delayed.
Instead, as British migration has remained fairly stable in recent years, we have based our estimates on an extended 15-month projection from the last RAPID data we have. This introduces some additional uncertainty in our British estimates. We intend to update these in May 2027 with more up to date RAPID data.
We are also working closely with the Home Office to investigate a data issue which affects our estimates of family migration. Although this only makes up a small proportion of overall migration flows, it does mean that the current family migration estimates could change once this work is complete.
What does this mean for the UK?
Alongside today’s long-term international migration estimates, we’ve published our provisional population estimates for YE June 2026. It shows the UK population has grown to 69.6 million people (69,564,000), an increase of around 0.12% (80,000) from this time last year. This is the lowest annual growth since 1983.
We estimate only around 3,000 more people were born than died in the latest period, so migration remains the main driver of change, as it has been in recent times. This contrasts sharply with 1997, when births exceeded deaths by 100,000 and natural change contributed more than twice as much as international migration to population growth.
We began producing these provisional estimates last year to provide a timelier update on the size of the UK population. We update them as more information becomes available and are aiming to publish the updated figures in summer 2027. However, last year the updated figures only differed from the provisional figures by around 3,000 people (less than 0.01%), and so this first glance is informative in providing an early understanding of the changing population in 2026.
Three decades of change
Looking back at 1997 shows how much migration has changed over the past 29 years, but also how much our methods for estimating it have evolved as well. This is very much an ongoing process. As outlined in our migration strategy, discussed in a previous blog, we have a programme of continuous improvement for our migration figures.
The figures published today feature some of this. For instance, we have improved how we categorise non-EU+ nationals with dependant joiner visas, allowing them to be more accurately classified as either work or study dependants. This change affects the breakdown by reason for migration but not overall totals.
Future improvements will include seeking additional administrative data, as well as developing methods to better account for those who travel without a visa, such as those who have indefinite leave to remain.
