TITLE: Only 6.9% of Americans' ChatGPT chats show intent to buy, SparkToro finds
OUTLET: PPC Land

SparkToro on November 17 published an analysis of 118,163 prompts that 12,321 people in the United States sent to ChatGPT between January 1 and October 29, 2026, and found that only 6.9% of the conversations showed someone researching something to buy. Most of the traffic went elsewhere: to image editing, trivia, household chores, health questions and help with emails and texts.
The research was written by Rand Fishkin, co-founder and chief executive of SparkToro, and rests on a panel run by Verso, a company that, according to the post, "makes opt-in, anonymized connections to OpenAI." Search engines publish query volumes through their advertising tools, and Google offers Google Trends for comparing interest in keywords. ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and Copilot publish nothing comparable, according to Fishkin, and that gap has left marketers "forced to make wild, impossible-to-verify guesses about the volume of demand for products or services in their sector that happen via an AI conversation."
The panel gives that demand a shape. Images and visual design led the 12 categories SparkToro used. Shopping, brands and local services accounted for between 5.3% and 5.7%, depending on which of the post's figures is used. ChatGPT's replies named at least one brand, product or company in 12.1% of conversations. Fishkin's verdict is blunt: ChatGPT is "a heavily-consumer app," and "the competition with Google has been massively overblown by marketing press and influencers."
In Short
SparkToro studied about 42,000 ChatGPT conversations from a US panel and found that only 6.9% involved someone researching a purchase, while 12.1% of ChatGPT's answers named a brand, product or company. For brands paying to be named in AI answers, or buying ChatGPT ads, that makes the commercial slice of ChatGPT much smaller than the one SparkToro previously measured on Google, where more than 15% of queries met the same test. The panel leaves out API traffic, likely under-counts desktop and work use, and some of the post's own figures disagree with each other, so the study describes what consumers ask far better than how much they ask.
Where the data comes from
Verso's dataset comes from an opt-in panel of US ChatGPT users who consented to share their chat history. According to the post, Verso co-founder Aaron Aknin approached Fishkin and supplied 118,163 prompts across 41,961 complete, anonymized conversations. Verso collected rich demographic data - gender, age, household income in bands and region - for most of the users.
The heaviest users were taken out first. "Before analyzing anything, we removed the 131 heaviest users, anyone who averaged more than 33 prompts per active month," the methodology states. "They were only 1% of panelists but sent 21% of all prompts, so leaving them in would have made the results describe a small group of power users rather than typical people." The post is not fully consistent about whether the 12,321 figure includes those 131 people. Its methodology says the dataset covers 12,321 people and that the removal happened afterwards, while its statistics section describes the 12,321-user group as "the whole entity (minus the ones I removed)."
Most of the charts use a subset weighted to US Census figures rather than the full panel. "Like any opt-in panel, this one doesn't perfectly match the US population. It skews younger, female, and lower-income," according to the methodology. SparkToro weighted the 9,675 panelists who had demographic records to 2024 US Census targets for age, gender, region, education and household income, and found that "No category moved by more than 1.4 percentage points." The captions on the charts list age, gender, region and household income, without education.
Two limits shape every figure. The panel cannot see requests made through OpenAI's API, which developers use to build OpenAI's models into their own software. And "Most panelists were recorded on phones, so desktop and work-account use is likely underrepresented (though we controlled for this using estimation)," the post states. The panelists were also light users. The full panel averaged 3.4 conversations and 9.6 prompts per person over ten months - by PPC Land's calculation, roughly one conversation every three months. Fishkin acknowledges that the panel "uses ChatGPT far less often" than ChatGPT's own claim of 330 million prompts a day in the US would suggest, and the methodology concludes that "this panel tells us what people ask ChatGPT far better than how much they use it."
What Americans ask ChatGPT
SparkToro sorted every conversation into one of 12 categories. Images and visual design covers editing uploaded photos, creating pictures from descriptions and designing flyers, logos, invitations and tattoos - including, in Fishkin's words, "the much-hated AI-generated posters and sandwich boards every mediocre bar in America seems to be using." Information and entertainment gathers curiosity questions, films, music, sports scores, news, betting picks, astrology and tarot. Everyday life and admin runs from car trouble and recipes to court dates, immigration paperwork and tenant rights.
Health and wellness spans symptoms, medications, lab results, diet and sleep. Money, work and business covers banking, loans, taxes, investing, job hunting and running a small business. Shopping, brands and local services is the category closest to search advertising: deciding what to buy or where to go, comparing products, finding a nearby business and its opening hours. Relationships and emotional support includes venting, loneliness and "using ChatGPT as a sounding board or stand-in therapist."
The two columns do not match. Everyday life and admin is 12.2% in the text and 10.3% on the chart, and health 10.3% against 8.5%. The order changes too: the text puts everyday life third, the chart puts writing third. The text describes the data as per-user, while the chart caption describes "the percent of AI conversations." According to the methodology, SparkToro calculated results both ways and "The two views tell essentially the same story," but the post does not say which set of figures comes from which view. The broad picture survives either reading: images first, shopping in the bottom third, coding near 4% to 5%.
Tech workers stand apart
SparkToro also isolated a group it labeled tech workers, identified by combining their conversations with their demographic data. Only 70 people met that definition, about 0.6% of the panel by PPC Land's calculation, and they generated 638 conversations. On PPC Land's reading of the bar chart, technology support and coding took about 32% of their conversations, against 4.7% for the census-weighted group. They asked far less about schoolwork (under 3%) and relationships (under 1%), and made fewer image requests, about 9.5%. Fishkin's explanation for the low relationships figure is speculative: "perhaps they realize the AI firms may not always keep their data private."
The coding share for ordinary users is a long way from earlier measurements. A 2023 SparkToro clickstream study, which the post links, was titled "We Analyzed Millions of ChatGPT User Sessions: Visits are Down 29% since May, Programming Assistance is 30% of Use." OpenAI's own data shows a similar slide: technical help fell from 13.5% to 4.6% of consumer ChatGPT requests between July 2024 and September 2026, with OpenAI's coding agent Codex excluded from those figures.
Brands in prompts and in replies
SparkToro's classifier tagged whether each prompt asked for brand, product or company information, and whether ChatGPT's answer contained any.
"No surprise, the responses contained a brand recommendation a little more often than the user requested it," Fishkin wrote. That holds for multiple brands - 7.0% of replies against 6.6% of prompts. Across both categories, prompts seeking brand information totaled 13.3% by PPC Land's calculation, slightly more than the 12.1% of replies that named at least one. The methodology adds a caveat on the prompt side: "The second rater flagged brand questions more often than the first, so our brand-question figure is probably conservative."
Fishkin set those numbers against OpenAI's published category of "seeking information," which he considers far broader than genuine brand demand. "I think they intentionally make their categories very broad to promote a belief that ChatGPT has more brand-related demand than it does," he wrote - his interpretation, which the post does not support with evidence about OpenAI's intent. He still treats the share as meaningful: "Still, 12.1% of responses containing one or more brands/products/companies is nothing to shake a stick at, especially given ChatGPT claims to have 330M prompts per day in the US." The post does not say where the 330 million figure comes from.
Purchase intent and the Google comparison
The last test was the one that matters most to advertisers. SparkToro classified each conversation for purchase intent in three groups. Conversations with no purchase intent included "make an illustration of my gnome wizard for my D&D game." Intent to buy while researching options covered prompts such as "best running shoe options for 50 year old men with plantar fasciitis." The third group analyzed a single brand, product or company with purchase-relevant questions.
Fishkin framed the question in terms of Google's business. "If ChatGPT has a lot of buyer-journey research, maybe it is displacing Google, and maybe its nascent advertising business has real potential," he wrote. His answer: "I suspect this is bad news for ChatGPT's advertising opportunities."
The comparison rests on SparkToro's earlier analysis of 332 million Google queries, conducted about two years ago, in which more than 15% matched the same definition. Fishkin then says Google is 5.5 times bigger than ChatGPT in total searches against prompts and about 2.8 times bigger in the share of searches with purchase intent. "Conservatively, I'd guess that people ask Google purchase-journey-related queries at 16X+ the rate of ChatGPT," he wrote, adding: "That market share number isn't dissimilar to the Google:Bing ratio."
The arithmetic deserves a closer look. Dividing 15% by 6.9% gives about 2.2, not 2.8, by PPC Land's calculation; the post does not show how the higher ratio was derived, though it describes the 15% as a floor. Nor does it give a source for the 5.5 times figure. Multiplying 5.5 by 2.8 produces 15.4, which Fishkin rounds to "16X+." Earlier estimates PPC Land has reported vary widely: Ahrefs said in February that ChatGPT handled the equivalent of 12% of Google's search volume while sending 190 times less traffic to websites, and SparkToro's own research with Datos, published in March, put Google at 73.7% of US desktop search activity in the fourth quarter of 2025, with Amazon, Bing and YouTube each ahead of ChatGPT.
OpenAI has described commercial demand differently. A working paper by Kaiser and Schulze, which PPC Land covered on November 2, relied on OpenAI's comments at Cannes, as reported by Marketing Dive, that a fifth of prompts carry commercial intent. "Commercial intent" and SparkToro's purchase classification are not the same measure, and neither party has published the two side by side. Fishkin's own conclusions add a third number: "That said, 12% of ChatGPT usage is part of the buyer journey." The post does not explain how that 12% relates to the 6.9% purchase-intent share; it is close to the 12.1% of replies that named a brand.
