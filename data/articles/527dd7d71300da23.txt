TITLE: ‘Shocking’ drop in hep B vaccination, including babies with infected mothers
OUTLET: NBC News

Fewer newborns are being vaccinated against hepatitis B, an incurable infection that can lead to liver disease, including babies whose mothers are known to have the virus, a large new analysis has found.
As of June, just 66% of newborns got the hepatitis B vaccine within a day of their birth, down from 80% in 2022. The birth dose serves as a safety net especially for babies whose pregnant mothers are unaware they’re infected with the virus and unknowingly transmit it during childbirth.
Up to 90% of babies with the virus develop chronic infections, putting them at risk for liver failure and liver cancer later in life. Vaccinating them at birth dramatically lowers those odds.
The new study from Truveta, a health care and analytics company, included medical records from more than 1.3 million babies born in the United States from January 2018 through June this year.
During that time period, newborn vaccination rates increased from 76% in 2018 to 80% in 2022 before declining in mid-2023, falling to 66% by June this year.
“It’s shocking to see a nearly 15% decline in three years,” said Nina Masters, lead author and principal applied research scientist at Truveta. “Infants born to mothers with hepatitis B have a 90% chance of perinatal infection. That can mean a lifetime of chronic liver disease, cirrhosis and early death. This is a real preventable tragedy.”
The decline began before federal vaccine advisers voted in December to abandon a decades-old recommendation that all babies be vaccinated against hepatitis B at birth. (That decision was later halted by a federal judge in response to a lawsuit filed by the American Academy of Pediatrics. The block remains in effect.)
The findings mirror increasing trends in anti-vaccine behavior in general. A recent NBC News data investigation found three-quarters of U.S. counties that track kindergarten immunization rates no longer meet the minimum threshold to prevent measles outbreaks.
Now, measles outbreaks are popping up nationwide, with nearly 6,000 cases and at least 13 deaths, according to state health departments. The U.S. lost its measles elimination status Nov. 16, reversing one of the country’s greatest public health achievements this century.
“We talk about measles being the canary in the coal mine. We’re seeing those outbreaks,” said Dr. Richard Besser, a pediatrician and president of the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation. “This one — hepatitis B and the rise in unvaccinated newborns — these are the kinds of things that are silent, that you don’t see for years and years until these babies grow up and end up having liver disease.”
The study, published Tuesday in NEJM Evidence, also revealed that hepatitis B vaccination rates have fallen among babies born to mothers known to be infected with the virus. Up to 98% of those babies were vaccinated between 2018 through 2025. But between January and June, that rate dropped to 94%.
Cases of acute hepatitis B fell dramatically in children after the the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention began recommending a universal birth dose in 1991, falling 99% from 1990 to 2019. A CDC analysis of children born from 1994 to 2023 estimated that hepatitis B vaccination prevented more than 6 million infections and nearly 1 million hospitalizations.
Last year, the University of Minnesota’s Vaccine Integrity Project published a review of more than 400 studies, finding no evidence of short- or long-term health problems from the hepatitis B shot after birth.
“Reversing the successes of previous public health programs puts individuals’ lives at risk,”
Dr. James Conway, a pediatric infectious disease physician and medical director of the UW Health Immunization Program in Madison, Wisconsin, wrote in an email. “It’s sad, tragic and preventable.”
