TITLE: Older adults' wisdom in age of technology: Research from SWPS University
OUTLET: EurekAlert!

What do smart technologies at home mean for older adults? Their resistance to new technologies does not result from a lack of skills, but rather from the fact that many of them need traditional items to preserve their identity and a sense of stability, researchers from SWPS University conclude in a paper published in the Polish Sociological Review.
In the age of demographic shifts, the concept of "aging in place" has emerged - the proposal that an older person should be able to live in their own home and familiar neighbourhood for as long as possible. While research suggests that "smart" home enhancements can alleviate the challenges of daily living, older generations are hesitant or resistant to implementing technological novelties into their everyday life practices, especially in the private spaces of their homes.
Aging in place
Sociologists Magdalena Romanowska (a doctoral candidate at the SWPS University Doctoral School) and Paula Pustułka, PhD, a professor at the SWPS University Faculty of Social Sciences in Warsaw, examined the role of technodigital objects in the homes of older adults in Warsaw in the context of the "aging in place" concept. The researchers focused on how older adults use cherished, traditional items (referred to as “wise” objects) together with (or instead of) smart technologies.
We employed a Participatory Action Research (PAR) methodology, which included in-depth interviews, virtual reality (VR) experiments, and participant photography where older adults themselves took photographs of their surroundings. The study involved older adults aged 66-90, and drew on data from 10 interviews, 6 VR recordings, and 180 photographs, describes the study author Magdalena Romanowska. The VR footage was produced in collaboration with the researcher by Piotr Szatyłowicz and Laura Sonik for the Centre for Intergenerational Activity. The findings were published in the Polish Sociological Review.
In the homes of older adults: Analogue, smart, and nostalgic objects
The researchers identified three categories of items significant to the participants: analogue and smart appliances, medical equipment, and nostalgic objects.
Modern home appliances such as dishwashers, robotic vacuum cleaners, and kitchen aids are used by people of all ages, including older adults. A common barrier to owning such items, however, is limited living space. Many older adults in Warsaw live in dwellings that are extremely small and lack the room for certain appliances, such as a dishwasher. I only have a fridge and a washing machine (…). I’d love to use [a dishwasher], but I have no place to put it. Besides, now that I live alone, I don’t really need a dishwasher because I eat my meals here [at the Warsaw intergenerational centre], says Jan (76), a study participant.
Many participants use traditional alarm clocks and wristwatches; this is especially true for those who do not use smartphones. However, attitudes toward new technologies are evolving. I don't use the Internet, but when I need it, I sit down and my grandson explains it. (…) I have Internet on my phone. (…) I've mastered the phone, the smartphone, and the Internet somehow. I prefer books, though, describes another participant, Krystyna (75).
The second important category of objects concerns medical equipment that appears in people's homes as they get older. Older adults view their health and safety as a personal responsibility, and do their best to avoid becoming a burden on their loved ones. Ramps, walkers, rehabilitation equipment, medical alert bracelets, etc., support remaining in good health and overcoming physical barriers. The most common medical objects used by study participants were items associated with worsening vision, while the "smartest" item in the study was an SOS emergency wristband worn by Genowefa (90).
The study participants use various tools to help them maintain good memory. These include board games, solving puzzles, and reading printed newspapers.
The final category includes nostalgic objects referred to as "wise". Old furniture, crystal glassware, or newspaper clippings constitute objects of sentimental value for older adults. They help them preserve their own identity and serve as bridges connecting the present and the past. Many older adults use them to pass on their values to younger generations. I think that seniors, in their adult lives, return to their youth, so they are very attached to these things and can’t get rid of them. (...) Seniors think that all the stuff they’ve accumulated over seventy years of their lives will be useful to us, the younger ones, like their grandchildren. But that’s not true, says Gabriela (69).
Sentimental objects as an expression of generational wisdom
According to the sociologists, the rejection of certain digital innovations by older adults is, in a social sense, linked to a desire to maintain autonomy, and to rely on knowledge gained from their own many years of experience. In an age of enthusiasm for artificial intelligence, this stance gives us food for thought. While some older adults avoid modern technologies due to a lack of skills, for others this attitude results from the conviction that delegating daily tasks to algorithms too readily could lead to a loss of independent thinking and analytical capacity.
For older adults, navigating daily life wisely means using technology where it is genuinely helpful, while simultaneously preserving their autonomy and maintaining a certain dose of criticism towards the ubiquitous digital innovations, Magdalena Romanowska concludes.
