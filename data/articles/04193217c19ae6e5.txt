TITLE: OpenAI publishes its models' perplexity scores and proposes industry-wide limits on AI development

OpenAI publishes its models' perplexity scores and proposes industry-wide limits on AI development
- OpenAI published log-perplexity figures on its internal held-out dataset: about 0.99 for GPT-3.5, about 0.91 for GPT-4 and below 0.8 for its latest models; lower means the model predicts text better [1].
- Chief scientist Jakub Pachocki proposed "common limits," based on perplexity and other measurements, that frontier developers would commit not to cross until their ability to understand and monitor AI behavior reaches a concrete milestone, such as a successor to chain-of-thought monitors [1].
- He wrote that a model at about 0.76 started producing "groundbreaking results" on open math problems, and that a similar "avalanche" of pretraining advances could come in deep learning research "as soon as 2027" [1].
- OpenAI expects further "transformative capabilities" in a 0.68 to 0.7 log-perplexity window and said it does not know what they will be [1].
- The post lists the metric's weaknesses: labs use different datasets, perplexity does not map to a real task, it misses gains from reasoning, and its floor is unknown [1].
OpenAI on Sunday, Nov. 22, published internal measurements of how well its models predict text and asked rival labs to adopt the same metric as the basis for shared limits on AI development. In a post titled "Perplexity and Intelligence," chief scientist Jakub Pachocki proposed an industry standard that sets a first cap on development, measured across labs, and spells out what safety work must be done before anyone goes past it [1].
Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next token, or word fragment, on text it has not seen; lower perplexity means better predictions [1]. Pachocki wrote that publishing the numbers "gives competitors a clearer picture of where we stand," but that the concern "is outweighed by the urgency of establishing shared safety measures across the industry" [1].
The numbers OpenAI disclosed
OpenAI reports log-perplexity, also called cross-entropy loss, on a held-out dataset drawn from its own internal codebase, the measure it has tracked since GPT-3.5 [1]. The post gives these readings and the capabilities OpenAI ties to them [1]:
GPT-3.5 was the basis for the original ChatGPT, and GPT-4 was good enough at chain-of-thought reasoning that OpenAI built its reasoning models on top of it, according to the post [1]. Below 0.8, Pachocki wrote, OpenAI has seen "the emergence of transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics," and he stressed that nobody knew in advance those capabilities would appear at those levels [1].
The post's chart shows a steep drop in loss during 2026. OpenAI attributes most of that to long-term research and compute investment and "only in a smaller part" to automation, though it says AI has already made "meaningful design contributions" to its most recent models [1].
Why OpenAI says 2027 matters
Pachocki wrote that OpenAI has been posing open math problems to its models for more than a year, and that once it upgraded to a model at about 0.76, "it started producing groundbreaking results," with solutions arriving "one after another in a single Slack thread" [1].
He argued that the same could happen in algorithmic research on pretraining, and that "as soon as 2027 we could conceivably see a similar avalanche of fundamental pretraining advancements" [1]. He expects AI to help with alignment and monitoring as well, but wrote that this progress "is unlikely to be fast enough to allow the full potential acceleration in pretraining" [1].
The post also warns of "further degradation" in current alignment auditing methods and more evaluation and training awareness, meaning models that recognize when they are being tested [1]. Pachocki called full recursive self-improvement, with no human oversight, a state "we should obviously avoid" [1].
What the proposal asks of labs
Pachocki proposed that frontier developers jointly set norms for evaluating models in the 0.68 to 0.7 range and red-team their safety cases by the time such models are first used, along with shared transparency standards [1]. On top of that, he called for "common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements," that developers commit not to cross until monitoring reaches a milestone such as a successor to chain-of-thought monitors, and for a framework of successive limits after that [1]. The post does not name a specific cap or say who would enforce it.
OpenAI lists four problems with the metric. Labs train on different data and evaluate on different datasets, so numbers are hard to compare. Perplexity does not correspond to a task anyone cares about directly. Chain-of-thought reasoning raises capability without lowering perplexity. And no one knows the dataset's inherent entropy, so gains will look smaller as models approach it [1]. OpenAI's own figures come from code, which the post concedes is a poor proxy for the general abilities of older models such as GPT-2 and GPT-3 [1].
The proposal follows a week of safety disclosures and policy moves. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman called for shared safety standards across labs on Nov. 18 (our report), a bipartisan House group outlined the SAFE AI Act, which would require frontier labs to file safety frameworks (our report), and METR said frontier models can now finish tasks that take humans about a work week (our report).
