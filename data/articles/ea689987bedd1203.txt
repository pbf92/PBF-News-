TITLE: Theta-burst brain stimulation shows preliminary functional gains in spinal cord injury
OUTLET: Bioengineer.org

A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating non-invasive brain stimulation has found that intermittent theta-burst stimulation, commonly known as iTBS, may offer preliminary benefits for corticospinal excitability and selected daily functional activities in adults living with spinal cord injury. The comprehensive analysis, conducted by Shiyao Chang and colleagues at Ningbo Rehabilitation Hospital and published in Frontiers in Neurology, synthesized evidence across seven randomized controlled trials comprising 254 unique human participants. Across eligible comparisons contrasting active magnetic stimulation against sham control procedures, the pooled data demonstrated statistically significant immediate post-intervention gains in motor-evoked potential amplitude, lower-extremity motor scores, and standardized measures of daily life functioning. However, the investigators caution that the overall certainty of evidence remains low to very low across evaluated endpoints, that individual trials exhibited substantial procedural and anatomical heterogeneity, and that pain-relief findings were conflicting and insufficient to establish a reliable therapeutic effect. Consequently, the research team emphasizes that current findings must be regarded as preliminary and exploratory rather than definitive justification for routine clinical adoption.
Spinal cord injury induces profound disruption of central neural pathways, frequently resulting in enduring sensorimotor paralysis, autonomic nervous system dysfunction, and debilitating neuropathic pain syndromes. Non-invasive brain stimulation techniques, particularly repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, can modulate the excitability of human motor-cortical networks over extended timeframes. Within this framework, intermittent theta-burst stimulation was originally introduced by Huang and colleagues in 2005 as a patterned magnetic protocol delivering bursts of three 50-hertz pulses repeated at a five-hertz theta rhythm, administering 600 total pulses over approximately 190 seconds. Compared to conventional ten-hertz repetitive stimulation protocols that often require nearly forty minutes per clinical session, the three-minute delivery profile of iTBS offers substantially greater practical efficiency while engaging NMDA-receptor-dependent neuroplasticity and regulating cortical intracortical inhibitory networks.
To rigorously examine whether these neurophysiological mechanisms yield tangible clinical rehabilitation outcomes, the review team conducted a comprehensive database search spanning PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and PEDro from their respective inceptions through January 20, 2026. Designed in concordance with PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines and prospectively registered on the PROSPERO international database under identifier CRD420261301386, the screening protocol applied no restrictions regarding publication language or geographic region. The search focused exclusively on randomized controlled trials evaluating adult spinal cord injury patients where active motor-cortex or neural-target iTBS was isolated against sham procedures. From an initial identification of 118 bibliographic records, 43 duplicate entries were eliminated, and 75 abstracts were scrutinized. Following full-text retrieval of 16 candidate reports, nine were excluded due to non-randomized designs, single-case formats, healthy cohort compositions, animal experimentation, conventional stimulation protocols, non-painful sensory outcomes, or unaligned electrophysiological parameters, while an initial misclassification of a trial by Shi and colleagues was corrected, leaving seven eligible randomized controlled trials for quantitative and qualitative synthesis.
The seven included randomized trials encompassed clinical investigations conducted across mainland China, Taiwan, the Republic of Korea, the United Kingdom, and Austria. Across these investigations, treatment courses varied widely in dose and duration, spanning from five focused sessions to comprehensive nine-week rehabilitation regimens. In trials such as those by Deng, Feng, and Shi, daily stimulation was paired directly with conventional physical therapy, whereas multi-arm trials, such as Lin’s study, were isolated to identical sham background interventions to preserve direct experimental contrast.
Because clinical and neurophysiological measurements varied widely across trials, the authors implemented generic inverse-variance random-effects meta-analyses using Review Manager 5.4 alongside specialized computational scripts in Python. Continuous outcome variables assessed across disparate measurement instruments were harmonized as standardized mean differences alongside corresponding 95 percent confidence intervals, with positive analytical directions configured to favor active stimulation. To prevent artificial inflation of statistical sample sizes, participants in crossover trials were counted only once, and paired effect sizes were computed assuming a conservative between-condition correlation of 0.5. Where trial publications failed to report exact change-score standard deviations, these values were statistically imputed from baseline and post-intervention variances, while median and quartile distributions from Deng were converted to parametric equivalents using established mathematical algorithms by Wan and colleagues. Furthermore, graphical data presented as standard error bars in the trial by Feng were converted into standard deviations to correct historical variance underestimations and eliminate false statistical heterogeneity.
In the quantitative synthesis of corticospinal excitability, three randomized controlled trials involving 46 unique participants contributed motor-evoked potential amplitude measurements. The revised random-effects pooling revealed a statistically significant increase in motor-evoked potential amplitude favoring active iTBS compared to sham stimulation, yielding a standardized mean difference of 0.73 with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning from 0.12 to 1.33 and a significance value of p equals 0.018. Statistical heterogeneity across this neurophysiological outcome remained low, displaying an I-squared statistic of 20 percent. The authors noted that this electrophysiological enhancement aligns with the theoretical hypothesis of cortical priming, in which transiently elevated corticospinal excitability may prime motor pathways for subsequent physical rehabilitation. Nevertheless, the review authors emphasized that motor-evoked potential amplitudes reflect complex composite interactions between cortical, spinal, and peripheral motor units rather than direct structural preservation of anatomical descending spinal tracts, warning that the small combined participant sample precludes sweeping neurobiological conclusions regarding preserved spinal pathway integrity.
Evaluating lower-limb functional recovery, four eligible studies encompassing 216 unique participants evaluated the Lower Extremity Motor Score, commonly abbreviated as LEMS. Initial random-effects synthesis indicated a statistically significant improvement favoring active theta-burst intervention, generating a pooled standardized mean difference of 0.70 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.19 to 1.21 and a significance value of p equals 0.007. However, this motor analysis exhibited substantial between-study statistical heterogeneity, quantified by an I-squared statistic of 68 percent. Crucially, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated that this outcome was overwhelmingly driven by a single large trial conducted by Shi and colleagues, which contributed 138 participants and over 32 percent of the pooled analytical weight. When the researchers omitted Shi’s trial from the statistical model, the effect size attenuated to a standardized mean difference of 0.63, with its 95 percent confidence interval widening from negative 0.14 to positive 1.40, crossing the threshold of no effect with a non-significant p-value of 0.111. This sensitivity led the authors to explicitly classify the pooled lower-extremity motor improvement as exploratory.
Assessment of broader daily functional autonomy was conducted across four trials comprising 216 unique participants, utilizing either the Spinal Cord Independence Measure in studies by Deng and Gharooni or the Modified Barthel Index in investigations by Feng and Shi. The synthesized random-effects model favored active stimulation with a standardized mean difference of 0.47, a 95 percent confidence interval between 0.22 and 0.73, and a high significance level of p less than 0.001. In contrast to lower-limb motor scores, this functional activities analysis showed an I-squared statistic of zero percent, indicating complete absence of observed statistical heterogeneity. Importantly, sensitivity testing confirmed that the positive functional effect remained statistically reliable even after excluding the large Shi trial, yielding a revised standardized mean difference of 0.51 with a confidence interval between 0.12 and 0.90. Nevertheless, the authors observed that standardized functional independence scales reflect composite accommodations, including compensatory strategies, environmental factors, and caregiver assistance, meaning that gains cannot be definitively attributed to direct neurophysiological restitution of damaged pathways.
In marked contrast to motor and functional metrics, intermittent theta-burst stimulation demonstrated highly inconsistent and inconclusive results regarding neuropathic pain relief. Only two randomized controlled trials, representing 32 unique individuals, evaluated clinical pain intensity, and their differing measurement tools and opposing findings precluded quantitative pooling. In a crossover feasibility study by Gharooni and colleagues, visual analogue scale ratings of pain yielded a baseline-adjusted intervention effect of negative 0.02 with a wide 95 percent confidence interval from negative 19.35 to positive 19.31, providing no discernible evidence of clinical analgesia. Conversely, a parallel-group trial by Kim and colleagues reported substantial reductions in pain intensity, documenting a decrease of 3.18 points on the Numeric Rating Scale following active stimulation compared to 0.45 points following sham intervention, alongside corresponding drops on the separate Neuropathic Pain Scale. The authors noted that post-injury neuropathic pain involves intricate supraspinal central sensitization and altered thalamocortical dynamics, concluding that existing clinical evidence fails to establish a reliable analgesic benefit across broader spinal cord injury cohorts.
Formal quality evaluation using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool revealed persistent methodological limitations across the evidence base, with common ambiguities surrounding allocation concealment, investigator blinding, and selective reporting verification. When evaluated under the GRADE framework, certainty of evidence was categorized as low for motor-evoked potential amplitude and activities of daily living, and downgraded to very low for lower-extremity motor scores and neuropathic pain due to risk of bias, small sample sizes, and statistical or clinical inconsistency. Furthermore, the systematic search omitted unpublished gray literature and clinical trial registries, and most included studies restricted clinical assessments to immediate post-treatment follow-up without verifying long-term therapeutic durability. Consequently, the authors conclude that while intermittent theta-burst stimulation remains a biologically plausible and time-efficient neurorehabilitation modality, well-designed, adequately blinded multicenter randomized trials utilizing standardized stimulation protocols, explicit coil architectures, and extended longitudinal monitoring are essential before clinical recommendations can be responsibly formulated.
