TITLE: Meta's Muse reaches Australia with 9 local partners, from Canva to Kogan
OUTLET: PPC Land

Meta made its Muse AI agent available in Australia on November 17, 2026, ten weeks after the product first went live in the United States and three weeks after it reached Singapore. Australians can use it on iOS, Android, the muse.ai website and a Mac app, according to Meta's announcement, with access through AI glasses described as "coming soon." Nine Australian companies are named as early partners, among them the online retailer Kogan, the beauty chain MECCA, Officeworks and both of the country's big property portals, Domain and realestate.com.au. When each of them will actually work inside the agent, Meta did not say.
In Short
Meta's Muse, an AI app that can send emails, book restaurants and buy things for you once you link your accounts, went live in Australia on November 17, 2026. It matters for Australian retailers and the people who shop with them, because nine local companies, including Kogan, MECCA, Officeworks, Ticketek and two property portals, have signed up to plug their services into the agent, while Amazon, Coles and Woolworths are not on the list. What changes is that a Meta app can now sit between some Australian shoppers and the businesses they buy from just as the festive season starts, although Meta has not said when each partner's features will be ready.
What Meta switched on in Australia
Meta describes Muse as a personal AI agent that "doesn't just answer questions, it actually does the work." The Australian announcement carries the headline "Muse Is Now Available in Australia: The World's First Personal AI Agent Built for Everyone," the same "world's first" billing Meta used for the US debut on September 8 and for Singapore on October 27. The post offers no measure of what makes Muse the first, and it does not compare the product with agents sold by other companies. Its opening line is plainer: "Following our global announcement of Muse, we are excited to share that Muse is now available in Australia."
The commercial structure is unchanged from the US. When Meta released Muse in the US, it was free for most use, with paid plans for heavier users. The Australian post repeats the model: "It's free for most of what people need, with subscription plans for people who want to do more." No Australian-dollar prices are given, and nothing in the post says which features sit behind a subscription.
What lets Muse act on a person's behalf is a set of connectors, links into services the person already uses. "Connectors are a big reason Muse can do so much," Meta writes. Four general services are named for Australian users - Gmail, Google Calendar, Instagram and OpenTable - so that Muse can "manage emails, coordinate schedules, draw on saved content and make a restaurant reservation."
The device list differs a little from earlier launches. The Australian post names a Mac app next to iOS, Android and muse.ai. The US launch in September also put Muse inside WhatsApp; the Australian post does not mention WhatsApp at all. On glasses, the wording is looser than Meta's earlier timetable. On October 21, Mark Zuckerberg told analysts that Muse would come to Meta's glasses the following month. Four weeks later, the Australian post still describes glasses as "coming soon."
Nine local partners, no fixed dates
Meta names nine "local connector partners" for Australia: Canva, Domain, Kogan, MECCA, Officeworks, realestate.com.au, SWEAT, ShopBack and Ticketek. The rollout is staggered. According to the post, "Connector features will roll out progressively, with some experiences becoming available after launch." Which of the nine worked on November 17, and which come later, is not stated.
The five areas are the categories listed in Meta's post. The assignment of each partner to an area is PPC Land's reading of the examples Meta gives.
The wording is conditional throughout. Each example is introduced with "As these experiences become available," and each uses "could": Muse could design with Canva, look at listings on Domain and realestate.com.au, help with fitness goals through SWEAT, or buy from Kogan.com, MECCA, Officeworks and Ticketek. Only the ShopBack example points beyond a single store: "With ShopBack, you could compare prices and earn cashback and rewards on flights, hotels and shopping."
Canva is the one partner with global reach. According to the post, it "is also available as a connector for Muse users around the world." That is not new for US businesses: when Meta connected Muse to ad accounts and 15 business tools for small firms in the US and Canada, Canva sat on the design list alongside Figma and Lovable.
The absences say as much as the names. Amazon, which said on September 20 that it had cut Muse off from buying on Amazon.com, is not among the nine, and GeekWire reported on November 17 that the instructions behind Muse's US shopping skill tell the agent to leave Amazon out of its searches unless a user asks for it. Neither of Australia's two largest supermarket groups, Coles and Woolworths, appears. There is no food delivery service and no airline. For travel, the only route Meta names is ShopBack's cashback on flights and hotels.
Partner signings continue elsewhere. On November 18, a day after the Australian launch, Meta named Loblaw as its first Canadian retail partner for Muse.
Australia against Singapore
Singapore came three weeks earlier. Comparing the two Meta posts shows how far the local line-ups differ.
ShopBack is the only company on both lists. Singapore's set leaned on travel and delivery, with Singapore Airlines, Agoda, Klook, Trip.com and Grab, plus the marketplaces Shopee and Lazada. Australia's swaps travel for property, fitness and ticketing, and its retail partners are specialist chains rather than marketplaces.
Voice and spaces from the first day
Muse learned to talk on November 5, but Meta limited the voice and avatar rollout to the US at first, leaving out Canadian users even though Muse was already available in Canada. Twelve days later, the Australian post states: "Real time conversations with your Muse are now possible on mobile, web or Mac." The mechanics match the US version - "Just tap the phone icon in the top-right corner to start talking." Not mentioned are the animated avatars that accompanied the US voice launch, or the number of voices on offer.
Spaces arrived a day after voice. Meta added spaces on November 6 as custom tools built from a written description, refreshed in the background with data from connected apps. In the Australian post, spaces are "a custom experience you create inside Muse by describing your idea," with a shopping hub, a school dashboard, a wedding planner and a trip planner offered as examples.
For a market outside North America, getting both features at launch reverses the earlier order. Singapore's post went up before either feature existed. Meta's voice announcement named only the US, and whether Canadian and Singaporean users have voice now, the Australian post does not say.
How Meta describes the safeguards
Meta says safety took most of the work. "Making this technology work for everyone requires careful design and engineering to operate more safely. That's where most of the effort on this project went," according to the post. It sets out the arrangement in five parts:
- A person connects Muse to services such as Gmail, Google Calendar, Instagram and OpenTable, and, as their features become available, to the nine local partners.
- Muse runs on Muse Secure VM, which the post describes as "a dedicated computer in the cloud that securely stores the data and credentials for the services you connect."
- That system keeps "passwords and payment methods out of the agent's view."
- The person sets the scope of access. In Meta's words, "You choose what your Muse can access."
- Muse asks before certain actions: "it checks with you before sensitive actions such as sending an email or making a purchase."
The post links to a longer explanation on Meta's research site and to a security page at security.muse.ai. Some detail from September is not repeated. At the US launch, Meta described a separate program, Sentinel, that must approve outbound traffic, and said that conversations and the data inside each Muse Secure VM are not shared with its ad systems. The Australian post says nothing about advertising, so whether that commitment applies unchanged in Australia is left unstated.
Age is also missing. The post sets no minimum. Meta's research material describes Muse as a service for people aged 18 and over, which would put it above the threshold of Australia's under-16 social media law, in force since December 10, 2025. Meta has had to apply that law across its apps: it removed access to 756,000 Facebook and Instagram accounts between December 1, 2025 and June 30, 2026. The Muse post refers neither to the law nor to any age check.
A pitch timed for the sales season
Meta's local executive tied the launch to the calendar. Will Easton, Managing Director, Meta Australia and New Zealand, said: "With the festive season coming up, the timing couldn't be better. From finding and buying the perfect gift to planning and booking a holiday, Muse is designed to take work off Australians' plates. As local experiences roll out, familiar services will make Muse even more useful in everyday life. And people stay in control, including choosing what Muse can access and approving purchases before they happen."
The season he refers to is also a period of regulatory attention. Black Friday falls on November 27 this year. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission opened its third annual sweep of Black Friday and Cyber Monday advertising on November 10, 2026, extending its checks to promotional emails and social media, after a 2025 sweep aimed at countdown timers, sitewide sale claims and strikethrough pricing.
Why it matters for marketers
In the US, Muse has become a large presence in automated traffic quickly. HUMAN Security's October data put Muse at 59% of all agentic traffic on the sites it protects, Sensor Tower estimated 10 million US downloads within 40 days, and Zuckerberg said in October that more than 10 million people in the US and Canada had set up a Muse agent. Those numbers come from North America. No Australian figures are given.
How the agent picks merchants has been the central question for retailers. An instruction file extracted from Muse by Profound, and not confirmed by Meta, required the agent to fill its top five picks with well-known merchants. For Australian retailers, the launch brings that question home. In agentic commerce, where software finds, compares and pays for goods on a person's behalf, a direct integration gives a merchant a defined route into the agent, rather than leaving it to depend on how the agent searches the open web. Meta has now named which Australian businesses have such an integration, at least on paper. How Muse treats Australian retailers that are not partners - whether it browses their sites, ranks them or leaves them out - the post does not explain.
The choice of countries is itself a signal of cost. Zuckerberg said in October that Muse is compute-intensive to run and that this is why it had not launched globally. Each new market, on that account, is a decision about where to spend data centre capacity. Its two most recent launches have both been in Asia-Pacific. The region matters to Meta's advertising business mainly through volume: in the third quarter, Asia-Pacific advertising revenue was $11.53 billion, against $10.02 billion a year earlier, with impressions up 14% and price per ad up 1%.
What the post leaves out
The post gives no date for any individual partner connector, no Australian-dollar subscription prices, no age requirement and no usage figures. It does not mention WhatsApp, avatars or advertising, and it does not say how Muse treats Australian merchants outside the partner list.
