TITLE: Amazon counts a shopper's rating as the click advertisers pay for
OUTLET: PPC Land

Amazon Ads has defined what advertisers pay for in Review Requests, its beta ad format that asks recent buyers to rate a product, and the answer is a rating or review rather than a tap on an ad. "Review Requests uses cost-per-click (CPC) billing, where the billable click is a shopper leaving a rating or review on the card," according to the format's FAQ in the Amazon Ads API documentation. "There is no charge for impressions or for shoppers who see the card without leaving a rating or review," the FAQ adds. The page carries no date. It sits beside an overview and a get started guide that accompany the release note, dated November 16, 2026, that brought Review Requests to the Amazon Ads API in the United States.
In Short
Amazon's FAQ for its Review Requests ads says the "click" an advertiser pays for is a shopper leaving a rating or review on the "How was your item?" card, and that seeing the card costs nothing. This matters to US sellers and vendors with products under 1,000 reviews, because the bid they set is now defined as the most they will pay for one rating or review, which is a different thing from the price of a click on an ordinary Amazon product ad. Review counts are still visible only in the Amazon Ads console, while the reporting API, the feed software uses to pull Amazon ad reports, returns impressions, clicks, click-through rate, spend and cost per click for these campaigns already.
The question the FAQ answers
Review Requests is not new. Amazon Ads announced the format on September 29, 2026 as a card shown to recent buyers on what it calls its highest-traffic pages, limited to products with fewer than 1,000 ratings and reviews, and run "using the same bidding and budget controls" as other Amazon Ads campaigns. The September page said nothing about what triggers a charge.
The Amazon Ads API is the connection that lets software, rather than a person in the console, create and manage campaigns. The API release note dated November 16 gave the first answer: "the advertiser pays only when a shopper leaves a rating or review." PPC Land reported that note on November 12, ahead of that date.
Then the vocabulary split. The Amazon Ads Support Center pages updated on November 16 list cost per click (CPC) among the campaign metrics and say nothing about billing, while the API get started guide describes the ad group bid as "the default maximum bid per billable click." PPC Land's November 17 article noted that no Amazon document defined a click for this format, and that a cost per click and a cost per rating would be the same number only if the click Amazon bills were the rating itself.
The FAQ settles it. Its full answer to the question "How am I billed, and how do I control spend?" reads:
"Review Requests uses cost-per-click (CPC) billing, where the billable click is a shopper leaving a rating or review on the card. There is no charge for impressions or for shoppers who see the card without leaving a rating or review. Advertisers control spend with two settings: a bid (the maximum paid per billable click) and a daily budget (the cap on daily spend)."
In most search advertising, including Amazon's own Sponsored Products ads, a click is a tap that takes a shopper to a product page. In Review Requests the paid event is a completed action by someone who already owns the product: a star rating, or a written review.
What the bid and the budget now mean
With the billable event defined, the two spending controls read differently from their namesakes elsewhere in Amazon's advertising. The bid is a ceiling on what one rating or review can cost. The daily budget caps what a campaign spends on ratings and reviews in a day. Nothing is spent on shoppers who look at the card and move on.
Amazon's documentation publishes no price. The get started guide contains sample requests, and the figures in them are placeholders rather than minimums, defaults or typical costs.
The sample amounts carry no currency field; the marketplace in the request is the United States.
The definition also says nothing about the score. As written, the billable event is a shopper leaving a rating or review on the card, so a one-star rating and a five-star rating are the same event for billing purposes. A separate FAQ answer removes any lever over the result. "Review Requests invites verified purchasers to leave an honest review, and all submissions are subject to Amazon's Community Guidelines," it states. "Advertisers cannot select individual shoppers or influence the star rating a shopper gives."
That separation is the line Amazon has been drawing for years around reviews. Its rules already bar sellers from offering refunds or other compensation in exchange for removing feedback, a ban that buyers threatening sellers with negative feedback have put to the test. Review Requests is a paid route that Amazon runs itself: the advertiser pays for the invitation to be answered, Amazon decides who is asked, and on the FAQ's wording the score does not affect the price.
What the reporting API returns for now
The release note said only that review-specific metrics would follow in the reporting API "in a later beta update." The FAQ says what is there in the meantime.
"Review performance for Review Requests campaigns is available in the Amazon Ads console. The Amazon Ads reporting API returns the standard delivery metrics (impressions, clicks, click-through rate, spend, cost-per-click) for Review Requests campaigns today; review-specific metrics in the reporting API will be added in a later beta update," it states. The overview page carries the same split: "Review-specific metrics in the Amazon Ads reporting API will be added in a later beta update; the standard delivery metrics are available today."
The FAQ does not say whether the "clicks" in those API reports are the billable clicks it defines. If they are, the clicks figure would count ratings and reviews collected, the cost per click would be the cost per rating or review, and the click-through rate would measure the share of card views that ended in a response. That reading fits the FAQ's definition, but Amazon has not stated it. The console splits "rating-only reviews" from "written reviews," and the API offers no such split yet.
Amazon's reporting tools have been moving for most of the year. Unified reporting in the Ads Console reached general availability in June 2026, while the Reporting API itself stayed in beta.
How the API sets up a campaign
According to Amazon's get started guide, a first Review Requests campaign takes five calls. The guide lists four conditions first: Amazon Ads API access with Login with Amazon access and refresh tokens; an Amazon-Ads-ClientId header and an Amazon-Advertising-API-Scope header set to the profile ID for the advertiser and marketplace; one or more eligible products "with fewer than 1,000 total reviews, in the United States"; and Review Requests enabled for the advertiser account. "During the beta, Review Requests is enabled per advertiser account. A request to create a Review Requests campaign for an account that is not yet enabled returns an error," the guide states.
- Create a campaign (POST /adsApi/v1/create/campaigns). The campaign holds the budget and bidding strategy. The goal is set to ENGAGEMENT "to identify it as a Review Requests campaign," the ad product to SPONSORED_PRODUCTS, the bid strategy to MANUAL, and automatic target creation is switched off in the sample. The call returns HTTP 207 with separate success and error lists.
- Create an ad group (POST /adsApi/v1/create/adGroups). "It requires no Review Requests-specific fields, and its bid sets the default maximum bid per billable click."
- Create the required target (POST /adsApi/v1/create/targets). "Every Review Requests ad group needs one target so the campaign can serve." The target type is THEME and the match type INTERESTED_AUDIENCE, which the guide calls "the fixed, required values for Review Requests."
- Create a Review Requests ad (POST /adsApi/v1/create/ads). The ad type is COMPONENT, with an action type of REVIEW_REQUEST and the product's ASIN, Amazon's catalogue number for the item. "Each ad covers a single product, and its action settings are fixed once created, so create one ad for each ASIN," the guide states.
- Query Review Requests campaigns (POST /adsApi/v1/query/campaigns). The request needs a goal filter of ENGAGEMENT. "Omitting the filter returns the other Sponsored Products campaigns only, so existing integrations are unaffected." The call returns HTTP 200, with a token for the next page when there are more results.
Rules the FAQ puts in writing
Several FAQ answers turn what the setup guide implies into stated rules.
The campaign type is permanent. "A campaign is a Review Requests campaign when optimizations.goalSettings.goal is set to ENGAGEMENT. This value is set when the campaign is created, and it cannot be changed afterward." A Review Requests campaign therefore cannot be turned into a regular Sponsored Products campaign, or the reverse.
There is no targeting. "Review Requests does not offer audience or keyword targeting," the FAQ states. "Keyword and product targets are not supported and return a targeting-type-not-supported error." The Support Center pages put the audience at buyers from 7 to 150 days after delivery; the API documents do not mention that window.
Existing campaigns are untouched. "Review Requests campaigns are separate from an advertiser's other campaigns and do not change how they run." Because they appear in query results only with the ENGAGEMENT filter, software that lists Sponsored Products campaigns will not see them unless it is changed to ask.
Eligibility is checked all the time. The overview's wording: "Review Requests is available for products with fewer than 1,000 total reviews. Eligibility is evaluated when a campaign is created and continuously while it runs. Review Requests ads for a product pause automatically once the product reaches 1,000 total reviews (star ratings and written reviews)." Since the cap counts star ratings and written reviews together, every response the campaign pays for also moves the product closer to the point where its ads stop.
No SKU. A SKU is a seller's own stock code for an item, while the ASIN is Amazon's identifier for the product listing. "Both vendors and sellers supply the ASIN in customerActionSettings.product.productId with productIdType: ASIN. A SKU is not required," according to the FAQ.
Why it matters
The FAQ names the unit of sale, not its price. Amazon has still published no cost per rating, and in October PPC Land noted that the only performance figure Amazon offered was its own claim that closed-beta products "received three times as many ratings and reviews per week compared to before the test," from a test run from June 3 to 21, 2026. What advertisers now know is what they buy: a response from one of Amazon's verified purchasers, at a price no higher than their bid, with no charge for exposure.
For agencies and software vendors, the definition has a bookkeeping consequence. On the FAQ's terms, the click a Review Requests advertiser pays for is a rating or review. If the cost per click in reports follows that definition, adding it into the same account totals as Sponsored Products campaigns, where a click is a visit to a product page, would blend prices for two different events. The query filter keeps the campaigns apart by default, but reporting that adds up spend and clicks across an account has no equivalent safeguard described in the documents.
The terms used across Amazon's own materials have not fully settled either. The release note and the FAQ speak of the "Amazon Ads console," while the Support Center pages refer to an Amazon Ads Agent account, the name Amazon gave its combined advertising platform on September 29.
