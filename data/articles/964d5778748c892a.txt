TITLE: Contemplative Prayer Is for Protestants Too
OUTLET: Christianity Today

It was a typical Monday night. My toddler was shrieking in her highchair, my dog was barking so ferociously there must have been an assassin at the door, and I was in the kitchen just trying to keep dinner from burning. My senses were overloaded, and I was tired.
I recalled Brother Lawrence, another Christian in a kitchen, who once wrote, “The time of busyness does not with me differ from the time of prayer, and in the noise and clatter of my kitchen, while several persons are at the same time calling for different things, I possess God in as great tranquility as if I were upon my knees before the Blessed Sacrament.”
In my kitchen, I felt nothing of the sort.
But I wondered: Is such a state of peace and communion with God really possible? If so, how? Brother Lawrence’s answer was contemplative prayer—the silent, wordless turning of one’s attention to God. I figured it was worth a try.
Contemplative prayer was born in the desert. Church historians generally credit the fourth-century desert monastics with formalizing it, but it has a long intellectual pedigree in both the East and the West, and no tradition owns it.
John Mark Comer and Dallas Willard were my introductions to the practice. Comer defines contemplative prayer as directing “the inner gaze of your heart at the Trinitarian community of love,” which is how he understands the admonition to “abide” in Christ (John 15:4, ESV). Willard called the act of constantly directing our minds to God “the with-God life,” and believed it was the secret to caring for our souls.
Contemplative prayer involves silence and an openness to God’s presence. We don’t speak. We merely sit with our Lord, “consciously aware,” as Richard Plass and James Cofield put it, of our “presence in Christ and attentive to Christ’s presence within [us].”
If this sounds mystical, that’s because it is—but in a sense Protestants already affirm. Paul speaks of Christ in us (Col. 1:27; Gal. 2:20), which Reformed theologians, following Calvin, have long called the “mystical union.” So if believing that Jesus dwells within me via the Holy Spirit makes me a mystic, then a mystic I am.
But the attractiveness of mystics, Comer explains, is that they “aren’t content to read books or hear sermons about this glorious reality; they want to experience this love and be transformed by it into people of love.”
That’s what I truly wanted: to know God, not simply know about him. I wanted to sit in his presence and be with him. Contemplative prayer seemingly offered the way.
However, many Protestants remain skeptical of this practice. A popular evangelical site even cautions against contemplative prayer and asserts that it has “no scriptural support whatsoever,” for prayer is “comprehendible communication with God,” not “mystical meditation.”
I find this line of thinking unconvincing for two reasons.
First, the Bible may not contain explicit instructions about contemplative prayer, but that doesn’t mean the practice lacks scriptural support. Paul instructs his recipients to “pray without ceasing” (1 Thess. 5:17). This is hard to imagine if prayer necessitates endless sentences sent heavenward. Additionally, in Luke 6:12, Jesus pulls an all-nighter in prayer—are we to assume this was a nonstop monologue? And Paul admits our prayers sometimes go beyond our words (Rom. 8:26). Moreover, themes of silence and stillness before God appear throughout the Psalms (Ps. 46:10, 62:1, 37:7).
The scriptural support for contemplative prayer may be implicit, but it’s not absent.
Second, even if contemplative prayer lacked scriptural support, that doesn’t mean we shouldn’t do it. Plenty of helpful spiritual practices aren’t in the Bible. Specific rhythms of fasting? Not in the Bible. Journaling? Not in there. Scripture memorization systems? Won’t find them (unless you count Deuteronomy 6:8-9). These are all beneficial practices to one’s spiritual life, and their absence from Scripture doesn’t make them any less helpful.
That’s not to say there is no danger to contemplative prayer, but the danger comes not from the act itself but from the posture we approach it in. We cannot delude ourselves into believing we’re responsible for experiencing God. There’s a great chasm between the type of being God is and the type of beings we are. If we encounter God, it won’t be because we ascended; it’ll be because he descended.
No matter how masterful we become at the technique of contemplative prayer, it’ll never be a tool that we use to produce intimacy with God. He must cross the chasm, not us. Contemplative prayer, therefore, is a means of opening ourselves up to God, to receive the gift of himself.
Brother Lawrence may have been able to achieve this openness in the clatter of his kitchen, but I had to start elsewhere.
It’s much quieter at 5:30 in the morning. The sun has not yet risen, my daughter and wife are still asleep, and work doesn’t begin until 6—an excellent time to pray. I sit at my desk, close my eyes, and breathe deeply three times. “Lord Jesus Christ,” I say to myself on the inhale. “Have mercy on me,” I say on the exhale. (This is known as the Jesus Prayer, borrowed from Eastern Orthodoxy.)
Then I say nothing.
And I sit.
At first, I play whack-a-mole with my thoughts, and I realize, as Henri Nouwen predicted, that my mind is like “a banana tree filled with monkeys jumping up and down.” Eventually, the monkeys relent, but then comes the darkness. Things I’d rather not remember. In the silence, I come nose-to-nose with sin—there is no running here.
I say the Jesus Prayer to refocus myself. It contains no magic powers, but it returns my mind to God. It’s an anchor.
After 15 minutes of silence, I open my eyes and feel … nothing.
Well, maybe a little more peaceful, but not much different. Frankly, I anticipated a transcendent peace and a halo to appear above my head. Here I am trying to commune with God, and yet I feel nothing. I know silence is not the same as absence, but absence would certainly explain the silence. It feels exasperating some days.
After doing this for over a year, I still haven’t received a halo.
Yet even if I did feel that transcendent peace after contemplative prayer, could I really attribute it to God’s presence? Or would it simply be the effects of a lowered heart rate and a quieter mind? If, physiologically, being with God and just being by myself in a meditative state are identical, then how can I tell the difference?
Thomas Merton, a Catholic who helped popularize contemplative prayer in the 20th century, warned against this very thing. According to Merton, we shouldn’t “judge the value of our meditation by ‘how we feel.’” He knows all who attempt contemplative prayer want to experience God’s presence, but he advises us to drop that desire. Release your expectations. Expect nothing.
Why? Because the merits of contemplative prayer are independent of the subjective experience it produces. The whole purpose—which I had lost sight of—is transformation and loving communion with God.
The question I should have been asking, then, was not “Do I feel more peaceful?” but “Is this helping me become a more loving person?” The latter is the better metric of success, not a subjective feeling of closeness to God. This aligns with the biblical test to examine a thing’s fruit (Matt. 7:16–20).
On a more recent Monday night, I was back in the chaos of my kitchen, stepping over plastic farm animals and a hairbrush while preparing dinner. I was still tired. My daughter was still crying. Household tasks were still making me feel like Sisyphus. But somehow, it was well with my soul. There was a bit more calm amid the storm. Though I still reprimanded that barking dog, I was a little slower to do it. My threshold was just a little bit higher.
If that’s the fruit of contemplative prayer—that incremental improvement—then I’ll keep at it. Even if the halo never appears.
