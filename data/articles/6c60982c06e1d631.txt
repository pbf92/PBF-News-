TITLE: Anthropic lets you install Claude ‘artifacts’ as Mac apps
OUTLET: Computerworld

Anthropic is now rolling out beta access to a new Claude feature that lets you save some of what it does as live apps on Macs, using “artifacts.” What’s new is that you can take an artifact and install it as an app on your Mac. The same trick works on Windows systems too.
The move makes Apple’s recent decision to give users more control over which apps and AI services have access to their data a lot more understandable.
An artifact is different from a standard Claude chat. Essentially, it is a standalone piece of output Claude builds; it can be a web page, interactive tool, document, slide deck, or design. You can open it, use it, edit it, and share it. Available on all Anthropic plans, artifacts exist in a separate section of the Claude sidebar, called, appropriately enough, Artifacts.
How to build an artifact
It’s pretty simple to make one of these things.
First, develop a complex question that defines the result. It could be to request a slide deck capturing the environmental consequences of data center deployments globally tracked over time graphed against consumer energy prices. You could ask for that to be generated using green and yellow nature colors, or even request a little widget that can predict future data based on different criteria.
Once the AI has finished working on the document, you can go in and edit it. For example, you may choose to take a screen grab of the trees marching against the tower of Saruman in the Lord of the Rings movie trilogy as a background image for one of the slides, or perhaps greenwash the entire deck with pictures of rainbows and flowers. When it’s done, you can save or share the artifact. I did that, and you can download the resulting slide deck as a PDF here, but only for a limited time.
When you share an artifact and install it as an app on your Mac, it gets its own icon and opens inside its own window. It also needs you to give it permission to work on your Mac, and you can set your data sharing restrictions. (And, thanks to Apple, rescind them.) These aren’t really for slide decks like mine; you’ll use them for things like meeting preparation systems to gather information for forthcoming sessions, order tracking, or other live tasks.
What about the permissions?
Anthropic says there are three levels of permission you can provide.
- Default: Installed apps can only do what they’d be able to do in a browser.
- App permissions: Apps can ask to use common features that Claude can tightly control, like creating always-on-top windows, listening for global shortcuts, or sending notifications.
- Full computer access: Apps can ask to run programs and read, change, or delete your files without asking each time. I really am not comfortable with giving this level of permission outside of a sandbox, and think most users should feel the same.
Apple may have seen this coming and earlier this month updated some of its Mac settings in such a way as to give Mac users more control over the data they share with apps. Particularly when it comes to providing an app with full access to the computer or a user’s data, Apple warned:
“Before granting an app such extraordinary access, users should understand that it puts all of the data on their Mac at risk. These changes are designed to help them do that and to empower them to make informed decisions about their own data and privacy.”
It seems very positive that users can disallow this kind of permission as easily as providing it.
What’s happening?
There are several things to think about here. For developers, the most important concern must be that as AI becomes better at inventing apps on the fly, the currency of the app economy will also change, and we’ll see everything critics ever said about Apple Sherlocking apps happen on steroids, the difference being that it will be AI companies doing the Sherlocking.
That’s going to have a dramatic effect on the app economy. It is also likely to impact app store sales, and, given the millions generated in digital store taxation, will have consequential economic and political impact.
There are other likely takeaways. One of these is all about you, your business, and your data: AI firms clearly want permission to work with all of that, and it seems of vital importance that computer users deeply consider the potential consequences of permitting such access. In the current security environment, I’m incredibly cautious. Who are these people? What do they want? Can I trust them with my data? Should they even have access to it?
But Anthropic’s move to turn artifacts into apps matches the mood music across the AI industry, because it recognizes that as AI becomes commodified, it must escape from the cloud and occupy the platform experience. That aspect, more than any rapid AI evolution, is the real challenge Apple must face.
I think trust, integration, and a focus on the user will see Apple through. The company must argue strenuously and profoundly for the need to ensure computer users can trust digital devices with their data. And it should continue its work to offer up alternative models that provide at least some of the convenience of AI with less of the risk — which is an argument in favor of doubling down on data retention, on-device AI, and privacy.
