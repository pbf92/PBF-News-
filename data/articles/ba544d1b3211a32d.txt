TITLE: One minute will be enough for Nielsen to credit a radio listener
OUTLET: PPC Land

Nielsen today said it will lower the amount of listening its Portable People Meter (PPM) radio ratings service needs before it credits a listener in a quarter-hour, from 3 minutes to 1 minute. The change is expected to take effect with the April 2027 survey period, and Nielsen expects it to increase measurable ad impressions by approximately 20%.
In Short
Nielsen will count a radio listener toward a station's quarter-hour audience after 1 minute of listening instead of 3, starting with the April 2027 PPM survey. Nielsen expects about 20% more measurable ad impressions, and those impressions are what radio stations sell and advertisers pay for. Audience estimates from April 2027 onward will rest on a different counting rule than earlier surveys, so part of any rise across that line will come from the method rather than from more listening.
Five minutes, then three, then one
Today's announcement is the second time in less than two years that Nielsen has cut the audio qualifier. The qualifier is the minimum amount of listening within a quarter-hour that PPM measurement needs before a person counts in a station's audience estimate for that quarter-hour. Nielsen moved PPM from a 5-minute to a 3-minute qualifier in January 2025, a rule that, according to the release, "requires at least 3 minutes of listening per quarter-hour to capture audience estimates."
From the April 2027 survey, one minute will be enough. Nielsen describes the purpose as aligning Audio PPM measurement "with the marketplace's modernized definition of Average Quarter-Hour (AQH)" and says the new rule "will more accurately reflect modern audio consumption and better capture impressions." The company presents it as part of its wider audience measurement work across television and audio, which it calls "media intelligence."
Measured from the original 5-minute rule, the cumulative reduction comes to 80%, according to PPC Land's arithmetic. That is the same proportional cut Nielsen applied to local television earlier this year. The step from 3 minutes to 1 is a reduction of about 67%.
The quarter-hour itself survives. Average Quarter-Hour remains the metric radio is sold on, and the new rule does not replace it with an average minute rating. What changes is which listening qualifies inside each 15-minute block.
How crediting works under the new rule
According to Nielsen's release, the change breaks down into five points:
- PPM measurement credits listening by quarter-hour. A listener must meet the qualifier within a given quarter-hour to be counted in audience estimates for it.
- Since January 2025, that qualifier has required "at least 3 minutes of listening per quarter-hour."
- With the April 2027 PPM survey, the qualifier drops to 1 minute. Nielsen describes the result as "counting every minute equally."
- The April 2027 survey "measures from April 1 - 28" and results release "starting in May." Nielsen says the 1-minute qualifier "is expected to go live as the new industry standard" with that survey.
- Until then, Nielsen "continues to work closely with clients to prepare them for the launch."
The quarter-hour break, illustrated
Nielsen's case rests on a specific weakness of multi-minute rules: what it calls "lost credit surrounding the quarter-hour break." A simplified PPC Land illustration, based only on the thresholds Nielsen describes and not on a Nielsen example, shows how it works.
Consider a listener who tunes in at 8:12 and leaves at 8:17. That is five minutes of listening. Three of those minutes (8:12, 8:13 and 8:14) fall in the 8:00 quarter-hour, and two (8:15 and 8:16) fall in the 8:15 quarter-hour.
Under the old 5-minute rule, five full minutes of listening produced no credit at all, because the session straddled the boundary. Under the current 3-minute rule, it produces one quarter-hour. Under the April 2027 rule, it produces two. The release does not say how listening of less than a full minute is treated, or how a minute split across two quarter-hours is assigned, so the illustration uses whole minutes.
The 20% estimate
"The updated 1-minute threshold is expected to increase measurable ad impressions by approximately 20%," according to Nielsen.
The key word is measurable. Nothing in the change makes people listen more. Short sessions that already happen, but fell below the 3-minute bar inside a quarter-hour, will start to count. Rich Tunkel, Managing Director of Nielsen Audio, framed it in exactly those terms: advertisers "will get to count every available impression in their schedules and in their outcome models."
That has a direct price consequence. If the cost of a radio schedule stayed the same while its counted impressions rose 20%, the cost per thousand impressions would fall by about 16.7%, according to PPC Land's calculation (1 divided by 1.2). The release does not discuss rates. It does not say whether stations will reprice inventory once larger audience estimates arrive, which leaves open the commercial question behind any measurement change: who keeps the extra 20%, stations through higher rates or advertisers through a lower effective cost?
The estimate is also a single number. Nielsen gives no breakdown by market, daypart, demographic or format, and no indication of whether it comes from parallel data, a test period or modelling.
The 20% figure and the 24% Nielsen cited for local television measure different things. The television figure described how many tuning events went unreported under a 5-minute rule; the audio figure is a forecast increase in ad impressions from a 3-minute starting point.
Show clocks and counter-programming
Nielsen also makes a programming argument, and it is the most specific part of the release. "By eliminating any chance for lost credit surrounding the quarter-hour break, programming teams have more freedom to innovate with show clocks, test fresh content strategies and counter-program more effectively," the company said.
Show clocks are the hourly templates radio stations use to place music, talk, news and commercial breaks. Under a multi-minute qualifier, where a break sat relative to the :00, :15, :30 and :45 marks could decide whether a listener counted in one quarter-hour, two, or none, as the illustration above shows. A 1-minute rule shrinks that effect. A listener still has to be present for a minute in each quarter-hour, but the penalty for a boundary that falls in the wrong place becomes far smaller.
"Ultimately, this flexibility creates the opportunity for a more seamless experience for listeners and advertisers," according to the release. Whether stations redesign their hours in response is a question the April 2027 data and the months after it may start to answer.
What Nielsen Audio said
Tunkel's is the release's only named statement. "As listening habits continue to evolve, it's vital to the industry that our measurement and media intelligence systems adapt in tandem," he said. "Adopting a 1-minute qualifier strengthens data for everyone, as it more closely aligns our metrics across platforms. Audio publishers can take full credit for all of their audiences and advertisers will get to count every available impression in their schedules and in their outcome models."
Tunkel was also the Nielsen executive quoted in March, when Magellan AI licensed Nielsen's local market data for podcast attribution, letting podcast campaign results be split across 210 US designated market areas.
Local television went first
The audio change follows a nearly identical move in television. On August 31, 2026, Nielsen activated four methodology changes in its Local TV currency data, one of which replaced the 5-minute quarter-hour qualifier with a 1-minute one. Nielsen said at the time that the 5-minute rule left upwards of 24% of tuning events unreported. The company said the change would give buyers and sellers a fuller view of local TV reach in every DMA in the country.
The other three changes on that date tied the two businesses together more directly. One was a co-viewing adjustment based on the Portable People Meter panel, the same metering system behind the radio ratings now changing. The others were an updated household demographic assignment model and a revised Spanish-language universe estimate, which took effect on September 26, 2026.
Today's release says the audio change "mirrors" the television one, "creating greater consistency across the audio and television measurement spaces." From August 31, 2026 to the start of the April 2027 survey, roughly seven months by PPC Land's count, local TV and radio sit on different rules: one minute for television, three for radio. A buyer comparing a local TV schedule with a radio schedule in the same market during that window is comparing audiences counted on different thresholds.
Local TV measurement has drawn industry scrutiny of its own this year. A Coalition for Innovative Media Measurement paper released on August 25 said as much as 20-30% of local viewing may go uncounted, citing station apps, browser streams, FAST channels and YouTube among the sources often missed. On the national side, Nielsen made Big Data + Panel the basis for TV press claims from the 2025 season, combining a 42,000-home panel with data from 45 million households.
A ratings business under pressure
The audio qualifier arrives at a difficult moment for radio and for Nielsen's radio business.
In court, Nielsen has spent most of the past year defending how it sells radio ratings. Cumulus Media sued in October 2025 over Nielsen's Network Policy, announced in September 2024, which conditioned the sale of Nielsen's national radio ratings to networks with local stations on those stations buying Nielsen's local data. A federal judge in New York blocked the policy on December 30, 2025, the Second Circuit stayed that injunction on February 3, 2026, and on July 13, 2026 the same court affirmed the injunction in full, finding that a standalone price of about ten times Cumulus's existing contract could work as a tie. Cumulus filed for Chapter 11 on March 10, 2026. The litigation concerns how Nielsen packages and prices national and local ratings, not how it counts listening, and today's release does not mention it.
On the books, the broadcast side of radio is shrinking. iHeartMedia cut its 2026 adjusted EBITDA forecast to $550 million to $600 million from about $800 million when it reported third-quarter results on November 9, with revenue in its broadcast-led Multiplatform Group down 4.9% to $562.4 million. For stations in that position, a methodology change that adds an estimated 20% to measurable impressions changes the numbers they take to buyers, whatever happens to actual listening.
Other companies are building radio measurement of their own. In March, Magellan AI launched Broadcast Radio Attribution, measuring form fills, leads and purchases from broadcast radio alongside podcasts and streaming audio. Tools of that kind are the "outcome models" Tunkel referred to, and they depend on the exposure data underneath. A larger count of measured impressions changes the denominator in any cost-per-outcome calculation built on Nielsen numbers.
Radio still holds a large share of the ad-supported audio day. When Nielsen launched its quarterly audio report The Record, the first edition put radio at nearly 70% of daily time spent with ad-supported audio in the first quarter of 2024, with podcasts at 20%.
What the release does not say
The release does not say how many markets PPM covers, whether Nielsen's diary-measured markets or its national radio ratings will change, or what the January 2025 move from 5 to 3 minutes did to audience estimates. It does not explain what the "marketplace's modernized definition" of AQH refers to, how the 1-minute rule affects cume or time spent listening, whether historical data will be restated or trended, or whether the Media Rating Council has reviewed the change.
