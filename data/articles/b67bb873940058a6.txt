TITLE: Daily Exercise Cuts Students’ Mental Health Risk, Norway Study Finds
OUTLET: Global Agriculture

25 November 2026, Bergen, NO: University students who exercise every day are less likely to develop common mental disorders such as anxiety and depression over the following year than those who seldom or never work out, according to a national study of more than 53,000 students in Norway that is drawing fresh attention this month.
The research, led by Michael Grasdalsmoen of the Western Norway University of Applied Sciences with colleagues at the Norwegian Institute of Public Health and the Helse Fonna health trust, was published in the open-access journal PLOS ONE in August and publicised by the journal on November 20. Co-authors include Erlingur Johannsson, Mari Hysing and Børge Sivertsen.
Common mental disorders is the umbrella term clinicians use for the most widespread conditions, chiefly depression and anxiety disorders.
How the study worked
The team drew on SHoT2022, Norway’s national Students’ Health and Wellbeing Study, which surveyed full-time higher education students aged 18 to 35. At the start, 53,362 students reported how often and how many hours a week they exercised. One year later, 10,460 of them completed a structured diagnostic interview, the Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI 5.0), a standardised tool used to identify mental disorders according to clinical criteria.
The researchers used a statistical method called inverse-probability weighting, which gives more weight to students who resemble those lost to follow-up, so that the smaller interviewed group better reflects the full starting population. Results were expressed as relative risks, which compare the chance of developing a disorder in one group with the chance in another. A relative risk of 1.33, for example, means a risk one-third higher.
Students who seldom or never exercised had a higher risk of a common mental disorder a year later than those who exercised daily. For women, the risk was 15% higher (relative risk 1.15, 95% confidence interval 1.04 to 1.28). For men, it was 33% higher (relative risk 1.33, 95% confidence interval 1.06 to 1.65). The more hours students exercised each week, the lower their risk, a pattern that held up to about 10 hours a week. The link did not differ meaningfully between younger and older students.
Because the study is observational, it shows an association rather than proof that exercise prevents mental illness. Students who are already struggling may find it harder to exercise, although following students forward in time and using a clinical interview at follow-up strengthens the case that activity levels came first.
Regular habits
In publicising the work, PLOS framed the findings around how often students exercise. The comparison at the heart of the study set daily exercisers against those who seldom or never exercised, and the second measure, weekly hours, showed risk falling as total exercise time rose, up to about 10 hours a week.
The SHoT study is funded by the Norwegian Ministry of Education and Research and the Norwegian Ministry of Health and Care Services, which had no role in the study’s design, analysis or decision to publish. The authors declared no competing interests. The paper, titled “Physical exercise and risk of common mental disorders among higher education students: A national prospective study”, is freely available under a Creative Commons licence.
